    11→    11→ Abstract. Folding schemes are gaining traction recently as they are suited to prove streaming com-
    12→    12→ putations with low memory footprint. In particular, there has been a growing interest in post-quantum
    13→    13→ folding schemes for more expressive relations, and with improved proof sizes. While the landscape is
    14→    14→ vast, every lattice-based construction, such as Latticefold+, (Super)Neo, and Cyclo, heavily rely on the
    15→    15→ sumcheck protocol. Sumcheck gives efficient proving times, but the verifier circuits become very large,
    16→    16→ partially because of the many random oracle invocations required. When folding sequential computa-
    17→    17→ tions, this significantly inflates the recursive overhead, as the prover must run the verification at every
    18→    18→ iteration.
    19→    19→ We present ProtogaLattice, a new lattice-based folding scheme for general high-degree polynomial
    20→    20→ relations that drastically reduces the size of the verifier’s circuits. Our folding backbone deviates from
    21→    21→ the sumcheck approach and uses algebraic techniques. Our contribution is twofold:
    22→    22→ i) a folding scheme that combines multiple instances of polynomial relations into accumulators for a
    23→    23→ bounded number of iterations, and
    24→    24→ ii) a bootstrapping protocol to reduce the norm of the witnesses underlying these accumulators, which
    25→    25→ can be used in complement to our bounded-depth folding scheme to unlock unlimited depth.
    26→    26→ A full iteration of ProtogaLattice for witnesses of length 230 gives a 4ˆ shorter proof size than the
    27→    27→ state-of-the-art, with only 8.13 KB. Additionally, ProtogaLattice requires less random oracle evaluations
    28→    28→ and achieves a smaller verifier circuit than its sumcheck counterparts.
    29→    29→ Our techniques open new directions towards building efficient lattice-based proofs for expressive
    30→    30→ relations and that present small recursion overheads, challenging the established sumcheck-based ap-
    31→    31→ proaches.

    33→    33→1 Introduction
    34→    34→
    35→    35→Folding Schemes, first introduced by Nova [KST22], allow one to accumulate multiple instances of
    36→    36→an NP relation into a single small instance while preserving satisfiability. As a better alternative
    37→    37→to classical recursive proof systems, such techniques unlock efficient instantiations of incrementally
    38→    38→verifiable computation (IVC) [Val08] or proof carrying data (PCD) [CT10].
    39→    39→ Works that build recursive proof schemes [BCMS20,BCL` 21] show how to amortize the recursive
    40→    40→costs by aggregating some parts of the proof, while the recursive statement only checks that the
    41→    41→aggregation was done correctly. These ideas were further developed into more efficient ways to
    42→    42→accumulate statements and defer hard computations, which led to the emergence of multiple folding
    43→    43→schemes.
    44→    44→ Recently, numerous works have extended the folding paradigm, improving efficiency, generality,
    45→    45→and modularity.
    46→    46→ In a nutshell, folding schemes allow one to aggregate multiple instances of a computational
    47→    47→relation into a single instance that captures their joint validity via the reduction of knowledge
    48→    48→abstraction [KP23]. To illustrate reductions of knowledge, let Ξ and Ξ ˚ be two instance–witness
    49→    49→relations. We denote instances by t and their corresponding witnesses by w. A reduction of knowl-
    50→    50→edge from Ξ to Ξ ˚ is an interactive protocol Π between a prover and a verifier with the following
    51→    51→structure: the verifier takes as input an instance t1 for Ξ, interacts with the prover, and outputs an
    52→    52→instance t2 for Ξ ˚ . The security property of reductions of knowledge ensures that if the prover can
    53→    53→subsequently produce a valid witness w2 (such that pt2 , w2 q P Ξ ˚ ), then there exists an efficient
    54→    54→extractor that recovers a valid witness w1 (such that pt1 , w1 q P Ξ).
    55→    55→ Then, a folding scheme for k witnesses is simply a reduction of knowledge tailored to accumu-
    56→    56→lations, which goes from a product relation Ξ ˚ ˆ Ξ k Ñ Ξ ˚ . Here, Ξ is the base relation and Ξ ˚ is
    57→    57→an accumulation relation whose instance compactly represent prior computation steps.
    58→    58→ To construct IVC, the prover can fold a sequence of computation steps into a single accumulator
    59→    59→instance whose size remains essentially fixed, which leads to memory-efficient proving. At the end
    60→    60→of the computation, it suffices to prove knowledge of a witness for the final Ξ ˚ instance.
    61→    61→ Folding schemes are characterized by their expressiveness, which is essentially the class of rela-
    62→    62→tions that they can fold. Given a witness w and an instance t, a general relation takes the form
    63→    63→
    64→    64→ Ξ “ tpt, wq : f pwq “ 0 ^ t “ cmpwqu ,
    65→    65→
    66→    66→where f is a polynomial map of total degree at most d, and cm is an additive homomorphic
    67→    67→commitment scheme. Depending on the form of f , a folding scheme may capture standard constraint
    68→    68→systems used in succinct arguments such as R1CS, Plonkish, CCS, or even arbitrary polynomial
    69→    69→constraints. Generally, supporting large d and arbitrary polynomial maps f is preferred as it enables
    70→    70→more efficient arithmetizations, improving the practicality.
    71→    71→ Without aiming to be exhaustive4 , we categorize folding schemes in two big families:
    72→    72→ Algebraic folding schemes define the folding step as a closed algebraic transformation on the rela-
    73→    73→tion itself, so each fold is implemented by evaluating and linearly combining polynomial constraints
    74→    74→on the relation witnesses directly. Then, the verifier combines the instances leveraging an additive
    75→    75→homomorphic commitment scheme. Examples include Nova [KST22] and FLIP-and-Prove [NPR26]
    76→    76→for quadratic relations, and Protostar [BC23] and Protogalaxy [EG23] for high-degree, arbitrary
    77→    77→polynomial relations.
    78→    78→ Sumcheck-based folding schemes such as Hypernova [KS24] or FLI [GM24] phrase the fold-
    79→    79→ing as a claim about a big polynomial (or about products of multilinear polynomials) and then
    80→    80→use the sumcheck protocol to reduce the claim to a small number of scalar checks, so the heavy
    81→    81→lifting is in the interactive polynomial reduction rather than in an algebraic gadget. This is also
    82→    82→the approach taken by all existing lattice-based folding schemes5 including Latticefold [BC25a],
    83→    83→Latticefold+ [BC25b], Neo/SuperNeo [NS26], and Cyclo [GLLO26] for high-degree customizable
    84→    84→constraint systems (CCS).
    85→    85→ None of the above approaches is strictly better than the other. On the one hand, algebraic
    86→    86→schemes give very structured accumulators, are more succinct, and, in general, have cheaper per-
    87→    87→step verification (partially because the round-efficiency, with few random oracle calls), resulting
    88→    88→in smaller recursive overheads for IVC. On the other hand, sumcheck-based schemes enjoy linear
    89→    89→proving time in k for multi-folding (k ą 1).

    98→    98→1.1 Lattice-based Folding
    99→    99→
   100→   100→Due to their plausible post-quantum security and to the capacity of supporting smaller (64-bit)
   101→   101→fields natively, lattice-based folding schemes have seen lots of research activity in recent years.6
   102→   102→All existing lattice-based folding schemes for high-degree relations work over rings of the form
   103→   103→Rq “ Zq rXs{pΦq for a cyclotomic polynomial Φ “ X N ` 1 and follow the same high-level recipe:
   104→   104→
   105→   105→1. Depart from a high degree-d relation that can be captured by products of multilinear polyno-
   106→   106→ mials, such as CCS [STW23]. Define witnesses wi for these relations with low norm, that is,
   107→   107→ ||wi || ď B for some bound B.
   108→   108→2. Commit to the wi ’s to be folded using Ajtai commitments [Ajt96], which are additively homo-
   109→   109→ morphic and binding for low-norm committed vectors. Besides, use a range proof to show that
   110→   110→ the committed wi ’s satisfy the norm bound ||wi || ď B.
   111→   111→3. Reduce, via the sumcheck protocol, the initial degree-d relation to a series of evaluation claims
   112→   112→ about multilinear (i.e. degree-1) polynomials. These claims are generally captured as a linearized
   113→   113→ relation (e.g. Linearized CCS).
   114→   114→4. Decompose the witnesses in some basis b to control the norm growth induced by the folding as
   115→   115→ needed.
   116→   116→
   117→   117→ This elegant approach has been proven to be successful, but it is currently the only recipe
   118→   118→for building lattice-based folding schemes with practical performance and which are expressive
   119→   119→enough to capture high-degree relations. Besides, it presents two important limitations, which
   120→   120→seem very intrinsic to the reliance on sumchecks. First, the high-degree relation used needs to be
   121→   121→sumcheck-friendly. This prevents one from folding arbitrary transcripts determined by polynomial
   122→   122→constraints, such as in algebraic folding schemes [BC23,EG23], which are more versatile and enable
   123→   123→native folding of Plonkish and lookup relations without translation overheads. Second, sumcheck
   124→   124→leads to large transcripts, many random oracle calls (e.g. Latticefold+ reports around 100 RO
   125→   125→calls), and therefore a complex verifier circuit. When used in an IVC construction, all verifier
   126→   126→operations, including evaluations of hash functions, have to be encoded in the prover circuit, which
   127→   127→is a significant source of inefficiency.
   128→   128→ Considering the state-of-the art in the lattice-based folding schemes, our work addresses the
   129→   129→following question:
   130→   130→ Can we build a lattice-based algebraic folding scheme that:
   131→   131→ (i) is round–efficient,
   132→   132→ (ii) communication–efficient,
   133→   133→ (iii) expressive for high-degree relations,
   134→   134→ (iv) and supports an unbounded number of folding iterations?

   253→   253→1.4 Summary and Open Problems
   254→   254→Summary. In this work, we construct ProtogaLattice, a novel lattice-based folding scheme for high-
   255→   255→degree polynomial relations that is round and communication efficient and allows for unbounded
   256→   256→folding depth. ProtogaLattice supports folding k witnesses at a time (although efficiency degrades
   257→   257→significantly for large k), and works by iteratively folding and bootstrapping witnesses to reduce
   258→   258→their norm.
   259→   259→ Our construction can be seen as a constant-round folding backbone to which an efficient range-
   260→   260→proof mechanism can be attached, making it modular and flexible for instantiations. This represents
   261→   261→a significant step forward in the design of low-round folding schemes for general relations.
   262→   262→Open Problems. While ProtogaLattice shows a novel approach towards realizing efficient lattice-
   263→   263→based folding for expressive general relations, there are some open questions that we consider
   264→   264→interesting and leave as future work.
   265→   265→
   266→   266→1. Constant-Round Norm Check. ProtogaLattice provides a flexible constant-round folding module
   267→   267→ to which any range proof available in the literature can be attached. We instantiate it with a
   268→   268→ sumcheck-based range proof adapted from (Super)Neo [NS26]. To the best of our knowledge,
   269→   269→ succinct range proofs with succinct verification from the literature follow either a split-and-fold
   270→   270→ paradigm (RoK and Roll [KLNO25], RoK Paper SISsors [KLNO24]) or rely on the sum-check
   271→   271→ protocol (LatticeFold+ [BC25b], SALSAA [KLOT25], RoKoko [KLN` 26]). All aforementioned
   272→   272→ range proofs have a logarithmic number of rounds. Therefore, instantiations using one of these
   273→   273→ schemes do not allow to fully realize the constant-round potential of the ProtogaLattice folding
   274→   274→ backbone. We leave as an interesting open question the design of a constant-round range proof
   275→   275→ that exploits the structure of ProtogaLattice.
   276→   276→2. Zero-Knowledge. Depending on the context in which Protogalattice is used, it may be useful to
   277→   277→ have a zero-knowledge variant of the scheme, or to study whether the scheme (or a modification
   278→   278→ of it) satisfies the randomizing property introduced in [KS24] for the purpose of building zero-
   279→   279→ knowledge IVC.
