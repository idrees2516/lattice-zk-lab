    11→                                            Abstract
    12→        Folding is a technique for building efficient succinct proof systems. Many existing
    13→   folding protocols rely on the discrete-log based Pedersen commitment scheme, and are
    14→   therefore not post-quantum secure and require a large (256-bit) field. Recently, Boneh
    15→   and Chen constructed LatticeFold, a folding protocol using lattice-based commitments
    16→   which is plausibly post-quantum secure and can operate with small (64-bit) fields. For
    17→   knowledge soundness, LatticeFold requires the prover to provide a range proof on all the
    18→   input witnesses using bit-decomposition, and this slows down the prover. In this work
    19→   we present LatticeFold+, a very different lattice-based folding protocol that improves
    20→   on LatticeFold in every respect: the prover is significantly faster, the verification circuit
    21→   is simpler, and the folding proofs are shorter. To do so we develop two novel lattice
    22→   techniques. First, we develop a new purely algebraic range proof which is much more
    23→   efficient than the one in LatticeFold, and may be of independent interest. We further
    24→   shrink the proof using double commitments (commitments of commitments). Second,
    25→   we show how to fold statements about double commitments using a new sumcheck-
    26→   based transformation.

    65→1       Introduction
    66→In recent years succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (SNARKs) have found
    67→many real-world applications: scaling and bridging blockchains [Whi18; Xie+22], authen-
    68→ticating media [NT16; DB22; KHSS22], machine learning [CWSK24; YCBC24], verifiable
    69→delay functions [BBBF18], and many others. To avoid the high memory requirements of
    70→some SNARK proof systems, provers in practice break the task of constructing a proof
    71→into small steps and prove each step separately. This approach is called incrementally ver-
    72→ifiable computation (IVC) [Val08] or proof carrying data (PCD) [CT10]. It also provides
    73→additional opportunities for parallelizing the prover [Ngu+24].
    74→     The original IVC/PCD schemes were built using SNARK recursion [Val08; BCTV14],
    75→where the SNARK verifier is embedded in every computation step being proved. A more
    76→efficient approach, called accumulation or folding, was introduced in Halo [BGH19] and
    77→further developed in [BCMS20; Bün+21; BDFG21] and Nova [KST22]. Many elegant
    78→ideas have since further optimized and extended the folding paradigm [KS24b; BC23;
    79→EG23; BMNW24; Moh23; NBS23; FKNP24; KS24a; AS24].
    80→     Folding is best explained using the language of reductions of knowledge [KP23] (see
    81→Section 2.4 for details). Let R1 and R2 be two instance-witness relations. A reduction
    82→of knowledge from R1 to R2 is a protocol Π between a prover and verifier. The verifier
    83→takes as input an instance x1 for R1 , interacts with the prover, and outputs an instance x2
    84→for R2 at the end of the protocol. The key requirement is that if the prover can present
    85→a witness w2 for x2 , then it is possible to extract from the prover a witnesses w1 for x1 .
    86→Hence, knowledge of a valid witness for x2 proves knowledge of a valid witness for x1 .
    87→     A folding scheme is a reduction of knowledge from some product relation Racc × Rcomp
    88→to Racc . That is, the folding scheme can fold a pair of instances (xacc , xcomp ) into a single
    89→new instance x′acc of Racc . By repeatedly folding in this way, the prover can accumulate
    90→many steps of a computation expressed by Rcomp into a single instance of the accumulation
    91→relation Racc . Eventually, the prover proves knowledge of a witness for the final Racc
    92→instance, and this proves knowledge of a valid witness for every step of the computation.
    93→Alternatively, folding can take place along a d-ary tree, with d-to-1 foldings, as in [Ngu+24;
    94→RZ22]. The benefit of folding schemes is that now the statement being proved at every
    95→step only needs to ensure that folding was performed correctly at the previous step, which
    96→is far simpler than a full SNARK verifier. Folding is also much faster for the prover
    97→because folding two witnesses into one is primarily a random linear combination of the two
    98→witnesses.
    99→     Folding schemes typically extend the input relation to include a short commitment to
   100→the witness. The commitment scheme is linearly homomorphic, so that the verifier can com-
   101→pute the folded instance as a a random linear combination of these input commitments1 .
   102→Often one uses Pedersen as the homomorphic commitment scheme. This poses three dif-

   110→Pedersen hash requires the prover to do a compute-intensive multiscalar multiplication over
   111→a large (256-bit) field; and third, the domain and range of the Pedersen hash are different
   112→groups, requiring arithmetic over two distinct fields to verify a folding proof. This can be
   113→expensive because arithmetic circuits natively support only one field.
   114→
   115→Lattice-based folding. Boneh and Chen [BC24] recently proposed a lattice-based fold-
   116→ing scheme, called LatticeFold, where the Pedersen commitment is replaced with an Ajtai
   117→commitment over modules. This resolves all three difficulties mentioned in the previous
   118→paragraph2 . However, using Ajtai commitments in folding introduces significant challenges.
   119→The difficulty is that Ajtai commitments are binding only when the input vector is low
   120→norm. This is incompatible with the random linear combination approach used in folding.
   121→Repeatedly taking a random linear combination of witnesses quickly increases the norm
   122→of the accumulated witness to the point where Ajtai commitments are no longer binding.
   123→This breaks soundness of the resulting folding scheme.
   124→    To address this issue, folding in LatticeFold is done in two primary steps. First, a norm
   125→reduction step decomposes each of the two input witnesses into d lower norm witnesses.
   126→Second, the resulting 2d instances are folded into a single low norm instance. This ensures
   127→that after repeated folding, the norm of the accumulated witness remains small. However,
   128→this by itself is insufficient. One must also exhibit an extractor that extracts from the prover
   129→low norm witnesses for the two input instances. LatticeFold ensures that the extracted
   130→witnesses are low norm by using a novel sumcheck-based range proof. Forcing the prover
   131→to output this range proof for the two input witnesses is sufficient to ensure that the
   132→extracted witnesses are valid and low norm. However, the range proof in LatticeFold uses
   133→bit decomposition and is therefore expensive to construct because it requires the prover to
   134→commit to many decomposed witnesses.
   135→    Experiments by Nethermind [Net24] show that this additional complexity in folding
   136→using Ajtai commitments results in a LatticeFold prover that runs in approximately the
   137→same time as the Pedersen-based HyperNova folding prover [KS24b]. While LatticeFold
   138→has the benefit of post-quantum security, one would further expect that Ajtai-based folding
   139→would be faster than Pedersen-based folding.

   141→Our results. In this work we design a new lattice-based folding scheme called Lattice-
   142→Fold+ that greatly improves the performance of LatticeFold using a number of new lattice-
   143→ring techniques.

   160→vector f ∈ Rqn , where the ℓ∞ norm of f satisfies ∥f ∥∞ < B (as defined in the next section).
   161→The elements r and v will be explained in Section 3. Here the witness f is an n-vector of
   162→elements in the ring Rq := Zq [X]/⟨X d + 1⟩ for some prime q and degree d > 1.
   163→     The core of LatticeFold+ takes as input L > 2 instances of Rlin,B and folds them into
   164→two instances of Rlin,B . It does so in two steps. In the first step, it folds the given L
   165→instances of Rlin,B into one instance of Rlin,B 2 , namely an instance with a norm bound B 2
   166→on the witness. The new range proof in this step is purely algebraic — it does not require
   167→commitments to bit-decomposed vectors. The increase in norm from B to B 2 has to be
   168→corrected, and LatticeFold+ does this in the second step. It uses witness decomposition to
   169→reduce a linear instance with norm bound B 2 to two linear instances with norm bound B
   170→by splitting every scalar in the witness into its high and low order bits. This second step is
   171→approximately the same as decomposition in LatticeFold. The key new insights are in the
   172→first folding step, which we discuss next.
   173→
   174→A new range proof. To ensure that an extractor is able to extract L low norm witnesses
   175→from a folding prover, the prover must provide a range proof that all the input witnesses
   176→are low norm. We develop a new range proof, presented in Section 4.3, that avoids bit
   177→decomposition and is far more efficient than the range proof in LatticeFold. This range
   178→proof method may be of independent interest. The key idea is as follows. Say we want
   179→to prove that a committed vector f = (f1 , . . . , fn ) ∈ Znq satisfies fi ∈ (−d/2, d/2) for all
   180→i ∈ [n]. The prover will commit to a vector of ring elements m = (m1 , . . . , mn ) ∈ Rqn
   181→where each mi ∈ Rq is the monomial mi := X fi . We denote this commitment by com (m)
   182→and note that computing the Ajtai commitment to m is fast because it is a vector of
   183→monomials (see Remark 4.3). Next we show in Lemma 2.2 that if mi is a monomial and
   184→mi and fi satisfy a simple algebraic relation then fi ∈ (−d/2, d/2). Hence, our range
   185→proof needs to (i) prove that com (m) is a commitment to a vector of monomials, which
   186→we do in Section 4.2, and (ii) prove that m and f satisfy a certain algebraic relation, which
   187→we do in Section 4.3. Both steps are presented as reductions of knowledge that reduce
   188→these checks to checking a simple evaluation relation. The resulting range proof requires
   189→no commitments to bit-decomposed vectors, and is significantly faster than the LatticeFold
   190→range proof.

   192→Double commitments. When applying this range proof to a vector of ring elements
   193→f ∈ Rqn , we could repeat the range proof above d times: once for the vector of all constant
   194→
   195→
   196→                                               5
   197→terms in f , once for the vector of all linear terms in f , and so on. This would require the
   198→prover to commit to d vectors m0 , . . . , md−1 ∈ Rqn which would result in a large transcript
   199→of d commitments c := (com (m0 ) , . . . , com (md−1 )) ∈ Rqκ×d that must be sent to the
   200→verifier. Instead, we introduce in Section 4.1 the concept of a double commitment, where
   201→the prover sends to the verifier a commitment to the vector of commitments c. This vector
   202→c is not low norm, so we must first decompose it to reduce its norm and then commit to
   203→the decomposition using an Ajtai commitment. We use dcom (M) to denote this double
   204→commitment to the matrix M := [m0 , . . . , md−1 ] ∈ Rqn×d . Note that dcom (M) ∈ Rqκ is
   205→short as required. These double commitments are crucial for making our folding proofs
   206→shorter and simpler to verify compared to LatticeFold. In Section 4.3 we present a range
   207→proof that uses double commitments to prove that a vector f ∈ Rqn has low ℓ∞ norm.
   208→
   209→Commitment transformation. Double commitments are not linearly homomorphic,
   210→and as a result statements that use double commitments are hard to fold using random
   211→linear combinations. In Section 4.4 we develop a technique that transforms such statements
   212→to ones involving only linear commitments to smaller witnesses. We use sumchecks to en-
   213→sure consistency between the double commitment of a witness M and a linear commitment
   214→to a transformed version of M. Again, this technique may be of independent interest.
   215→
   216→Supporting small field arithmetic. Following Neo’s approach [NS25], LatticeFold+
   217→naturally supports small prime moduli, which we reinterpret using the tensor-of-rings
   218→                                                                                  d/e
   219→framework in Appendix B. The folding challenge set is of size q e if Rq ∼     = Fqe , and the
   220→sumchecks are running over an extension field Fqt . We can achieve the desired security by
   221→setting e and t appropriately. For example, if q is a 64-bit prime, to achieve 128-bit security,
   222→all the sumchecks will be over the extension field Fq2 . If d ≫ 2, a sumcheck over Fq2 for a
   223→size-n statement is more efficient than computing a module-based Ajtai commitment to a
   224→vector of size n over Rq . Hence, the prover cost for sumcheck is insignificant. We refer to
   225→the “Challenge sets” paragraph in Section 4 and Appendix B for a detailed discussion.
   226→
   227→Evaluation. LatticeFold+ improves over LatticeFold in every respect: the folding prover
   228→is faster, the folding verifier is simpler, and the folding proof is shorter. We give a de-
   229→tailed evaluation analysis in Section 5.3. Here we present a brief summary when folding L
   230→witnesses each of dimension n and norm less than B:
   231→ • Prover complexity: In LatticeFold, the prover’s complexity is dominated by a degree-
   232→    4 sumcheck over Rq of size n and the computation of L log2 (B) commitments to bit-
   233→    decomposed vectors. LatticeFold+ eliminates all these commitments and is consequently
   234→    Ω(log(B))-times faster. Concretely, going by Nethermind’s LatticeFold benchmark [Net24],
   235→    we estimate that the LatticeFold+ prover is five times faster than the LatticeFold prover.
   236→
   237→• Verifier circuit complexity: The verifier circuit size is dominated by hashing needed for
   238→
   239→
   240→                                               6
   241→   the Fiat-Shamir transform. In LatticeFold, all the L log2 (B) decomposed commitments
   242→   must be hashed. LatticeFold+ eliminates all these commitments thereby shrinking the
   243→   size of the verification circuit.
   244→
   245→ • Proof size: The LatticeFold proof size is Oλ (κd log B + d log n) bits. In LatticeFold+ the
   246→   proof size is only Oλ (κd + log n) bits. Since a folding circuit needs to compute a Fiat-
   247→   Shamir hash of its input, this shorter proof leads to a simpler circuit when compared to
   248→   LatticeFold. We also emphasize that, in folding-based IVC schemes, folding proofs serve
   249→   as recursive witnesses and are not part of the final output proof.
   250→Finally, since the prover running time in LatticeFold is comparable to HyperNova [Net24],
   251→we expect the prover time in LatticeFold+ to be significantly faster than HyperNova. Hence,
   252→LatticeFold+ provides both a fast prover and plausible post-quantum security.

   254→1.1   Additional related work
   255→Several post-quantum monolithic SNARKs (i.e., not using folding) are built from hash-
   256→based Merkle commitments: Stark [BBHR18b], Ligero [AHIV17], Aurora [Ben+19], Brake-
   257→down [Gol+23], BaseFold [ZCF24], Blaze [Bre+24], and Fractal [COS20]. Their proof
   258→sizes scale sublinearly with the witness size. In practice they require a significant amount
   259→of memory when proving a large statement. Several elegant post-quantum lattice-based
   260→proof systems also offer sublinear proof size [Bau+18; BLNS20; ACK21; Alb+22; BCS23],
   261→however the resulting proofs are larger than the hash-based schemes. One exception is
   262→LaBRADOR [BS23] that produces relatively short proofs, but has a linear time verifier. We
   263→note that LaBRADOR also uses commitments to commitments to shrink the proof size. A
   264→polynomial commitment scheme derived from LaBRADOR, called Greyhound [NS24], has a
   265→square root time verifier. Other lattice-based proof systems, such as [ENS20; LNP22], per-
   266→form well for small statements, but their proof size is linear in the size of the witness. Cini
   267→et al. [CMNW24] recently presented a lattice-based polynomial commitment scheme (PCS)
   268→from Bulletproof/FRI-like techniques [BBHR18a; Bün+18]. Their approach achieves bet-
   269→ter control over witness norm/slack blowup than previous works. However, their scheme
   270→incurs norm blowup/slack at each step, limiting it to supporting only a logarithmic number
   271→of folding steps.
   272→    Following LatticeFold [BC24], which uses module-based Ajtai commitments and the ℓ∞ -
   273→norm, Fenzi et al. proposed Lova [FKNP24] which operates similarly, but using integer-
   274→based Ajtai commitments and the ℓ2 -norm. Lova [FKNP24] relies on the integer-based
   275→SIS assumption, but has worse concrete efficiency. Very recently, Klooß et al. [KLNO24]
   276→suggest another approach to ℓ2 -norm range proofs that can apply to LatticeFold. Com-
   277→pared to LatticeFold+, their approach requires the use of subtractive sets which are only of
   278→polynomial size. To boost soundness, they rely on parallel repetition of folding challenges,
   279→which impacts efficiency.
   280→    Concurrent to LatticeFold+, Neo [NS25] addresses an orthogonal problem and suggests
   281→
   282→
   283→                                              7
   284→a new approach for embedding small field elements into the cyclotomic polynomial ring
   285→used in LatticeFold. This technique is naturally compatible with LatticeFold+.
