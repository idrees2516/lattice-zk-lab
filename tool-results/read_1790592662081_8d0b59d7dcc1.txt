    19→    Abstract. Lattice-based polynomial commitment schemes (PCSs) promise post-quantum
    20→    SNARKs with two properties that elliptic curves provide and hash-based schemes, today’s
    21→    deployed post-quantum default, do not: concretely small proofs and commitment time propor-
    22→    tional to the number of nonzero entries in the committed polynomial rather than its length.
    23→    The second property is essential to Twist and Shout (CRYPTO 2026), the fastest known
    24→    memory-checking arguments and a core component of the Jolt zero-knowledge virtual machine
    25→    (zkVM): their prover commits to enormous polynomials that are almost entirely zero. Yet
    26→    despite a wave of recent work, existing lattice-based PCSs achieve at most two of the three
    27→    properties that deployment demands: small proof size, fast verification, and soundness from
    28→    standard assumptions such as Module-SIS.
    29→        We present Akita, a lattice-based PCS that achieves all three. We improve on the square-
    30→    root-time verifier of Hachi (ePrint 2026), our direct predecessor, through a new setup offloading
    31→    technique: the public setup matrices are committed ahead of time, and the verifier’s work in
    32→    processing them is deferred and proved against these commitments. For any fixed K ≥ 2, this
    33→    reduces verification time to O eK,λ (N 1/K ) while preserving OeK,λ (log N ) proof size, O
    34→                                                                                             eK,λ (N )
    35→    prover time, and security from standard Module-SIS. We also optimize every fold from root to
    36→    tail and iterate the fold to completion. This includes an optimized digit range check, relation-
    37→    specific ring dimensions and subring challenges, complementary methods for embedding field
    38→    evaluations and checking ring relations, commitments compressed to 128 bytes each, and exact
    39→    Euclidean norm checks for tighter Module-SIS parameters.
    40→        Beyond the core protocol, Akita provides the capabilities needed for deployment in a zkVM:
    41→    batched openings of separately committed polynomials, low-communication distributed proving,
    42→    and an offline planner for selecting secure parameters under configurable cost objectives. We
    43→    implement Akita in Rust and benchmark it against existing lattice-based and hash-based PCSs.
    44→    Across these benchmarks, Akita produces proofs of only 61–72 KB, matching Greyhound’s when
    45→    both schemes are calibrated to the same lattice-security target, while verifying 19.2× to 89.7×
    46→    faster. Akita’s prover uses the least memory: beyond storing the polynomial itself, its memory
    47→    overhead grows sublinearly in the polynomial size. We also integrate Akita into Jolt. For every
    48→    program size we evaluate, Jolt-with-Akita achieves a 1.3× to 2.2× prover speedup and 2.2× to
    49→    7.4× verifier speedup over Jolt-with-Dory, while producing comparable proof sizes, with every
    50→    proof below 100 KB.

   177→1   Introduction
   178→Succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (SNARKs) are undergoing the same post-quantum transi-
   179→tion as the rest of deployed cryptography, away from the elliptic curves that many SNARKs in production
   180→today [45,41,26] are based on. Most SNARKs follow a common template: an information-theoretic polyno-
   181→mial interactive oracle proof (PIOP) [29,27] composed with a polynomial commitment scheme (PCS) [55].
   182→The migration thus concentrates in the PCS: replacing a curve-based PCS with a post-quantum one makes
   183→the entire argument post-quantum.1
   184→    In practice, this “post-quantum PCS” slot has been filled almost entirely by hash-based schemes: Reed-
   185→Solomon-based protocols such as FRI [15], STIR [8], and WHIR [9]; general code-based multilinear com-
   186→mitments such as Ligero [6], Brakedown [44], BaseFold [89], and Ligerito [81]; and the binary-field line of
   187→Binius [34,35], Blaze [25], and Bolt [47]. These now underpin deployed proof systems at scale, including
   188→Succinct’s SP1 [87], Lighter’s Plonky2-based exchange rollup [69], and the many provers in the Ethereum
   189→Foundation’s real-time proving effort, Ethproofs [36,37].
   190→    The dominant hash-based PCS paradigm gives up two advantages of elliptic-curve commitments that
   191→matter: small proofs and pay-per-bit commitment time. On proof size, its concrete proofs commonly reach
   192→hundreds of kilobytes: with proven soundness (that is, no reliance on conjectured proximity gaps [7,38]) and
   193→practical code rates such as 1/2 or 1/4 (avoiding the prover slowdown of lower rates), the smallest proofs
   194→(using WHIR) are O(λ2 log N + λ log2 N/ log log N ) asymptotically, where N is the polynomial size, and
   195→at least 160KB for non-trivial instances  P [9,67]. On commitment time, a curve-based commitment applies a
   196→linear map directly to the data, m 7→ i mi Gi , for fixed public group elements Gi . Committing to a sparse
   197→vector therefore costs only its support, the set of nonzero entries. The dominant Reed–Solomon-and-Merkle
   198→construction instead commits to a dense error-corrected codeword, so its standard commitment procedure
   199→processes the entire codeword even when the original vector is sparse.2
   200→    Lattice-based PCSs recover both properties in principle: an Ajtai-style commitment applies a linear map
   201→to a small-integer decomposition of the message, retaining pay-per-bit commitment time. Some schemes
   202→achieve logarithmic-size opening proofs; among these, some attain concrete proof sizes as small as 50–
   203→60 KB [78,76]. A flurry of recent constructions [16,78,76,52,56,57,58,53,51] has pushed the family toward
   204→practicality, yet none is ready to be deployed, in a sense we make precise by looking at what our target
   205→application demands.3
   206→    Jolt [10,11] is a zero-knowledge virtual machine (zkVM) that proves correct execution of (64-bit) RISC-
   207→V programs, and its Twist and Shout memory-checking arguments [86] commit to one-hot polynomials:
   208→concatenations of standard basis vectors ej , one per execution step, with j the memory cell that step touched.
   209→Hence, we first desire that commitments and openings exhibit pay-per-bit cost. Second, the opening protocol
   210→must verify in tens of milliseconds rather than hundreds of milliseconds or seconds. Third, proof size should
   211→be concretely small, tens of kilobytes rather than the hundreds that hash-based schemes pay, and finally,
   212→security should rest on standard, well-studied assumptions. Jolt today uses Dory [68], a curve-based PCS
   213→that satisfies all four, but is not post-quantum secure; a lattice-based PCS built on Ajtai commitments seems
   214→to be the natural replacement.
   215→    What, then, prevents Jolt from adopting an existing lattice-based PCS? For existing constructions, the
   216→latter three requirements form a trilemma: each scheme achieves at most two of the three. Greyhound [78]
   217→and Hachi [76] obtain small proofs from standard Module-SIS,4 but verify in time square-root in the polyno-
   218→mial size. Alternate designs such as RoKoKo [58] and Grand Danois [53] reach polylogarithmic verification
   219→1
   220→  A rigorous proof requires security against quantum adversaries, which means, among other things, that Fiat-Shamir
   221→  security needs to be in the quantum random oracle model (QROM); we discuss the status of Akita in Section 1.1.
   222→2
   223→  This linear cost is not inherent to every code. Systematic-code constructions such as Brakedown and Bolt [44,47]
   224→  retain the original message as part of the codeword, and hence could benefit from one-hot or other highly repetitive
   225→  structure, though the practical speedups remain unclear.
   226→3
   227→  The closest to deployment is the recent LaBRADOR-based zero-knowledge toolkit of Biasioli et al. [17], which
   228→  extends the LaZer library with a concrete implementation. Its verification time is linear, as in LaBRADOR: on
   229→  large instances, verification takes more than one second and runs at one-third to one-half of the proving time (see
   230→  Tables 1 and 2 of [17]).
   231→4
   232→  Module-SIS (along with Module-LWE and their unstructured versions) are among the most well-studied lattice
   233→  assumptions, with known worst-case-to-average-case reductions [2,60], an extensive history of cryptanalysis, and
   234→  underlying the security of NIST-standardized ML-DSA [75].
   235→6        Dao, Bodaghi, Khajehpour, Vitto et al.
   236→
   237→by leaning on stronger, more structured assumption variants (vanishing SIS [30]) that have received far less
   238→cryptanalytic attention, and the former also on a heuristic challenge set ensemble. The final set of schemes
   239→obtains polylogarithmic verification from standard lattice assumptions [23,3,31], but either gives no concrete
   240→parameterization or carries proofs in the megabyte range. This trilemma is only the first obstacle. A de-
   241→ployable PCS must also provide the specific capabilities that a zkVM requires. Namely, Jolt commits to its
   242→execution trace, untrusted advice, and preprocessed program data at different stages of the protocol, includ-
   243→ing offline stages, then opens them together in one proof, each commitment’s parameters fixed long before
   244→the online commitments are known. Its prover must scale beyond a single machine for large statements;
   245→its many interacting parameters must be chosen mechanically and auditably rather than by hand; and its
   246→implementation must withstand scrutiny.5
   247→    This brings us to the central question this paper sets out to answer:
   248→
   249→      Can we design and implement a lattice-based PCS with concretely small proofs, a fast
   250→                      verifier, and security from standard Module-SIS?

   253→1.1    Our Contributions
   254→
   255→We answer this question with Akita, a new lattice-based PCS that satisfies all three properties. We build
   256→on Greyhound and Hachi, which achieve small proofs under standard Module-SIS but retain a square-
   257→root-time verifier [78,76]. Our new setup offloading technique reduces the verifier’s asymptotic work to
   258→any constant root; with concrete parameter tuning, single-threaded verification takes less than 20 ms even
   259→for a 230 -sized polynomial (Theorem 1.1 and Section 13). We also introduce techniques that substantially
   260→improve Hachi’s central folding reduction, making its range checks and ring-relation checks cheaper and
   261→its commitments smaller, and tune these techniques across the recursion to make folding to completion
   262→practical. Our evaluation shows that Akita is competitive with existing lattice-based and hash-based PCSs,
   263→and when integrated into Jolt, gives 1.3–2.2× faster proving and 2.2–7.4× faster verification, with proofs
   264→remaining below 100 KB (Section 13). We prove noninteractive knowledge soundness of Akita in the classical
   265→random oracle model (ROM); a proof in the quantum random oracle model (QROM) remains future work
   266→(Sections 10 and 14).6

   268→Verifier offloading and constant-root verification. We start by isolating the two contributions to Hachi’s
   269→verifier cost, and explain how we overcome the square-root barrier. For a multilinear polynomial represented
   270→by N Boolean evaluations, Hachi divides these evaluations into B blocks; in the first fold, the verifier processes
   271→B folding weights and evaluates a public commitment
   272→                                               √       matrix on a block of length roughly N/B. These costs
   273→sum to roughly B + N/B, minimized at B ≈ N ; using fewer blocks is offset by a larger matrix evaluation
   274→(Section 9.1).
   275→    We break this tradeoff by asking the prover to prove the matrix contribution using Akita itself. Since the
   276→setup is public and fixed in advance, we can commit to the required setup prefixes during preprocessing.7
   277→At opening time, an inner-product sum-check reduces the matrix computation to an evaluation of a setup
   278→polynomial. We pass this opening to the next fold alongside the recursive-witness opening, so both claims
   279→are checked in that fold against their respective commitments (Section 9). We can repeat this process in
   280→subsequent folds until offloading no longer provides a benefit, then return to direct matrix evaluation.
   281→    We tune the block counts and offloading depth to obtain an asymptotically Kth-root verifier for any
   282→constant K ≥ 2, while preserving quasilinear prover time and polylogarithmic proof size. The recursion
   283→schedules keep the combined witness and setup obligations shrinking through the recursion, giving the
   284→following guarantee.

   344→An optimized digit range check. Norm checks are a central component of lattice-based proof systems; existing
   345→works either check coefficient-wise (L∞ ) or Euclidean (L2 ) norm of the recursive witness. Hachi uses sum-
   346→check to prove the former, which take the form of a degree-b vanishing polynomial for a balanced b-element
   347→digit alphabet;8 for L digits, this incurs O(b2 L) prover work and Θ(b log L) proof size. The optimizations we
   348→provide reduce the prover time to O(bL), and, with equality-factored sum-checks [46], the range sum-check
   349→round communication to 2(log2 b − 1)⌈log2 L⌉ + O(1) challenge-field elements; see Sections 2.3 and 6.1.
   350→    Our first optimization exploits the symmetry of the balanced alphabet: since the digits k and −(k + 1)
   351→give the same value under w 7→ w(w + 1), we only need to check half as many transformed values. We then
   352→apply a GKR-style product-tree reduction [43] to this smaller (b/2)-degree predicate, checking layers with
   353→branching factors of two or four to give the smallest proof size. Our implementation further accelerates the
   354→prover using the small-value techniques of Dao et al. [33].

   366→norm checks (in addition to the digit range check above) to reduce the size of the remaining witness parts.
   367→These considerations are possible due to the different ways in which we embed evaluation claims, check ring
   368→equations, send commitments, and bound responses across the recursion.
   369→    First, to embed evaluation claims for the large early witnesses, we pack coefficients directly into the
   370→commitment ring and restrict folding challenges to a subring, allowing each block’s partial evaluation to
   371→occupy fewer coordinates. In later folds, Hachi’s trace-based packing permits smaller commitment rings and
   372→shorter opening material, at the cost of using Diamond and Posen’s tensor reduction [35] to connect the
   373→original base-field evaluation to the packed claim. This reduction supplies the binding missing from Hachi’s
   374→base-field shortcut [76, Section 3.2, p. 14] (Appendix F.1). We also incorporate the trace identity directly
   375→into the field relation, removing the ring element Hachi transmits for its trace check (Section 2.5).
   376→    Second, we introduce a quotient-free method for checking the ring equations over a field, using sum-check
   377→over the field itself. The alternative used in Hachi is the quotient-lifting technique of Huang, Mao, and
   378→Zhang [50], whose tensor-structured matrix weights give cheaper prover and verifier evaluations, but need
   379→to supply additional quotient polynomials that become extra witness material for subsequent folds. Our
   380→quotient-free method instead incorporate the ring reduction into public weights, using the same linear map
   381→that concurrent Grand Danois [53] expresses through a rotation matrix. These weights are concretely more
   382→expensive to process (though asymptotically the same), but eliminating the quotient material benefits the tail
   383→end, where witness savings are important. We otherwise adopt the ring quotient-lift sum-check unchanged
   384→from Hachi at the early folds (Section 2.6).
   385→    Third, we reduce the size of the two commitments sent in each fold by compressing them to only 128
   386→bytes each. The idea is simple: we start with normal commitments (which is anywhere from 1 − 8KB), then
   387→repeatedly digit-decompose then re-commit, using binary digits for the smallest final payload; for 128-bit
   388→security, two rounds of this is enough (Section 2.7). This closely matches the idea in Orthus [18]; however, we
   389→use a more gradual process of compression, compared to their one-step approach, which yields compressed
   390→commitments more than twice as large (Appendix F.2), and handle the resulting binary-ness obligation
   391→differently, by fusing with the already-present digit range check. We eventually stop compression when the
   392→compression binary digits starts becoming a non-trivial part of the next witness.
   393→    Fourth, we further reduce the witness size by adopting sum-checks that certify an exact bound on the
   394→response’s total squared norm, combined with possible operator-norm rejection sampling for the ring folding
   395→challenges.9 While both are established ideas and adopted in many prior works, here our contributions
   396→are two-fold: we verify exact, not approximate L2 norm, and we seamlessly integrate this check with the
   397→existing sum-check instances for range and ring-relation checks (Section 2.8). When the field is too small for
   398→the whole-response bound to fit, we prove smaller inner products between the digits, recover their integer
   399→values, and reconstruct the full squared norm over the integers (Section 6.2). Finally, we adopt LaBRADOR’s
   400→specialized handling of the terminal opening. In particular, we use a single layer of commitment, send the
   401→partial opening evaluations instead of committing to them, and send only the folded response last.

   403→Batching commitments formed at different times. We support opening independently formed commitments
   404→in one proof, as required by Jolt, which commits to its trace, advice polynomials, and preprocessed data at
   405→different stages of execution. This builds on Greyhound’s batching [78, §3.2], but extends it to handle these
   406→temporally separated cases. The main issue in our setting is the digit alphabet certified by the shared range
   407→check. We fix this alphabet before forming the commitments,10 while allowing the source, carried-digit, and
   408→response bases to differ. Their digits must fit the common alphabet, and each commitment must be sized
   409→for its full digit-difference bound. We impose the same compatibility condition when batching a setup-prefix
   410→opening with the next recursive witness (Sections 4.6 and 5.5).

   430→Offline planning and independent validation. Akita is a complex protocol with many moving parameters,
   431→making it a non-trivial task to choose parameters that lead to secure and efficient protocols. While many
   432→parameters have clear, Pareto-optimal choices, others may not, wherein increasing one may positively affect
   433→some aspects (e.g., proof size or prove time) at the expense of another (e.g., verifier time).
   434→   We provide a comprehensive offline planner that does this automatically, given the input polynomial
   435→shapes. Our planner searches exhaustively over complete recursive schedules; For each candidate fold, it
   436→derives the witness and any setup opening passed to its successor, then compares the cost of finishing the
   437→proof. We set the planner to roughly prioritize for verifier efficiency (via the minimum setup size as a rough
   438→metric), followed by proof size and prover work. A selected schedule, once created, is validated to be secure
   439→independently of the search. We provide a separate validator that reconstructs every folding transitions and
   440→checks the protocol and security conditions. Prover and verifier then reuse the validated schedule (Sections 10
   441→and 12.3).
   442→
   443→Implementation and evaluation. We implement Akita in Rust with optimized field and cyclotomic-ring arith-
   444→metic, sparse commitments for Jolt’s one-hot inputs, batched openings, distributed proving, and independent
   445→schedule validation. Our evaluation answers two questions: whether Akita is competitive with existing lattice-
   446→based and hash-based PCSs, and whether Jolt integrated with Akita is meaningfully better than the current
   447→version of Jolt (with Dory as the PCS). We report on the numbers, including Akita’s strengths and current
   448→weaknesses in (Section 13).
   449→   Another standalone implementation we provide is a SIS estimator written in Rust, following the Python /
   450→Sage version [4]. Its efficiency is around 3 orders of magnitude over the latter, which provides the scalability
   451→needed to generate a comprehensive table of secure SIS parameters; our planner subsequently uses these
   452→cached tables to generate schedules.

   455→1.2   Related Work
   456→
   457→From LaBRADOR to Hachi. We build on a line of lattice arguments that predates the first lattice PCS.
   458→Bootle, Lyubashevsky, Nguyen, and Seiler developed sublinear lattice arguments without PCPs [24], while
   459→Bootle, Chiesa, and Sotiraki brought sum-check into this setting [22]. ALS20 introduced the weak-opening
   460→abstraction and the cross-multiplication argument that underlies its binding [12]. LaBRADOR assembled
   461→these ingredients into a recursively self-composed argument with a two-tier Ajtai commitment and an explicit
   462→norm reset [16]. Its proofs grow logarithmically, with reported sizes of 47–58 KB for R1CS instances with
   463→210 –220 constraints, but its verifier remains linear in the witness size [16]. Our aim is to retain this concrete
   464→compactness while reducing the verifier work.
   465→    The short ring challenges used in this lineage have a still older provenance. Sparse, small-norm challenges
   466→already appear in lattice identification and Fiat–Shamir protocols [71]. Lyubashevsky and Seiler [73] proved
   467→the small-element invertibility theorem for partially splitting cyclotomic rings that makes the difference of
   468→two such challenges invertible.
   469→    Greyhound turned the LaBRADOR machinery into the first concretely efficient lattice PCS [78]. Its split-
   470→and-fold evaluation protocol has a square-root verifier, and recursive compression gives a reported 53 KB
   471→proof at degree 230 . Hachi moves the verification of a fold from ring arithmetic to field arithmetic using
   472→two ingredients [76]. Its trace identity for embedding extension-field inner products into a cyclotomic ring
   473→generalizes the trace technique of LNP22 [72]; its reduction of ring relations to field sum-check is the quotient-
   474→based ring-switching method of Huang, Mao, and Zhang [50]. Hachi reports a 12.5× verification speedup over
   475→Greyhound at comparable proof size, but its concrete protocol performs one Hachi fold before delegating the
   476→remaining witness to a Greyhound tail.
   477→10     Dao, Bodaghi, Khajehpour, Vitto et al.
   478→
   479→Recent extensions of LaBRADOR. The LaZer toolkit makes LaBRADOR useful for witness-private ap-
   480→plications by integrating the linear-size zero-knowledge proof of LNP22 and implementing the resulting
   481→composition [17,72]. Orthus addresses a different limitation: it adds a reduction for batched lattice relations
   482→that achieves square-root verification under standard assumptions [18]. These are substantive advances in
   483→privacy and verification, respectively. Akita’s verifier offers substantially better runtimes, even when used in
   484→no-offloading mode against Orthus’ verifier, while still having security based on standard Module-SIS.
   485→
   486→Lattice PCSs with single-level folding. CELPC [52] obtains square-root proof size and verification with a
   487→transparent setup. Jindo [51] combines ideas from CELPC and Greyhound for client-side proving, reaching
   488→cube-root communication and verification and reporting an order-of-magnitude improvement over CELPC
   489→across its measured metrics. Both schemes fold only once, leading to large proof sizes but no further recursive
   490→folds. Jindo’s concrete parameter choices also include a heuristic challenge set.
   491→
   492→Asymptotically succinct lattice PCSs. A separate line aims directly for polylogarithmic proof size and ver-
   493→ification. Fenzi, Moghaddas, and Nguyen [39] construct a PCS under a ring variant of BASIS; SLAP [3]
   494→uses a multi-instance Power-Ring BASIS assumption and gives a further reduction to Module-SIS; and Cini,
   495→Malavolta, Nguyen, and Wee [31] give a transparent-setup construction from standard Module-SIS with
   496→knowledge soundness against quantum adversaries. These results obtain asymptotics stronger than Grey-
   497→hound and Hachi, but their concrete proof sizes are much larger at the scales we target. At degree 230 , the
   498→respective papers report proofs of 8.3 MB, 767 MB, and 5.17 MB [39,3,31]. We approach the tradeoff from
   499→the other direction: we retain the small concrete proofs of the Greyhound and Hachi family, then further
   500→optimize the verifier concretely and asymptotically.
   501→
   502→Verifier-succinct arguments from structured assumptions. RoK, Paper, SISsors [56] develops verifier-succinct
   503→reductions of knowledge for exact norm bounds; RoK and Roll [57] adds structured random projections;
   504→SALSAA [59] integrates sum-check to obtain linear prover time; and RoKoKo [58] commits the folding cross-
   505→terms rather than sending them, thereby supporting large folding arity. This line obtains polylogarithmic
   506→verification by giving the public matrices additional algebraic structure, with its strongest concrete results
   507→stated under vanishing SIS or related structured assumptions. We do not claim these asymptotics. Instead,
   508→we retain ordinary Module-SIS and similar (though still somewhat higher) verifier runtime in practice, thanks
   509→to setup offloading (Section 9).
   510→
   511→Concurrent work: Grand Danois. Closest to Akita within the preceding line, Grand Danois [53] concurrently
   512→proposes a lattice PCS with polylogarithmic verification under Module-SIS and vanishing SIS. Its current
   513→version checks multiplication by a public ring element through a random field projection of the corresponding
   514→negacyclic rotation matrix. This produces the same coefficients as our quotient-free transpose-convolution
   515→check. Grand Danois uses that direct check throughout a fixed-dimension relation and obtains succinct setup
   516→evaluation from its tensor-structured assumption. Our work points out the tradeoff of this method against the
   517→quotient-lifting method used in Hachi, and only selectively chooses it at the tail of Akita. Grand Danois also
   518→integrates a Johnson–Lindenstrauss norm proof into the Greyhound relation and estimates roughly 80 KB
   519→proofs for 232 -size evaluations, though without any implementation.
   520→
   521→Lattice-based folding schemes. A parallel line studies folding for incrementally verifiable computation and
   522→proof-carrying data. LatticeFold [19] gave the first folding scheme from Module-SIS; LatticeFold+ [20] re-
   523→placed its bit-decomposition range proof with an algebraic one; Neo and SuperNeo [79,80] obtained pay-
   524→per-bit commitment costs over small fields; Cyclo [42] obtained additive accumulator-norm growth; Sym-
   525→phony [28] used high-arity folding in a lattice SNARK compiler; and ProtogaLattice [13] folded high-degree
   526→relations in a constant number of rounds. Akita shares many techniques and security analyses with these
   527→works, but is designed as a lattice-based PCS rather than a folding scheme; we view extending Akita’s
   528→technique to construct a more practical folding scheme to be exciting future work.
   529→
   530→Concurrent work: PikkuFold. Building on the fixed-weight challenges and operator-norm rejection sketched in
   531→LaBRADOR [16], PikkuFold [82] gives a rigorous fixed-Hamming-weight base family with pairwise-invertible
   532→differences under the exact partial-splitting conditions of LS18, then measures the concrete rejection rates. We
   533→compare with Akita’s effort on this below. PikkuFold also proves a concrete modular Johnson–Lindenstrauss
   534→theorem; Akita uses no Johnson–Lindenstrauss projection.

   537→Concrete challenge-set instantiations. Low-norm folding challenges must allow extraction from invertible
   538→challenge differences. One approach guarantees that every pair of distinct challenges has an invertible differ-
   539→ence, as in the LS18 families. Another uses approximate sampling and bounds the probability of a noninvert-
   540→ible difference, allowing rings with greater splitting and faster arithmetic. RoKoKo uses the latter approach:

   547→Choosing parameters and schedules. We briefly compare how Akita does parameter selection compared to
   548→other lattice-based schemes. The Greyhound implementation adapts to online-computed folded witness norms
   549→during proving, while the LaZer LaBRADOR implementation generates a parameter sequence beforehand

   555→Hash-based PCSs. The hash-based line, from FRI [15] through STIR [8], BaseFold [89], Ligerito [81], and
