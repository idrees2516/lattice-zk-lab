    13→    13→         Abstract. Folding is a powerful technique for constructing efficient succinct proof systems, especially
    14→    14→         for computations that are expressed in a streaming fashion. In this work, we present Cyclo, a new lattice-
    15→    15→         based folding protocol that improves upon LatticeFold+ [Boneh and Chen ’25] in multiple dimensions
    16→    16→         and which incorporates, among others, the pay-per-bit techniques from Neo when folding constraints
    17→    17→         expressed over a field Fq [Nguyen and Setty ’25]. Cyclo proposes a new framework for building lattice-
    18→    18→         based folding schemes that eliminates the need for norm checks on the accumulator by adopting an
    19→    19→         amortized norm-refreshing design, ensuring that the witness norm grows additively per round within a
    20→    20→         (generously) bounded number of folds. This design simplifies the protocol and reduces prover overhead.
    21→    21→         In particular, Cyclo only performs range checks on the input non-accumulated witness, and when ap-
    22→    22→         plied to fold constraints over Fq , it does not decompose any witnesses into low-norm chunks within the
    23→    23→         folding protocol itself. Cyclo, supporting a complete family of cyclotomic rings, combines two simple
    24→    24→         building blocks: an extension commitment that reduces the norm of the witness by decomposing it and
    25→    25→         recommitting, and an ℓ∞ range test via a sum-check protocol. We demonstrate, by proving communi-
    26→    26→         cation and runtime estimates that the construction results in an efficient and proof-size-friendly folding
    27→    27→         scheme. We also establish an algebraic connection between Rq and Fq using the polynomial evaluation
    28→    28→         map, enabling efficient reduction from R1CS/CCS over Fq to a linear relation over Rq , providing a new
    29→    29→         and simpler formulation of the techniques in [Nguyen and Setty ’25]. In practical settings, Cyclo achieves
    30→    30→         succinct proof sizes on the order of 30 KB, improving by an order of magnitude over LatticeFold+. Our
    31→    31→         efficiency benchmarks indicate that our protocol also outperforms LatticeFold+ in practice.

    72→    72→1     Introduction
    73→    73→
    74→    74→In recent years, succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (SNARKs) have become central
    75→    75→to applications such as blockchains [XZC+ 22,GKO24,LM25,LZW+ 25], privacy-preserving authen-
    76→    76→tication [EHRS24], and verifiable computation [ACGS24,CCC+ 25]. Despite their power, SNARKs
    77→    77→suffer from a major memory bottleneck that limits their applicability[LZW+ 24,ZSCZ25]: the prover
    78→    78→must load the entire computation into memory. This is infeasible for large computations and, more
    79→    79→broadly, fails to capture how computations are often carried out in practice, namely in a streaming
    80→    80→manner.
    81→    81→    This setting, motivated in particular by practical applications such as blockchains and verifi-
    82→    82→able outsourced computation (e.g., cloud/edge ML inference and long-running services), has led
    83→    83→to the framework of incrementally verifiable computation (IVC) [Val08,NPR19] and its generaliza-
    84→    84→tion, proof-carrying data [CT10,BCL+ 21,BDFG21,CCG+ 23]. Early IVC protocols were built from
    85→    85→SNARKs and can be viewed as producing, at each step, a proof for the computation performed
    86→    86→by the verifier in the previous step [Val08,BCCT12,BCTV14]. More modern approaches use folding
    87→    87→schemes [BGH19,BDFG21,BCL+ 21,BC25a,BC25b], which let the prover aggregate many compu-
    88→    88→tation steps into a single instance of an accumulator relation. This is substantially simpler than
    89→    89→proving the entire computation at each step and yields improved efficiency.
    90→    90→    Folding can be understood via reductions of knowledge [ACK21,KP22]. A reduction of knowledge
    91→    91→from a relation Ξ0 to a relation Ξ1 is a protocol Π between a prover and a verifier in which the
    92→    92→                                        x
    93→    93→verifier, given an instance 0 for Ξ0 , interacts with the prover and ultimately outputs an instance
    94→    94→x 1 for Ξ1 . The key property is that if the prover can provide a witness        1 for 1 , then one can         w          x
    95→    95→                           w           x
    96→    96→extract a witness 0 for 0 from the prover. A folding scheme realizes a reduction of knowledge
    97→    97→from the product relation Ξacc × Ξ0 to the accumulation relation Ξacc : it folds a pair of instances
    98→    98→ x x                                                  x
    99→    99→( acc , 0 ) into a single new instance ′acc of Ξacc . Repeating this reduction lets the prover aggregate
   100→   100→many computation steps (expressed by Ξ0 ) into a single instance of the accumulator relation Ξacc .
   101→   101→This intuition also extends naturally to multiple-input relations, allowing folding with inputs from
   102→   102→Ξacc × (Ξ0 × · · · × Ξd−1 ).
   103→   103→    The best-established folding schemes rely on linearly homomorphic commitments, such as Ped-
   104→   104→ersen commitments [Ped92], to commit to the witness of the input relation. Folding then amounts
   105→   105→                                    Cyclo: Lightweight Lattice-based Folding via Partial Range Checks   3
   106→   106→
   107→   107→to combining the per-step commitments into a single commitment to the folded instance. Unfortu-
   108→   108→nately, Pedersen relies on the discrete logarithm assumption and is therefore vulnerable to quantum
   109→   109→attacks [Sho94]. A compelling alternative is the Ajtai commitment [Ajt96], which is based on the
   110→   110→hardness of lattice problems and is widely viewed as post-quantum secure; we discuss lattice-based
   111→   111→folding schemes below. Another line of work builds folding schemes from hash-based commitments
   112→   112→and error-correcting codes [BMNW25a,BCFW25,BMNW25b].
   113→   113→Lattice-based folding schemes. A major recent advance is the development of lattice-based fold-
   114→   114→ing schemes, including Lova [FKNP24], LatticeFold [BC25a], LatticeFold+ [BC25b], and Neo [NS25].
   115→   115→These schemes replace Pedersen commitments with plausibly quantum-secure Ajtai commitments,
   116→   116→yielding plausible post-quantum security. However, translating Pedersen-based folding to the lat-
   117→   117→tice setting is not straightforward: Ajtai commitments critically depend on the committed message
   118→   118→being short, i.e., the witness having low norm. This shortness constraint creates difficulties because
   119→   119→the witness norm typically grows with each folding step. Moreover, norm growth arises not only
   120→   120→in folding (the “correctness direction”), but also in extraction (the “extraction direction”), when
   121→   121→recovering a witness for the input relation from a witness for the folded relation. This growth com-
   122→   122→plicates the soundness analysis, since one must ensure that the witness norm stays within acceptable
   123→   123→bounds throughout folding.
   124→   124→    To address the norm growth issues, prior lattice-based folding schemes combine two general
   125→   125→techniques, which are afterward instantiated differently in each folding scheme:
   126→   126→
   127→   127→ – Decomposition. Given a witness vector of norm at most B, this procedure decomposes it into
   128→   128→   chunks of smaller norm b. The chunks are later randomly combined using low-norm verifier
   129→   129→   challenges so that the resulting linear combination has norm at most B. This step is needed for
   130→   130→   completeness, i.e., to ensure the accumulated output witness remains low-norm.
   131→   131→ – Range-check. These are variants of range proofs that certify a witness vector satisfies a prescribed
   132→   132→   bound. This step is needed for soundness, ensuring that extractors output low-norm witness
   133→   133→   vectors.
   134→   134→Under this lens, LatticeFold and LatticeFold+ exhibit the following cost profiles. LatticeFold uses
   135→   135→grand-product-sum-based range-checks for very small bounds (e.g., b = 2). A single such range
   136→   136→check, as we show in this work, is efficient. However, during decomposition, LatticeFold splits each
   137→   137→input witness into k low-norm chunks, commits to them, and then applies a sum-check-based range-
   138→   138→check to each chunk (the sum-checks are batched, but prover time still grows linearly in 2k); hence
   139→   139→the overall combined procedure is relatively expensive, cf. [Res24]. In contrast, LatticeFold+ uses a
   140→   140→very lightweight decomposition step (it decomposes into only 2 vectors).
   141→   141→    The most efficient instantiations of lattice-based primitives use cyclotomic rings of the form
   142→   142→R = Z[X]/⟨Φf (X)⟩, where Φf (X) is the f-th cyclotomic polynomial. Over R, the LatticeFold+ range-
   143→   143→check (which computes so-called double-commitments) is relatively costly: it requires computing at
   144→   144→least φ (the ring degree) “single” Ajtai commitments and then re-committing to them. A cheap
   145→   145→grand-product sum-check-based range-check is unavailable here because the verified norm bound B
   146→   146→is not small enough. Other norm checks (tailored to the ℓ2 -norm) have been used in lattice-based
   147→   147→argument systems [BS23,KLNO24,KLNO25b] and transfer almost directly to the folding setting.
   148→   148→Lattice-based folding schemes for constraints expressed over finite fields. The ring R
   149→   149→admits a natural embedding into Zφ(f) , where φ is Euler’s totient function. However, in many
   150→   150→cryptographic protocols, relations are defined over finite fields Fq . Thus, using cyclotomic rings in
   151→   151→such protocols requires translating relations from Fq to Rq = R/qR. This translation is nontrivial:
   152→   152→4       Albert Garreta, Helger Lipmaa, Urmas Luhaäär, and Michal Osadnik
   153→   153→
   154→   154→one must preserve the algebraic structure of the original relation in the ring setting. Moreover,
   155→   155→Fq -based relations (e.g., R1CS or CCS) typically omit norm constraints, which are essential for
   156→   156→lattice-based security. Accordingly, the translation must also include an appropriate decomposition
   157→   157→so that the resulting relation over Rq is compatible with Ajtai commitments.
   158→   158→    A natural idea is to use the Number Theoretic Transform (NTT), which provides a ring isomor-
   159→   159→                   φ(f)
   160→   160→phism Rq ∼   = Fq (when q splits Φf (X) completely), mapping each ring element to its evaluations
   161→   161→at the roots of Φf (X). Each coordinate of this embedding lies in Fq , so one can encode field elements
   162→   162→into individual “slots” of a ring element. For example, consider the cyclotomic ring Z[X]/⟨X 4 + 1⟩
   163→   163→with q = 17. Since X 4 +1 splits completely modulo 17 as (X −2)(X −4)(X −8)(X −15), the Chinese
   164→   164→Remainder Theorem gives an isomorphism Rq ∼          = F417 , mapping a polynomial f (X) to its evaluations
   165→   165→(f (2), f (4), f (8), f (15)). Thus, four field elements a0 , a1 , a2 , a3 ∈ F17 can be “packed” into one ring
   166→   166→element by finding the unique polynomial f (X) ∈ Rq satisfying f (2) = a0 , f (4) = a1 , f (8) = a2 ,
   167→   167→f (15) = a3 . Nevertheless, this approach faces several challenges: (1) The NTT embedding is useful
   168→   168→only when the relation can be “packed” into slots, which typically requires working with multiple
   169→   169→instances of the relation. This is not always possible. Otherwise, the embedding can impose substan-
   170→   170→tial overhead, since the relation must be expanded to fill the slots, increasing complexity. (2) Even
   171→   171→if the Fq witness has small norm, there is no guarantee that the corresponding witness over Rq does,
   172→   172→which is crucial both for the security of Ajtai commitments and for the efficiency of the commitment
   173→   173→step. Hence, the Rq relation must be further decomposed to enforce norm bounds. This decomposi-
   174→   174→tion can be intricate and may inflate proofs and computation, potentially offsetting any gains from
   175→   175→packing multiple field elements into one ring element. (3) Naively encoding Fq -native relations over
   176→   176→Rq forces the folding scheme to operate over Rq . This can be wasteful: operations in Rq are far
   177→   177→more expensive than in Fq , and ring elements are much larger than field elements. It is therefore
   178→   178→desirable to minimize computation over Rq . (4) The NTT embedding requires a modulus q that
   179→   179→splits the cyclotomic polynomial Φf (X), which can severely restrict parameter choices and may be
   180→   180→impractical. Worse, this splitting condition can conflict with other requirements, such as invertibility
   181→   181→of small-norm elements [LS18,ACX19,BS23] or the existence of large subfields [KLNO24].
   182→   182→    To overcome these challenges, Nguyen and Setty (Neo, [NS25]) propose a translation from Fq -
   183→   183→based relations to Rq -based relations that does not rely on the NTT embedding. Neo encodes a
   184→   184→field element c ∈ Fq as a ring element pc (X) ∈ Rq whose coefficients are the base-b decomposition
   185→   185→of c, for a suitable b. This resolves issues 1, 2, and 4 above. In addition, Neo performs one folding
   186→   186→step (analogous to the linearization step in Hypernova [KS24]) entirely over Fq , rather than over
   187→   187→Rq , where prover time would be substantially higher. This addresses issue 3.
   188→   188→    Although powerful, Neo [NS25] introduces fairly complex, ad hoc machinery and terminology
   189→   189→(e.g., matrix commitments and commutative subrings of matrices in place of standard ring-based
   190→   190→constructions). Building on Neo’s key ideas, we show that the same translation from Fq -based to Rq -
   191→   191→based relations can be achieved within a simpler framework that works exclusively with cyclotomic
   192→   192→rings, finite fields, and standard linear maps, yielding a more accessible and modular construction.

   194→   194→1.1   Our contributions
   195→   195→With this work, we make the following contributions:
   196→   196→No decomposition and range-check for input accumulated witnesses. As we mentioned,
   197→   197→all current lattice-based folding schemes from Ξacc × Ξ0 to Ξacc use range-checks to make sure that
   198→   198→the norm of the accumulated instances stays within some specified bound B. This range-check is
   199→   199→ultimately applied to both the input witness from the relation Ξ0 and the input witness from the
   200→   200→                                          Cyclo: Lightweight Lattice-based Folding via Partial Range Checks       5
   201→   201→
   202→   202→accumulated relation Ξacc . In this work we make the key observation that, if one omits the latter
   203→   203→range-check, then one can ensure that the norm growth of the output accumulated witness only grows
   204→   204→by a small additive factor (see Section 2.5 in the technical overview for more details).
   205→   205→   Consequently, we can perform folding without applying any decomposition or range-check to
   206→   206→the input witness from Ξacc , at the expense of not being able to perform arbitrarily many foldings:
   207→   207→indeed, at each folding step, the norm grows by a small additive factor, and so after a certain finite
   208→   208→number ℓfold of folds, the scheme stops being sound. We instantiate our scheme with ℓfold ranging
   209→   209→from 27 to 220 , which we believe is enough for overwhelmingly many applications. In addition, one
   210→   210→can use a different folding scheme, say LatticeFold+, once every ℓfold folding steps to “refresh” the
   211→   211→norm of the accumulated witness.
   212→   212→No witness decomposition when folding R1CS/CCS constraints over a finite field Fq
   213→   213→with an accumulated instance. As we discuss later, when seeking to apply our folding scheme
   214→   214→on R1CS/CCS constraints over Fq , we encode witnesses as cyclotomic ring elements of very low
   215→   215→norm b (say, b = 2), as done in Neo [NS25]. Thus, the input witness from Ξ0 does not require any
   216→   216→“decomposition,” unlike those mentioned earlier. In this specific setting, thanks to our key obser-
   217→   217→vation, we can perform (a bounded number of) foldings by only range-checking the input witness
   218→   218→from Ξ0 , and then folding with the input accumulated witness. In particular: (1) no decomposi-
   219→   219→tion step is ever applied to any witness, (2) range-check is only applied to the input witness from
   220→   220→Ξ0 , and (3) folding is performed in a standard manner between the range-checked input witness
   221→   221→from Ξ0 and the input accumulated witness. This avoids the most expensive steps of LatticeFold+,
   222→   222→Neo, and LatticeFold, namely the double commitment for the first two and the decomposition into
   223→   223→chunks and range-checking for every chunk in the latter. Ultimately, our scheme only requires two
   224→   224→non-batched4 sum-checks over an extension of Fq for log(mφ)-variate low-degree polynomials. Here,
   225→   225→m is the length of the packed witness w ∈ Rm  q in ring elements.
   226→   226→
   227→   227→Remark 1. We stress that the above efficiency gains refer to the folding protocol itself, without
   228→   228→considering the overhead of recursive verification (i.e., in-circuit verification of the folding scheme).
   229→   229→In the recursive setting, the benefit analysis is more nuanced. Encoding field elements as low-
   230→   230→norm polynomials in Rq may increase the witness representation size, since each Fq element is
   231→   231→represented as a ring element of potentially larger bit-length. However, the verifier does not operate
   232→   232→on the witness directly, and the absence of decomposition and extension commitment steps means
   233→   233→that the verifier performs fewer operations throughout the scheme. Therefore, the recursion circuit
   234→   234→can still be significantly smaller than in folding schemes that require decomposition and double
   235→   235→commitment. This advantage is further amplified if Cyclo is (trivially) modified so that the public
   236→   236→instance x (which does appear in the verifier’s circuit) is kept outside the Ajtai commitment and
   237→   237→can thus be represented as a vector of field elements rather than ring elements.
   238→   238→
   239→   239→A new take on witness decomposition + range-check. The fact that we do not need to apply
   240→   240→any decomposition or range-check to the input accumulated witness has yet another consequence
   241→   241→for us. Namely, we can design a new “decomposition + range-check step” using a technique we call
   242→   242→extension commitment (see the technical overview for more details). As we demonstrate, extension
   243→   243→commitments can be an order of magnitude more efficient than LatticeFold+’s double commitments.
   244→   244→4
   245→   245→    Some folding schemes, like LatticeFold, batch many sum-checks, say k, into one, which effectively increases the
   246→   246→    prover time linearly in k. In LatticeFold in particular, k is the number of chunks produced in the decomposition
   247→   247→    step
   248→   248→6       Albert Garreta, Helger Lipmaa, Urmas Luhaäär, and Michal Osadnik
   249→   249→
   250→   250→Our extension commitment is then followed by a simple sum-check-based range-check, which, as we
   251→   251→mentioned, is cheap when one does not need to batch many of them, which is our case.
   252→   252→   We emphasize that this technique is not usable in a setting where one must apply decomposition
   253→   253→and range-checking to the accumulated input witness. Hence, our aforementioned key observation
   254→   254→enables simpler approaches to the decomposition and range-check steps.
   255→   255→Parameter and efficiency improvements. Our folding scheme is efficient and memory-friendly,
