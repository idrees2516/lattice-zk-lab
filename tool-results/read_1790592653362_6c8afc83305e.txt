    14→        Abstract. We present RoKoko, a new lattice-based succinct argument system that achieves a linear-
    15→        time prover alongside polylogarithmic communication and verifier complexity. Asymptotically, our
    16→        construction improves upon RoK and Roll (ASIACRYPT 2025), the first post-quantum SNARK with
    17→        Õ(λ) proof size, by a multiplicative factor of Θ(log λ). Practically, our system yields proofs around 110 KB,
    18→        while outperforming the state-of-the-art polynomial commitment scheme Greyhound (CRYPTO 2024)
    19→        with ∼ 50× faster verification, similar prover time, and competitive proof size. Our framework natively
    20→        supports (tensor-)structured relations, such as polynomial evaluation and sumcheck relations.
    21→        At a high level, our construction follows the recursive split-and-fold paradigm: the prover first splits the
    22→        witness into ρ sub-witnesses, sends the corresponding cross-terms, and then folds them into a single
    23→        witness that is shorter by a factor of ρ using verifier challenges. Prior works typically restrict ρ = O(1)
    24→        to preserve succinct verification and maintain the optimal Õ(λ) proof size. We overcome this “constant
    25→        barrier”, which enables larger ρ and thereby reduces the proof size. To achieve this, we introduce the
    26→        following technical contributions.
    27→         (i) Committed folding. Instead of sending O(ρ) cross-terms in the clear, the prover commits to the
    28→             messages and later proves that the committed vector satisfies the verification relations. This enables
    29→             a larger shrinking factor, reducing the number of recursion rounds. While this strategy has been
    30→             successfully used in LaBRADOR (CRYPTO 2023), our main novelty is to do so while preserving
    31→             succinct verification.
    32→        (ii) Recursive commitments. We generalise the double-commitment technique from LaBRADOR into
    33→             a framework for recursive commitments, yielding further compression in commitment size. This
    34→             results in concrete improvements in communication within each recursion round.
    35→       (iii) Sumcheck-driven structured recursion. We extend the sumcheck framework from SALSAA (ePrint
    36→             2025/2124) to prove substantially more complex constraints arising in our construction, including
    37→             correctness of random projections, inner-product claims and well-formedness of recursive commit-
    38→             ments, and open for future extensions. Expressing these constraints as sumcheck relations takes
    39→             considerable effort, but the resulting protocols compose seamlessly with the structured recursion,
    40→             yielding linear-time proving and succinct verification.

    43→1     Introduction
    44→
    45→Succinct argument systems [Kil92, Mic94] allow a prover to convince a verifier of a statement’s
    46→validity using communication that is significantly shorter than the witness itself. This powerful
    47→compression capability underlies some of the most impactful applications in modern cryptography,
    48→including verifiable outsourced computation [KP16, Lee21, GSW23], verifiable fully-homomorphic
    49→encryption [GBK+ 24, LLZ+ 25, ABPS24, ZLW+ 25], aggregate signatures [BK20, AAB+ 24], and
    50→verifiable delay functions [LM23, OKC+ 25].
    51→   As quantum computers threaten the security foundations of group-based cryptography, the
    52→demand for post-quantum secure succinct arguments has become increasingly urgent. Lattice-based
    53→constructions offer a compelling direction as they rely on well-studied hardness assumptions believed

    62→1.1   Two Approaches in Lattice-Based Succinct Arguments
    63→

    97→    As analysed in [AL21, ACK21, BCS21], the lattice Bulletproofs protocol suffers from several
    98→efficiency drawbacks. Most notably, knowledge soundness critically depends on subtractive sets C, in
    99→which the difference of any two distinct c, c′ ∈ C must be invertible in Rq := R/qR with a short
   100→inverse. This limits C to polynomial size [AL21], implying an inverse-polynomial soundness error;
   101→amplifying it to 2−λ then increases communication by a factor Ω(λ/ log λ). As a consequence, lattice
   102→Bulletproofs have large communication, both in theory and in practice.
   103→    Subsequent work in this area has diverged into two main directions. One path focuses on
   104→constructing succinct arguments with concretely efficient proof sizes, while the other aims at
   105→asymptotically efficient arguments with polylogarithmic verification. We elaborate on both directions
   106→below.
   107→
   108→Path 1: Concrete efficiency and proof size. The lattice Bulletproofs protocol was substantially
   109→advanced by LaBRADOR [BS23], which introduces several key modifications. The first is that it
   110→departs from subtractive sets and instead adopts the random-projection technique of [BL17, LNS21,
   111→
   112→                                                         2
   113→GHL22, LNP22, BS23]. To demonstrate that w is (approximately) short, the verifier samples a
   114→random integer matrix J ∈ Zr×mφ with small entries (e.g. from {−1, 0, 1}). The prover computes the
   115→projected vector v := Jcf(w) mod q ∈ Zrq , where cf(w) ∈ Zmφ is the standard coefficient embedding
   116→of the ring vector w. The verifier checks that v has small norm, while the prover additionally proves
   117→v well-formed; this is a linear relation over Rq [LNP22]. Crucially, this removes the dependence on
   118→subtractive sets, so the protocol needs no repetitions.
   119→    The second change is that the prover no longer transmits all ρ2 cross-terms gi,j . Instead, the
   120→prover commits to the concatenated vector g := (gi,j )i∈[ρ],j∈[ρ] , and later proves that the committed
   121→value satisfies the linear constraints required for verification, namely those in Equations (2) and (3).
   122→                                                                                                    1/3
   123→This committed folding approach allows LaBRADOR to select large shrinking factors ρi = O(mi )
   124→at each round (where mi is the witness size entering round i), yielding only O(log log m) recursive
   125→rounds.

   127→the commitment tout to a short vector w is defined by

   137→    As a consequence of the aforementioned improvements, LaBRADOR achieves concretely efficient
   138→proof sizes, around 60 KB even for large statements (with witness consisting of ≈ 220 ring elements).
   139→Its main drawback is the linear verification time. Indeed, even processing the random projection
   140→matrix J already takes time Ω(m). The subsequent work Greyhound [NS24] addresses this limitation

   142→by invoking LaBRADOR as a subroutine, where each invocation is on a witness of length O( m).
   143→As a result, Greyhound inherits the appealing proof sizes of LaBRADOR while achieving sublinear
   144→verification time.
   145→
   146→Path 2: Succinct verification. Motivated by the growing interest in constructing SNARKs from
   147→Polynomial Interactive Oracle Proofs, there has been much research on lattice-based polynomial
   148→commitment schemes (PCS). In this direction, [CLM23] first adapted lattice Bulletproofs to sup-
   149→port succinct verification by endowing the commitment matrix F with a tensor structure. This
   150→modification leads to a commitment that is binding under a new assumption, called the Vanishing
   151→SIS (vSIS) assumption. Independently, [BCS23] achieved fast verification for lattice Bulletproofs
   152→via additional preprocessing. However, both constructions inherit all the underlying limitations of
   153→lattice Bulletproofs itself [BCS23, CLM23].
   154→    A different line of work [AFLN24, CMNW24, KLNO24b] develops succinct arguments with
   155→polylogarithmic verification time by applying FRI-style [BBHR18] split-and-fold techniques. These
   156→protocols still require repetitions due to the use of subtractive sets, but they yield more efficient
   157→proofs than Bulletproofs, since they involve only O(ρ) cross-terms, compared to O(ρ2 ) required in
   158→lattice Bulletproofs.
   159→    The reliance on subtractive sets was later removed by [KLNO25], which adapts the random
   160→projection strategy to demonstrate shortness. Directly using LaBRADOR-style random projections
   161→is infeasible here due to the goal of achieving fast verification. To address this, the authors employ
   162→4

   167→ tensor-structured projection matrices of the form J := I ⊗ J′ , where I is the identity matrix and J′
   168→ is a short random challenge matrix with O(λ) rows and columns. Consequently, the verifier only
   169→ needs to operate on J′ , and with a carefully designed split-and-fold protocol, succinct verification
   170→ becomes possible. As a result, [KLNO25] achieves asymptotically optimal Õ(λ)-sized proofs, notable
   171→ since all prior post-quantum argument systems, including hash-based ones [ACFY24, ACFY25], only
   172→ obtained Õ(λ2 ) proofs. Despite this asymptotic, concrete efficiency remains limited as proof sizes
   173→ scale to a few megabytes (with witness of ≈ 223 ring elements).
   174→     While random projections can demonstrate that the committed witness is approximately short,
   175→ they cannot on their own certify exact norm bounds. To address this, [KLNO24b, KLNO25] showed
   176→ how to prove inner products between committed vectors modulo q. Provided that w is sufficiently
   177→ short, no modular wrap-around occurs, enabling the prover to establish inner products over Z.
   178→ Building on this, SALSAA [KLOT25] adds sumcheck protocols [LFKN92] to verify exact ℓ2 norm
   179→ and, unlike [KLNO24b, KLNO25], enjoys a linear prover.
   180→
   181→ Bridging the gap. LaBRADOR [BS23] achieves remarkably small proof sizes, owing to its use of
   182→ super-constant folding, double-layer commitments, and random projections to establish shortness.
   183→ However, its principal limitation is the linear verification time, and even with the improvement offered
   184→ by Greyhound [NS24], the verifier remains more than two orders of magnitude slower than hash-
   185→ based alternatives. Conversely, argument systems that support succinct verification are still far from
   186→ concretely competitive. Although recent work incorporates structured Johnson–Lindenstrauss pro-
   187→ jections into this setting, it remains unclear whether the other techniques underpinning LaBRADOR
   188→ adapt to the succinct-verifier regime while preserving practical efficiency. This raises a question: can
   189→ one design an argument system that admits polylogarithmic verification while achieving proof sizes
   190→ and concrete performance similar to LaBRADOR and Greyhound?

   192→ 1.2   Our contributions
   193→ We present RoKoko – a lattice-based argument system that unifies the strengths of both paths,
   194→ achieving nearly the best of both worlds:
   195→  (i) Succinct verification: polylogarithmic verifier runtime, matching the best Path 2 constructions;
   196→ (ii) Optimal prover: linear-time proving, matching the lower bound;
   197→(iii) Compact proofs: proof sizes substantially improving on prior succinct-verification work,
   198→      narrowing the gap to the LaBRADOR line;
   199→(iv) Rich expressiveness: native support for structured relations, polynomial commitments, and
   200→      natural arithmetisations such as R1CS.
   201→
   202→Technical innovations. Our main technical contribution is to adapt committed superconstant folding
   203→– a technique pioneered by LaBRADOR [BS23] – to the vSIS-based framework that supports succinct
   204→verification. In the LaBRADOR line, the verifier performs work proportional to the witness size, so
   205→the folding structure can remain opaque to the verifier. In the succinct-verification regime, however,
   206→the verifier must efficiently check consistency of the folded instances without implicitly reading
   207→the full witness, which requires the folding to respect the algebraic structure of the underlying
   208→commitment scheme. Concretely:
   209→
   210→  – Structured superconstant folding. We show how to fold by a superconstant factor within
   211→    the vSIS-based framework while preserving the structure needed for succinct verification. We
   212→
   213→                                                    4
   214→   extend the random projection technique of [KLNO25] to handle this more aggressive folding,
   215→   yielding a significant reduction in the number of rounds.
   216→ – Recursive commitments. Inspired by [BS23, BC25b], we introduce a recursive commitment
   217→   structure where the outermost commitment – the only one the verifier inspects explicitly – has
   218→   low rank independent of the witness size. This compresses the commitment component of the
   219→   proof, which dominates proof size in prior constructions from Path 2 (the succinct-verification
   220→   regime); such recursive commitments were not previously available in the structured setting.
   221→ – Sumcheck-based linear-time proving. Building on [KLOT25], we integrate sumcheck pro-
   222→   tocols to efficiently prove well-formedness of the recursive commitments and correctness of
   223→   projections. The sumcheck machinery suits the recursive commitment structure well: the prover
   224→   shows each commitment layer is correctly formed without materialising intermediate witnesses,
   225→   achieving linear-time proving without sacrificing succinctness.
   226→ – Implementation and evaluation. We implement RoKoko and conduct experiments, demon-
   227→   strating that our improvements translate into practice: competitive proof sizes alongside faster
   228→   verification compared to prior lattice-based constructions. We also give a complete asymptotic
   229→   analysis and instantiate RoKoko as a SNARK for structured relations and as a PCS.
   230→ – Incomplete-NTT library. As a side contribution, we provide the most efficient open-source
   231→   standalone implementation, to date, of the “almost-splitting” cyclotomic ring arithmetic under-
   232→   pinning our construction.

   245→1.3   Related work
   246→Group- and hash-based succinct arguments. Group-based arguments such as Groth16 [Gro16],
   247→PLONK [GWC19] and others [PHGR13, GGPR13, WTs+ 18, MBKM19, XZZ+ 19, BFS20, CHM+ 20]
   248→achieve strong efficiency but rely on assumptions like discrete logarithm, and are thus insecure against
   249→quantum adversaries. Hash-based argument systems, such as FRI [BBHR18], Ligero [AHIV23],
   250→Aurora [BCR+ 19], Brakedown [GLS+ 23], and WHIR [ACFY25], offer post-quantum security and
   251→transparent setup. In particular, WHIR achieves extremely efficient verification time. However,
   252→hash-based schemes generally fall short in prover efficiency and proof size compared to lattice-based
   253→alternatives.
   254→
   255→Non-succinct lattice-based arguments. Non-succinct lattice-based arguments [LNSW13,BLS19,
   256→ALS20, LNP22] have proof size and verification time linear in the witness size, offering high concrete
   257→efficiency for small relations. These protocols typically focus on the zero-knowledge property rather
   258→than succinctness.

   289→Folding schemes. Orthogonal to “monolithic” SNARKs discussed above (following the terminology
   290→of [BCFW25]), a line of works [BC25a, FKNP24, BMNW25a, BMNW25b, NS25, BC25b, BCFW25]
   291→have proposed post-quantum folding schemes (or accumulation schemes) that can be used as building
   292→blocks of incrementally-verifiable computation protocols [Val08] and proof-carrying data [CT10].
   293→Folding schemes also compile generically into SNARKs [KST21].
   294→    Among recent quantum-safe folding schemes, several rely on lattice assumptions [BC25a,FKNP24,
   295→NS25, BC25b]. As in our setting, the prover must prove (exact) shortness of the witness. To achieve
   296→this, [BC25a, NS25, BC25b] apply the sumcheck protocol to prove the ℓ∞ norm bound. However, as
   297→the communication complexity scales linearly with the range, this approach becomes impractical for
   298→large ℓ∞ bounds and is not well-suited to prove ℓ2 norms.

   307→2.1    Background
   308→
   309→Let R ∼= Z[x]/⟨Φf (x)⟩ be a cyclotomic ring and Rq = R/qR for a prime q. Works on lattice-based
   310→succinct arguments typically consider a sufficiently expressive relation defined over Rq where the
   311→witness is a matrix W ∈ Rmw ×r of norm at most some βw . The goal is always to progressively shrink
   312→the witness dimension mw until it is small enough to be sent in the clear. Two intertwined challenges
   313→must be addressed:
   314→
   315→                                                           6
   316→ 1. Witness folding. The basic idea of shrinking the witness W is to fold W into Wc by some verifier
   317→    challenge c, and then repack the witness into a smaller matrix W′ .5 The shrink factor ρ, i.e. the
   318→    rate at which the witness dimension is reduced, is roughly the dimension of c. What distinguishes
   319→    the semi-6 and fully-succinct settings is that, in the latter, the verifier must be able to check
   320→    (among other constraints due to norm control) the correctness of folding in polylogarithmic time.
   321→ 2. Norm control. While shrinking the witness dimension, we must keep the witness norm at around
   322→    the same level, say βw . This splits into two sub-challenges. Completeness, i.e. the honest prover
   323→    can produce a valid witness for the reduced instance. Folding W into Wc may increase the norm;
   324→    the standard fix is to decompose the folded witness Wc, giving a net dimension reduction when
   325→    the shrinking by c outweighs the decomposition expansion. We take this for granted and omit it
   326→    henceforth. Extraction, i.e. the extractor can recover a valid witness for the original instance. As
   327→    usual for lattice-based arguments, it recovers not an exact witness but a “relaxed” one (W, s)
   328→    with a short “slack” vector s, relaxed in two ways: it only guarantees ∥W · diag(s)∥ ≤ βw′ (not
   329→    necessarily ∥W∥ ≤ βw′ ), and the bound may be looser than the original (βw′ > βw ). This too can
   330→    accumulate throughout composition.
   331→
   332→    Numerous prior works address subsets of these challenges with different trade-offs; we highlight
   333→the most relevant.
   334→    Regarding witness folding, [CLM23] proposes using vSIS commitments so that the witness
   335→can be folded in a structured way that enables a polylogarithmic verifier. This method is refined
   336→in [KLNO24b] and adopted in [KLNO25, KLOT25]. In [KLNO24b, KLNO25, KLOT25], folding the
   337→witness by a factor ρ requires communication proportional to ρ per round, forcing them to settle on
   338→                                                                       1/3
   339→ρ = O(1). LaBRADOR [BS23] achieves a shrink factor of ρ = O(mw ) with essentially constant
   340→communication per round by having the prover commit to the cross-terms instead of sending them
   341→in the clear. However, this method of folding breaks whatever structure the original relation might
   342→have, causing the verifier to run in time proportional to mw .
   343→    Regarding norm control, LaBRADOR [BS23] uses random projections to eliminate the slack
   344→vector s. However, the projection matrices are unstructured, which again causes the verifier to run
   345→in time proportional to mw . RoK and Roll [KLNO25] adapted this technique to the fully-succinct
   346→setting by introducing structured projections. Then, to force the extracted witness norm βw′ to be
   347→the same as the original norm βw , SALSAA [KLOT25] adapted the sumcheck protocol to prove
   348→the exact norm of the witness with a prover that runs in strict- (as opposed to quasi-)linear time,
   349→improving upon the norm-check RoK of [KLNO24b].
   350→    The main contribution of this work is to extend the committed folding technique (explored in the
   351→literature in [BS23] or [BC25b] in the context of folding schemes) to the fully-succinct setting while
   352→retaining all techniques introduced in [KLNO24b, KLNO25, KLOT25] for structure-preserving norm
   353→control. This will ultimately allow us to achieve a more favourable trade-off than the state-of-the-art.
   354→Our construction of succinct arguments is built by composing a small number of reductions of
   355→knowledge (RoKs) which map between two relations – a committed-linear relation ΞCOM          lin   and a

   359→                                                                                     lin
   360→easier to explain. In other words, we will build a succinct argument system for ΞCOM .

   371→At a high level, in ΞCOM   the prover’s witness wit mainly consists of a matrix W ∈ Rmw ×r satisfying
   372→linear constraints Fi W = Hi Yi mod q for all i, where we can think of Y1 as a commitment of W.
   373→The matrices Yi on the right-hand side are auxiliary witness components which are themselves
   374→(recursively) committed. The main witness component W additionally satisfies a linear constraint
   375→of the form ℓT Wr = t mod q. Intuitively, think of W as a very tall matrix, e.g. of height mw = 220 ,
   376→while Yi is relatively much smaller. Furthermore, the witness components W, Yi must be low-norm.
   377→The functional-sumcheck relation ΞCOM  sum has the same kind of committed low-norm witness W (with
   378→
   379→a single auxiliary block Y), but replaces the constraints by sumcheck claims. Linear constraints,
   380→commitment verification and exact norm claims are all expressed in that form, which is what allows
   381→the folding RoK below to output a single relation.
   382→    Our goal is to iteratively reduce a large ΞCOM lin   instance into progressively smaller ones while
   383→maintaining full-succinctness – the verifier’s runtime (and hence the proof size) must be at most
   384→poly(λ, log |wit|) = poly(λ, log mw r). In a nutshell, each round of our protocol consists of three (fami-
   385→lies of) RoKs: Π proj for random projection, Π fold-split for folding, splitting and encoding constraints
   386→into functional-sumcheck claims, and Π lin for turning sumcheck claims back into linear constraints.
   387→Symbolically, each round is a sequential composition of

   398→Committed random projection Π proj : ΞCOM lin        lin . This RoK addresses norm control : inspired
   399→                                                → ΞCOM
   400→                                          lin
   401→by [KLNO25], it is a self-reduction on ΞCOM that eliminates the extraction-side norm slack. The key
   402→idea relies on a variant of the Johnson–Lindenstrauss (JL) lemma: a random matrix J ∈ Znrp ×mrp
   403→with small i.i.d. entries maps x ∈ Zmrp so that the ratio ∥Jx∥/∥x∥ lies in a bounded interval [αrp , βrp ]
   404→with high probability – the norm is scaled up but tightly controlled. Naively, one would apply such
   405→a J to the entire witness of dimension mw , but the verifier would then need to read all nrp × mw
   406→entries of J, breaking succinctness. Instead, the prover applies a structured variant (I ⊗ J) where
   407→J ∈ Znrp ×mrp with mrp ≪ mw : the verifier only samples the small matrix J, and the block-diagonal
   408→structure preserves the JL guarantee on each block. The JL guarantee ensures that a short projection
   409→image implies the original witness is short, i.e. without slack.
   410→    We instantiate this idea in three variants, which differ in the granularity at which they project
   411→and hence in their cost. The coarse variant (Π proj-c ) projects ring elements directly and is used while
   412→mw ≥ mrp : it is the cheapest, since computing (I ⊗ J)W only requires additions and subtractions
   413→of ring elements. The fine variant (Π proj-f ) projects their coefficients via the trace-dual embedding
   414→(used once mw drops below mrp , relaxing the dimension requirement to mrp | φmw ), and then batches
   415→the projection and lifts to constraints over Rq : the consistency between W and its projection is
   416→a statement over Zq , which is batched with random Zq challenges into nbat constraints over Rq ,
   417→so that the round communicates nbat additional ring elements. The lift-and-batch variant (Π proj-fl )
   418→generalises the fine variant by exploiting the chain of intermediate subfields Fqa of Rq to gradually
   419→lift the projection image instead of immediately lifting to Rq , which removes the overhead of nbat
   420→ring elements and yields further asymptotic improvements. All variants share the same (I ⊗ J)
   421→structure, and – crucially – the prover commits to the (gadget-decomposed) projection image rather
   422→than sending it in the clear.

   485→2.3     Putting Everything Together
   486→Composition and asymptotics. The full composition proceeds in two phases: first, a coarse chain
   487→(Π lin ◦ Π fold-split ◦ Π proj-c ) is iterated while mw ≥ mrp ; once mw drops below mrp , the fine chain
   488→(Π lin ◦ Π fold-split ◦ Π proj-f ) takes over for the remaining rounds8 . With ρ = λ, the full composition
   489→yields a prover runtime of O(mw ) Rq operations (linear-time), verifier runtime of O(λ3 log mw / log λ),
   490→and proof size of O(λ log3 mw / log2 λ) bits. Compared to SALSAA [KLOT25], which is effectively
   491→limited to ρ = O(1) because their verifier complexity grows linearly in ρ, we reduce proof size by a
   492→factor of log λ while matching prover complexity, at the cost of a theoretically larger verifier runtime.
   493→
   494→Applications. A succinct argument for ΞCOM lin immediately yields a polynomial commitment scheme
   495→(PCS): the polynomial evaluation claim p(v) = t is a linear constraint ℓT Wr = t mod q on the
   496→committed coefficient matrix W, which is already a ΞCOMlin  instance (as noted above). If we instead
   497→                            sum
   498→take the more expressive ΞCOM as the starting relation, we obtain a SNARK for R1CS: the R1CS
   499→                                                                            sum , though the verifier
   500→constraints are naturally expressible as sumcheck constraints captured by ΞCOM
   501→must additionally evaluate multilinear extensions on public R1CS matrices.
   502→
   503→Implementation. We implement RoKoko in Rust, targeting almost-splitting power-of-two cyclotomic
   504→rings (Rq ∼= (Fq2 )φ/2 , with φ = 128, q ≈ 250 ). Almost-splitting rings (residue degree e = 2) provide
   505→NTT slots Fq2 large enough for sufficient per-round soundness while admitting efficient incomplete-
   506→NTT arithmetic, with ring multiplication reducing to φ/2 independent quadratic products. We
   507→release the ring arithmetic layer as an independent open-source library. On top of it, we build a
   508→sumcheck module with a generic interface for expressing sumcheck relations, and instantiate it to
   509→deliver a PCS. For a PCS over 226 coefficients in Zq (single-threaded, λ = 128, κ = 2−100 ), our
   510→implementation produces a 112 KB proof in 3.38 s (commit + prove), with verification in 8.12 ms.
   511→Compared to SALSAA [KLOT25], this is a ∼10× reduction in proof size and ∼8× faster proving,
   512→while verification is ∼1.8× slower; compared to Greyhound [NS24], we offer ∼52× faster verification
   513→(reaching ∼90× on the largest instance run on the server-grade machine; see Table 8).
