    13→    13→         Abstract. This work introduces HyperWolf, a Hypercube-Wise Opti-
    14→    14→         mized polynomial commitment scheme based on Lattices over ring Fields.
    15→    15→         The scheme achieves succinctness with O(log N ) proof size and verifier
    16→    16→         time, along with linear prover cost. It supports both univariate and mul-
    17→    17→         tilinear polynomials under a unified framework. Inspired by the two-
    18→    18→         dimensional tensor structure employed in [GLS+ 21] to achieve sublinear
    19→    19→         efficiency, we generalize the idea to a k-dimensional tensor (hypercube)
    20→    20→         structure and design a k-round recursive proof protocol, where each
    21→    21→         round performs a dimensionality reduction, which results in an over-
    22→    22→         all efficiency of O(kN 1/k ). By setting k = log N , our scheme achieves
    23→    23→         near-optimal asymptotic performance.
    24→    24→         HyperWolf is fully transparent and relies only on the standard lattice
    25→    25→         assumption over rings. In terms of concrete efficiency, for polynomials
    26→    26→         with N = 225 coefficients, our scheme yields proof sizes that are 8×
    27→    27→         smaller than the lattice-based scheme of [CMNW24] (Crypto24), and
    28→    28→         over 200× smaller than SLAP [AFLN24] (Eurocrypt24). Compared to
    29→    29→         Greyhound [NS24] (Crypto24), our proof size is of similar magnitude,
    30→    30→         while achieving significantly lower verifier time.

    36→    36→1      Introduction
    37→    37→
    38→    38→A Polynomial Commitment Scheme (PCS) is a cryptographic primitive that en-
    39→    39→ables a prover to commit to a polynomial f —which may be univariate [KZG10]
    40→    40→or multilinear [GLS+ 21], [ZCF24]—by publishing a compact commitment. Later,
    41→    41→for given public inputs x (or ⃗x in the multilinear case) and y, the prover can
    42→    42→generate a succinct proof π attesting that y = f (x) or f (⃗x). For practical
    43→    43→efficiency, the proof size must be succinct and the verification cost must be
    44→    44→efficient (i.e. less than sublinear in the input size). PCS are widely used in
    45→    45→cryptographic protocols, enabling applications such as verifiable secret sharing
    46→    46→⋆⋆
    47→    47→     In the future, this scheme will be implemented by Mr. Kurt Pan, and he will be
    48→    48→     included in the author list at that time.
    49→    49→[BDK13], secure multi-party computation [BHV+ 23], and authentication mecha-
    50→    50→nisms. Their most prominent application lies in constructing zero-knowledge suc-
    51→    51→cinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (zkSNARKs) [BFS20], [CHM+ 20],
    52→    52→[CBBZ23], [BSBHR18b], where they serve as a fundamental primitive.
    53→    53→    Significant research efforts have been made on PCS, however, the majority of
    54→    54→existing schemes depend on discrete logarithm problem assumptions [BBB+ 18],
    55→    55→[KZG10], [Lee21], which are vulnerable to quantum computing attacks. Although
    56→    56→post-quantum secure alternatives have been developed - notably those employing
    57→    57→error-correcting codes [BSBHR18a], [GLS+ 21] - these solutions typically com-
    58→    58→promise on homomorphic properties, which are essential for supporting efficient
    59→    59→proof composition in advanced cryptographic protocols.
    60→    60→    Lattice-based cryptography has emerged as a particularly promising ap-
    61→    61→proach for constructing quantum-resistant primitives, owing to its computa-
    62→    62→tional efficiency, natural homomorphic capabilities, and proven security against
    63→    63→quantum attacks. Despite these advantages, the development of lattice-based
    64→    64→polynomial commitment schemes has progressed slowly, mainly due to funda-
    65→    65→mental difficulties in achieving exact extractability guarantees. While recent
    66→    66→work has begun to address this gap, existing solutions inevitably introduce
    67→    67→substantial compromises. Some proposals depend on non-standard lattice as-
    68→    68→sumptions [WW23a], [AFLN24], whose long-term security against quantum ad-
    69→    69→versaries remains uncertain. Others require trusted setup procedures [HSS24],
    70→    70→undermining their practical applicability. Although certain constructions [NS24]
    71→    71→and [CMNW24] maintain transparent setup properties, they are respectively
    72→    72→constrained by their restriction to univariate polynomials and the computational
    73→    73→overhead associated with integer field operations. These limitations collectively
    74→    74→underscore the importance of continued research to advance lattice-based poly-
    75→    75→nomial commitment schemes.

    79→    79→1.1   Related Works
    80→    80→
    81→    81→
    82→    82→According to the underlying construction frameworks, the existing lattice-based
    83→    83→polynomial commitment schemes can be broadly classified into two categories:
    84→    84→    Constructions Based on Inner-Product Relations. These schemes
    85→    85→interpret the polynomial evaluation process f (x) = y as an inner-product rela-
    86→    86→tion ⟨f⃗, ⃗x⟩, where ⃗x = (1, x, . . . , xN −1 ) in the univariate case [ACL+ 22, dCP23,
    87→    87→WW23a, CLM23]. They leverage the Bulletproofs protocol [BBB+ 18] to gener-
    88→    88→ate succinct proofs. However, the verification cost is linear to the polynomial
    89→    89→degree N because the evaluation point cannot be chosen in advance to ensure
    90→    90→soundness. Recent works [BCS23, FLV23] address this issue by integrating the
    91→    91→Dory-based delegation protocol [Lee21] or introduced more structured commit-
    92→    92→ment scheme for fast verification, though they still necessitate a preprocessing
    93→    93→phase. Furthermore, most constructions do not guarantee extractability. Only a
    94→    94→few schemes [FLV23, ACL+ 22, CLM23] achieve this property, but they depend
    95→    95→
    96→    96→                                           2
    97→    97→on non-standard lattice assumptions, such as the k-M-SIS assumption, which is
    98→    98→susceptible to being broken. 1
    99→    99→    Constructions Based on Recursive Approaches. Another approach to
   100→   100→constructing lattice-based polynomial evaluation schemes utilizes the recursive
   101→   101→computational framework. [FMN24] was the first to introduce the split-and-fold
   102→   102→technique from [BSBHR18a] into the lattice setting, enabling fast verification.
   103→   103→This advancement, however, came at the cost of relying on the new PowerBASIS
   104→   104→assumption, a variant of the BASIS assumption proposed in [WW23b], and re-
   105→   105→quired a large common reference string (CRS) of size quadratic in the input size.
   106→   106→A subsequent work, SLAP [AFLN24], addressed these limitations by reducing
   107→   107→the size of the CRS and mitigating the reliance on non-standard assumptions.
   108→   108→While this refinement improved efficiency, it introduced a quasi-linear prover
   109→   109→cost and still required a trusted setup.
   110→   110→    Most recently, Nguyen et al. [NS24] introduced Greyhound, a transparent
   111→   111→univariate polynomial commitment scheme building upon the Labrador proto-
   112→   112→col [BS23]. Their construction employs recursive techniques to achieve compact
   113→   113→proof sizes while incorporating bi-variate sum-check technique to enable sub-
   114→   114→linear verification time under standard lattice assumptions. Nevertheless, this
   115→   115→approach maintains two key limitations: it only supports univariate polynomi-
   116→   116→als and specifically operates under infinity norm bounds. Concurrently, Cini et
   117→   117→al. [CMNW24] presented an SIS-based polynomial commitment scheme with a
   118→   118→transparent setup that accommodates both univariate and multilinear polynomi-
   119→   119→als. While this construction achieves broader functionality, it incurs substantial
   120→   120→computational overhead due to its reliance on integer field arithmetic.
   121→   121→    Building on the discussion above, this work explores the following research
   122→   122→question:
   123→   123→    Can we design a general PCS for univariate and multilinear polynomials,
   124→   124→based on standard lattice assumptions, that supports a transparent setup, nearly
   125→   125→optimal (logarithmic level) proof size and verification time, and linear prover
   126→   126→time?
   127→   127→
   128→   128→
   129→   129→1.2    Our Contributions
   130→   130→
   131→   131→We answer the above question in the affirmative by introducing HyperWolf, a
   132→   132→novel and efficient lattice-based proof system that achieves all the desired prop-
   133→   133→erties. We present three main contributions, each of independent interest.
   134→   134→    Unified Framework. First, we identify and formalize a common algebraic
   135→   135→structure shared by univariate and multilinear polynomial evaluations, and pro-
   136→   136→pose a unified framework that accommodates both cases. This framework is
   137→   137→flexibly parameterized, allowing it to specialize to either setting through the
   138→   138→adjustment of auxiliary parameters.
   139→   139→    Recursive-Friendly Proof System. Our second contribution is the design
   140→   140→of a proof system works in recursive manner for the unified framework. This

   147→   147→ployed in prior works such as [NS24], [GLS+ 21], and [EG25] to achieve sublinear
   148→   148→proof sizes and verifier time. The core idea behind these schemes is to reinterpret
   149→   149→the witness—specifically, the coefficient vector f⃗ in a PCS—as a vector of length
   150→   150→N , and express the proof relation as an inner product ⟨f⃗, ⃗x⟩ = y. By reshaping
   151→   151→f⃗ into an n × n matrix with n2 = N , the original relation can be decomposed
   152→   152→to two sub-relations: first, an inner product between a length-n vector and an
   153→   153→n × n matrix; second, an inner product between two vectors of length n, which
   154→   154→can then be proved using a bivariate sumcheck protocol. Each sub-proof yields
   155→   155→an O(n)-size proof and supports O(n)-time verification, resulting in an overall
   156→   156→sublinear proof system that maintains completeness and soundness.
   157→   157→    In our proposed scheme, we generalize and extend this idea by reshaping
   158→   158→a length-N witness into a k-dimensional hypercube structure, and designing
   159→   159→a k-round recursive proof system where each round performs a reduction of
   160→   160→knowledge from a high-dimensional relation to one with dimension decreased
   161→   161→by one. Crucially, both the proof size and verification time in each round are
   162→   162→bounded by O(N 1/k ). After completing all k rounds, the total cost accumulates
   163→   163→to O(k · N 1/k ). Notably, by optimizing the parameter choice to k = log N , both
   164→   164→the proof size and verifier time can be reduced to O(log N ), achieving near-
   165→   165→optimal asymptotic efficiency. Furthermore, our scheme maintains linear prover

   328→   328→2.3   Interactive Proofs and Polynomial Commitment Schemes
   329→   329→Let R ⊆ {0, 1}∗ × {0, 1}∗ × {0, 1}∗ be a ternary relation. For (pp, x, w) ∈ R, we
   330→   330→denote that pp as the public parameters, x as a statement and w as a witness
   331→   331→for x. For fixed pp and x, we define the set R(pp, x) := {w : (pp, x, w) ∈ R}.
   332→   332→Definition 3. (Interactive Proof System) An interactive proof system Π =
   333→   333→(S, P, V) for the relation R is an interactive protocol that consists of the prob-
   334→   334→abilistic polynomial-time (PPT) setup algorithm S and a protocol between an
   335→   335→interactive PPT prover P and an interactive verifier V. This system enables
   336→   336→the prover to convince the verifier of the existence of a witness w for a public
   337→   337→statement x. Both P and V receive the statement x and the public parameters
   338→   338→pp generated by the setup algorithm S as public input. Additionally, P has pri-
   339→   339→vate input in the form of a witness w ∈ R(pp, x). At the end of the protocol,
   340→   340→V either accepts or rejects the prover’s claim of knowing a witness w such that
   341→   341→(pp, x, w) ∈ R. Consequently, we classify the corresponding transcript (i.e., the
   342→   342→collection of all messages exchanged during the protocol execution) as either ac-
   343→   343→cepting or rejecting.

   429→   429→Coordinate-Wise Special Soundness is an extension of the concept of special
   430→   430→soundness, which can lead to knowledge soundness for a (2µ + 1)-round interac-
   431→   431→tive proof system, as proposed in [FMN24].
   432→   432→    First, consider two vectors ⃗x := (x1 , x2 , · · · , xℓ ), ⃗y := (y1 , y2 , · · · , yℓ ) ∈ S ℓ .
   433→   433→We define a relation ≡i for i ∈ [ℓ] as follows:
   434→   434→
   435→   435→                        ⃗x ≡i ⃗y ⇔ xi ̸= yi ∧ ∀j ∈ [ℓ] \ {i}, xj = yj .
   436→   436→
   437→   437→This means that the vectors ⃗x and ⃗y are identical in all coordinates except for
   438→   438→the i-th one. Next, we define the set:
   439→   439→                                                                                         
   440→   440→                                                   ∃e ∈ [K], ∀i ∈ [ℓ],                   
   441→   441→SS(S, ℓ, k) := {⃗x1 , ⃗x2 , . . . , ⃗xK } ⊆ (S ℓ )K ∃J = {j1 , . . . , jk−1 } ⊆ [K] \ {e}, ,
   442→   442→                                                    ∀j ∈ J, ⃗xe ≡i ⃗xj
   443→   443→                                                                                         
   444→   444→
   445→   445→where K := ℓ(k − 1) + 1. The interpretation of the set SS(S, ℓ, k) is that there
   446→   446→exists a “central” vector ⃗xe such that for each coordinate of ⃗xe , there are k − 1
   447→   447→other vectors that differ from it only at that coordinate. In essence, for every
   448→   448→coordinate, there are k vectors that differ from each other only in that coordinate
   449→   449→with ⃗xe always being one of them.
   450→   450→
   451→   451→Definition 10. (CWSS for Multi-Round Protocols) Let Π = (S, P, V) be
   452→   452→public-coin (2µ + 1)-round interactive proof system for relation R, where in each
   453→   453→round the challenge space of V is defined as C = S ℓ . A tree of transcripts is a
   454→   454→set of K = (ℓ(k − 1) + 1)µ arranged in the tree structure: The nodes in the tree
   455→   455→correspond to the prover’s messages and the edges correspond to the verifier’s
   456→   456→challenges. Each node at depth i has exactly ℓ(k − 1) + 1 children corresponding
   457→   457→to ℓ(k − 1) + 1 distinct challenges which, as a set of vectors, lie in SS(S, ℓ, k).
   458→   458→Every transcript corresponds to exactly one path from the root to a leaf node.
   459→   459→We say that Π is ℓ-coordinate-wise k-special sound if there is a polynomial time
   460→   460→algorithm that, given a statement x and the tree of transcripts, outputs a witness
   461→   461→w ∈ R(i, x).

   464→   464→ness property.

   535→   535→vector, we can utilize the Johnson-Lindenstrauss (JL) Lemma. The key intuition
   536→   536→behind the JL Lemma is that a random linear projection of the original vector

   555→   555→
