    11→ Abstract. Folding is a powerful technique for constructing efficient succinct proof systems, especially
    12→ for computations that are expressed in a streaming fashion. In this work, we present Cyclo, a new lattice-
    13→ based folding protocol that improves upon LatticeFold+ [Boneh and Chen ’25] in multiple dimensions
    14→ and which incorporates, among others, the pay-per-bit techniques from Neo when folding constraints
    15→ expressed over a field Fq [Nguyen and Setty ’25]. Cyclo proposes a new framework for building lattice-
    16→ based folding schemes that eliminates the need for norm checks on the accumulator by adopting an
    17→ amortized norm-refreshing design, ensuring that the witness norm grows additively per round within a
    18→ (generously) bounded number of folds. This design simplifies the protocol and reduces prover overhead.
    19→ In particular, Cyclo only performs range checks on the input non-accumulated witness, and when ap-
    20→ plied to fold constraints over Fq , it does not decompose any witnesses into low-norm chunks within the
    21→ folding protocol itself. Cyclo, supporting a complete family of cyclotomic rings, combines two simple
    22→ building blocks: an extension commitment that reduces the norm of the witness by decomposing it and
    23→ recommitting, and an ℓ∞ range test via a sum-check protocol. We demonstrate, by proving communi-
    24→ cation and runtime estimates that the construction results in an efficient and proof-size-friendly folding
    25→ scheme. We also establish an algebraic connection between Rq and Fq using the polynomial evaluation
    26→ map, enabling efficient reduction from R1CS/CCS over Fq to a linear relation over Rq , providing a new
    27→ and simpler formulation of the techniques in [Nguyen and Setty ’25]. In practical settings, Cyclo achieves
    28→ succinct proof sizes on the order of 30 KB, improving by an order of magnitude over LatticeFold+. Our
    29→ efficiency benchmarks indicate that our protocol also outperforms LatticeFold+ in practice.

    69→1 Introduction
    70→
    71→In recent years, succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (SNARKs) have become central
    72→to applications such as blockchains [XZC+ 22,GKO24,LM25,LZW+ 25], privacy-preserving authen-
    73→tication [EHRS24], and verifiable computation [ACGS24,CCC+ 25]. Despite their power, SNARKs
    74→suffer from a major memory bottleneck that limits their applicability[LZW+ 24,ZSCZ25]: the prover
    75→must load the entire computation into memory. This is infeasible for large computations and, more
    76→broadly, fails to capture how computations are often carried out in practice, namely in a streaming
    77→manner.
    78→ This setting, motivated in particular by practical applications such as blockchains and verifi-
    79→able outsourced computation (e.g., cloud/edge ML inference and long-running services), has led
    80→to the framework of incrementally verifiable computation (IVC) [Val08,NPR19] and its generaliza-
    81→tion, proof-carrying data [CT10,BCL+ 21,BDFG21,CCG+ 23]. Early IVC protocols were built from
    82→SNARKs and can be viewed as producing, at each step, a proof for the computation performed
    83→by the verifier in the previous step [Val08,BCCT12,BCTV14]. More modern approaches use folding
    84→schemes [BGH19,BDFG21,BCL+ 21,BC25a,BC25b], which let the prover aggregate many compu-
    85→tation steps into a single instance of an accumulator relation. This is substantially simpler than
    86→proving the entire computation at each step and yields improved efficiency.
    87→ Folding can be understood via reductions of knowledge [ACK21,KP22]. A reduction of knowledge
    88→from a relation Ξ0 to a relation Ξ1 is a protocol Π between a prover and a verifier in which the
    89→ x
    90→verifier, given an instance 0 for Ξ0 , interacts with the prover and ultimately outputs an instance
    91→x 1 for Ξ1 . The key property is that if the prover can provide a witness 1 for 1 , then one can w x
    92→ w x
    93→extract a witness 0 for 0 from the prover. A folding scheme realizes a reduction of knowledge
    94→from the product relation Ξacc × Ξ0 to the accumulation relation Ξacc : it folds a pair of instances
    95→ x x x
    96→( acc , 0 ) into a single new instance ′acc of Ξacc . Repeating this reduction lets the prover aggregate
    97→many computation steps (expressed by Ξ0 ) into a single instance of the accumulator relation Ξacc .
    98→This intuition also extends naturally to multiple-input relations, allowing folding with inputs from
    99→Ξacc × (Ξ0 × · · · × Ξd−1 ).
   100→ The best-established folding schemes rely on linearly homomorphic commitments, such as Ped-
   101→ersen commitments [Ped92], to commit to the witness of the input relation. Folding then amounts
   102→ Cyclo: Lightweight Lattice-based Folding via Partial Range Checks 3
   103→
   104→to combining the per-step commitments into a single commitment to the folded instance. Unfortu-
   105→nately, Pedersen relies on the discrete logarithm assumption and is therefore vulnerable to quantum
   106→attacks [Sho94]. A compelling alternative is the Ajtai commitment [Ajt96], which is based on the
   107→hardness of lattice problems and is widely viewed as post-quantum secure; we discuss lattice-based
   108→folding schemes below. Another line of work builds folding schemes from hash-based commitments
   109→and error-correcting codes [BMNW25a,BCFW25,BMNW25b].
   110→Lattice-based folding schemes. A major recent advance is the development of lattice-based fold-
   111→ing schemes, including Lova [FKNP24], LatticeFold [BC25a], LatticeFold+ [BC25b], and Neo [NS25].
   112→These schemes replace Pedersen commitments with plausibly quantum-secure Ajtai commitments,
   113→yielding plausible post-quantum security. However, translating Pedersen-based folding to the lat-
   114→tice setting is not straightforward: Ajtai commitments critically depend on the committed message
   115→being short, i.e., the witness having low norm. This shortness constraint creates difficulties because
   116→the witness norm typically grows with each folding step. Moreover, norm growth arises not only
   117→in folding (the “correctness direction”), but also in extraction (the “extraction direction”), when
   118→recovering a witness for the input relation from a witness for the folded relation. This growth com-
   119→plicates the soundness analysis, since one must ensure that the witness norm stays within acceptable
   120→bounds throughout folding.
   121→ To address the norm growth issues, prior lattice-based folding schemes combine two general
   122→techniques, which are afterward instantiated differently in each folding scheme:
   123→
   124→ – Decomposition. Given a witness vector of norm at most B, this procedure decomposes it into
   125→ chunks of smaller norm b. The chunks are later randomly combined using low-norm verifier
   126→ challenges so that the resulting linear combination has norm at most B. This step is needed for
   127→ completeness, i.e., to ensure the accumulated output witness remains low-norm.
   128→ – Range-check. These are variants of range proofs that certify a witness vector satisfies a prescribed
   129→ bound. This step is needed for soundness, ensuring that extractors output low-norm witness
   130→ vectors.
   131→Under this lens, LatticeFold and LatticeFold+ exhibit the following cost profiles. LatticeFold uses
   132→grand-product-sum-based range-checks for very small bounds (e.g., b = 2). A single such range
   133→check, as we show in this work, is efficient. However, during decomposition, LatticeFold splits each
   134→input witness into k low-norm chunks, commits to them, and then applies a sum-check-based range-
   135→check to each chunk (the sum-checks are batched, but prover time still grows linearly in 2k); hence
   136→the overall combined procedure is relatively expensive, cf. [Res24]. In contrast, LatticeFold+ uses a
   137→very lightweight decomposition step (it decomposes into only 2 vectors).
   138→ The most efficient instantiations of lattice-based primitives use cyclotomic rings of the form
   139→R = Z[X]/⟨Φf (X)⟩, where Φf (X) is the f-th cyclotomic polynomial. Over R, the LatticeFold+ range-
   140→check (which computes so-called double-commitments) is relatively costly: it requires computing at
   141→least φ (the ring degree) “single” Ajtai commitments and then re-committing to them. A cheap
   142→grand-product sum-check-based range-check is unavailable here because the verified norm bound B
   143→is not small enough. Other norm checks (tailored to the ℓ2 -norm) have been used in lattice-based
   144→argument systems [BS23,KLNO24,KLNO25b] and transfer almost directly to the folding setting.
   145→Lattice-based folding schemes for constraints expressed over finite fields. The ring R
   146→admits a natural embedding into Zφ(f) , where φ is Euler’s totient function. However, in many
   147→cryptographic protocols, relations are defined over finite fields Fq . Thus, using cyclotomic rings in
   148→such protocols requires translating relations from Fq to Rq = R/qR. This translation is nontrivial:
   149→4 Albert Garreta, Helger Lipmaa, Urmas Luhaäär, and Michal Osadnik
   150→
   151→one must preserve the algebraic structure of the original relation in the ring setting. Moreover,
   152→Fq -based relations (e.g., R1CS or CCS) typically omit norm constraints, which are essential for
   153→lattice-based security. Accordingly, the translation must also include an appropriate decomposition
   154→so that the resulting relation over Rq is compatible with Ajtai commitments.
   155→ A natural idea is to use the Number Theoretic Transform (NTT), which provides a ring isomor-
   156→ φ(f)
   157→phism Rq ∼ = Fq (when q splits Φf (X) completely), mapping each ring element to its evaluations
   158→at the roots of Φf (X). Each coordinate of this embedding lies in Fq , so one can encode field elements
   159→into individual “slots” of a ring element. For example, consider the cyclotomic ring Z[X]/⟨X 4 + 1⟩
   160→with q = 17. Since X 4 +1 splits completely modulo 17 as (X −2)(X −4)(X −8)(X −15), the Chinese
   161→Remainder Theorem gives an isomorphism Rq ∼ = F417 , mapping a polynomial f (X) to its evaluations
   162→(f (2), f (4), f (8), f (15)). Thus, four field elements a0 , a1 , a2 , a3 ∈ F17 can be “packed” into one ring
   163→element by finding the unique polynomial f (X) ∈ Rq satisfying f (2) = a0 , f (4) = a1 , f (8) = a2 ,
   164→f (15) = a3 . Nevertheless, this approach faces several challenges: (1) The NTT embedding is useful
   165→only when the relation can be “packed” into slots, which typically requires working with multiple
   166→instances of the relation. This is not always possible. Otherwise, the embedding can impose substan-
   167→tial overhead, since the relation must be expanded to fill the slots, increasing complexity. (2) Even
   168→if the Fq witness has small norm, there is no guarantee that the corresponding witness over Rq does,
   169→which is crucial both for the security of Ajtai commitments and for the efficiency of the commitment
   170→step. Hence, the Rq relation must be further decomposed to enforce norm bounds. This decomposi-
   171→tion can be intricate and may inflate proofs and computation, potentially offsetting any gains from
   172→packing multiple field elements into one ring element. (3) Naively encoding Fq -native relations over
   173→Rq forces the folding scheme to operate over Rq . This can be wasteful: operations in Rq are far
   174→more expensive than in Fq , and ring elements are much larger than field elements. It is therefore
   175→desirable to minimize computation over Rq . (4) The NTT embedding requires a modulus q that
   176→splits the cyclotomic polynomial Φf (X), which can severely restrict parameter choices and may be
   177→impractical. Worse, this splitting condition can conflict with other requirements, such as invertibility
   178→of small-norm elements [LS18,ACX19,BS23] or the existence of large subfields [KLNO24].
   179→ To overcome these challenges, Nguyen and Setty (Neo, [NS25]) propose a translation from Fq -
   180→based relations to Rq -based relations that does not rely on the NTT embedding. Neo encodes a
   181→field element c ∈ Fq as a ring element pc (X) ∈ Rq whose coefficients are the base-b decomposition
   182→of c, for a suitable b. This resolves issues 1, 2, and 4 above. In addition, Neo performs one folding
   183→step (analogous to the linearization step in Hypernova [KS24]) entirely over Fq , rather than over
   184→Rq , where prover time would be substantially higher. This addresses issue 3.
   185→ Although powerful, Neo [NS25] introduces fairly complex, ad hoc machinery and terminology
   186→(e.g., matrix commitments and commutative subrings of matrices in place of standard ring-based
   187→constructions). Building on Neo’s key ideas, we show that the same translation from Fq -based to Rq -
   188→based relations can be achieved within a simpler framework that works exclusively with cyclotomic
   189→rings, finite fields, and standard linear maps, yielding a more accessible and modular construction.

   191→1.1 Our contributions
   192→With this work, we make the following contributions:
   193→No decomposition and range-check for input accumulated witnesses. As we mentioned,
   194→all current lattice-based folding schemes from Ξacc × Ξ0 to Ξacc use range-checks to make sure that
   195→the norm of the accumulated instances stays within some specified bound B. This range-check is
   196→ultimately applied to both the input witness from the relation Ξ0 and the input witness from the
   197→ Cyclo: Lightweight Lattice-based Folding via Partial Range Checks 5
   198→
   199→accumulated relation Ξacc . In this work we make the key observation that, if one omits the latter
   200→range-check, then one can ensure that the norm growth of the output accumulated witness only grows
   201→by a small additive factor (see Section 2.5 in the technical overview for more details).
   202→ Consequently, we can perform folding without applying any decomposition or range-check to
   203→the input witness from Ξacc , at the expense of not being able to perform arbitrarily many foldings:
   204→indeed, at each folding step, the norm grows by a small additive factor, and so after a certain finite
   205→number ℓfold of folds, the scheme stops being sound. We instantiate our scheme with ℓfold ranging
   206→from 27 to 220 , which we believe is enough for overwhelmingly many applications. In addition, one
   207→can use a different folding scheme, say LatticeFold+, once every ℓfold folding steps to “refresh” the
   208→norm of the accumulated witness.
   209→No witness decomposition when folding R1CS/CCS constraints over a finite field Fq
   210→with an accumulated instance. As we discuss later, when seeking to apply our folding scheme
   211→on R1CS/CCS constraints over Fq , we encode witnesses as cyclotomic ring elements of very low
   212→norm b (say, b = 2), as done in Neo [NS25]. Thus, the input witness from Ξ0 does not require any
   213→“decomposition,” unlike those mentioned earlier. In this specific setting, thanks to our key obser-
   214→vation, we can perform (a bounded number of) foldings by only range-checking the input witness
   215→from Ξ0 , and then folding with the input accumulated witness. In particular: (1) no decomposi-
   216→tion step is ever applied to any witness, (2) range-check is only applied to the input witness from
   217→Ξ0 , and (3) folding is performed in a standard manner between the range-checked input witness
   218→from Ξ0 and the input accumulated witness. This avoids the most expensive steps of LatticeFold+,
   219→Neo, and LatticeFold, namely the double commitment for the first two and the decomposition into
   220→chunks and range-checking for every chunk in the latter. Ultimately, our scheme only requires two
   221→non-batched4 sum-checks over an extension of Fq for log(mφ)-variate low-degree polynomials. Here,
   222→m is the length of the packed witness w ∈ Rm q in ring elements.
   223→
   224→Remark 1. We stress that the above efficiency gains refer to the folding protocol itself, without
   225→considering the overhead of recursive verification (i.e., in-circuit verification of the folding scheme).
   226→In the recursive setting, the benefit analysis is more nuanced. Encoding field elements as low-
   227→norm polynomials in Rq may increase the witness representation size, since each Fq element is
   228→represented as a ring element of potentially larger bit-length. However, the verifier does not operate
   229→on the witness directly, and the absence of decomposition and extension commitment steps means
   230→that the verifier performs fewer operations throughout the scheme. Therefore, the recursion circuit
   231→can still be significantly smaller than in folding schemes that require decomposition and double
   232→commitment. This advantage is further amplified if Cyclo is (trivially) modified so that the public
   233→instance x (which does appear in the verifier’s circuit) is kept outside the Ajtai commitment and
   234→can thus be represented as a vector of field elements rather than ring elements.
   235→
   236→A new take on witness decomposition + range-check. The fact that we do not need to apply
   237→any decomposition or range-check to the input accumulated witness has yet another consequence
   238→for us. Namely, we can design a new “decomposition + range-check step” using a technique we call
   239→extension commitment (see the technical overview for more details). As we demonstrate, extension
   240→commitments can be an order of magnitude more efficient than LatticeFold+’s double commitments.
   241→4

   243→ prover time linearly in k. In LatticeFold in particular, k is the number of chunks produced in the decomposition

   247→Our extension commitment is then followed by a simple sum-check-based range-check, which, as we
   248→mentioned, is cheap when one does not need to batch many of them, which is our case.

   252→Parameter and efficiency improvements. Our folding scheme is efficient and memory-friendly,
   253→i.e., we obtain numbers which are orders of magnitude smaller than those from [BC25b]. (See Sec-
   254→tion 6.1.) In particular, we show that the folding proofs are around 30 KB for practical parameters,
   255→which is an order of magnitude smaller than those of LatticeFold+.
   256→ We provide efficiency estimates concerning the most computationally expensive part of our fold-
   257→ing scheme, i.e., the extension commitment. We also provide an evaluation of the corresponding
   258→part of LatticeFold+ [BC25b], i.e., double commitment, for comparison.
   259→Bit-size preserving embedding of Fq into Rq via homomorphic preimages, and reduction
   260→from R1CS relations over Fq to linear relations over Rq . As we mentioned, Neo’s approach to
   261→folding R1CS/CCS constraints over Fq relies on relatively non-standard terminology and techniques.
   262→Building on Neo’s ideas, we reformulate them in terms of cyclotomic rings, finite fields, and linear
   263→maps, resulting in a construction that requires only standard algebraic tools and no specialized
   264→matrix-algebraic machinery. Crucially, we observe that Neo can be understood as a scheme that
   265→works in a hybrid manner over Rq and Fq , and which relies on the additive homomorphism θb :
   266→Rq → Fq where θb (f (X)) = f (b) to link both structures.
   267→ We emphasize that Neo’s and our approaches are ultimately equivalent. However, we believe
   268→this equivalence is far from obvious, and that our simple algebraic viewpoint suggests a general
   269→framework for integrating lattice-based cryptography in modern proof systems: namely, to use Rq
   270→solely for commitment purposes, and work otherwise on the base field (or different rings) of interest,
   271→using a morphism to link both structures.
   272→An approximately strong sampling set over NTT-friendly cyclotomics. As a side contribu-
   273→tion, we provide a heuristic analysis of the ternary distribution (i.e., sampling coefficients uniformly
   274→from {−1, 0, 1}) as an approximate strong sampling set, which is of independent interest. Our analy-
   275→sis allows the use of more NTT-friendly cyclotomic rings (e.g., splitting down to quadratic extension
   276→fields with a modulus that fits into a machine word). The results apply to other contexts where
   277→strong sampling sets are needed, e.g., in lattice-based argument systems [BS23,KLNO25b] and could
   278→be viewed as a statistical alternative to the analysis of [LS18,ACX19], offering more flexibility in
   279→the choice of parameters.

   285→ct be the map that takes a ring element to its constant term. Let Rq := R/qR for a prime q. Let
   286→cf be the map that takes a ring element to its coefficient vector in Zφ . Let cf−1 be its inverse map
   287→Zφ → R. Furthermore, we define a map cf∨ (together with its inverse) as cf∨ (a) = (a∨ i )i∈[φ] , where
   288→a ∈ R and the coefficients of a are (a0 , . . . , aφ−1 ), and a∨
   289→ i := ai if i = 0, and a ∨ := −a

   398→rounds, the obtained norm growth is acceptable. Notably, for the hardness of the underlying SIS
   399→problem, parameters are only mildly affected by the number of folding rounds. Concretely, we impose
   400→a bound on the number of folding rounds to be relatively large, e.g., 210 , which is acceptable in
   401→practice.
