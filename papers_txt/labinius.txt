      Abstract. Lattice-based proof systems are one of the leading routes to quantum-safe
      succinct proofs. Existing lattice-based schemes come with inherent limitations arising
      from the coupling between the commitment and constraint domains. First, commit-
      ment efficiency is restricted by the need for invertible challenge differences, which
      precludes the use of fully splitting moduli that enable the fastest lattice arithmetic.
      Second, the characteristic of the constraint field must equal the commitment modulus.
      This limits the efficiency of proving binary computations, including standard hash
      function evaluations.
      We present LaBinius, a lattice-based polynomial commitment scheme, in which the
      evaluation arithmetic is separated from that of the commitment. The commitment
      runs over a composite modulus, and all of its prime factors are NTT-friendly. The
      evaluation claim lives in a binary extension field, without bitness checks or overhead
      over a binary arithmetisation.
      We implement the scheme with AVX-512 kernels and connect it to the Binius and Flock
      front ends in an almost black-box way, which lets us use their arithmetisations tailored
      to binary fields. As a result, we obtain a SNARK for standard hashes. Proving
      Keccak-256, SHA-256 and BLAKE3 on one core, our prover takes at most 1.2 times as
      long as the hash-based prover supplied with each system, and we obtain a single-core
      throughput of over 67,000 BLAKE3 compressions per second with verification in under
      40 milliseconds.
      With LaBRADOR as a recursive compressor, the prover takes less than twice as long
      as the hash-based provers at the largest sizes we consider, while the proof size is
      below 100 KiB. This is about five times smaller than the proofs of Binius and Flock
      at those sizes and constitutes the smallest quantum-safe proof of a standard hash
      evaluation to date.


1     Introduction
Lattice-based proof systems have emerged as a promising class of practical quantum-
safe succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (SNARKs). Compared with other
post-quantum alternatives, lattice proofs offer leading proof sizes below 100 kilobytes
[BS23, KK26, NOZ26, DBK+ 26]. Verifier runtimes can scale polylogarithmically and reach
the single-digit millisecond range in practice [KLN+ 26]. Furthermore, prover runtimes
remain practical even for very large instances.
    A central metric for practical deployment is the total prover runtime, including both
commitment and opening. This is particularly relevant in settings where data are con-
tinuously produced and proofs must be generated efficiently. In blockchain systems, for
example, proof generation must keep pace with incoming transactions, so prover runtime
can directly constrain throughput.

    Strong performance on selected algebraic statements, such as polynomial evaluations
over prime fields, does not necessarily translate into efficient proofs of application-level com-
putations, such as hash function evaluations. This distinction is particularly important to
state-of-the-art lattice-based proof systems, including RoKoko [KLN+ 26], Akita [DBK+ 26],
and Greyhound [NS24]. These systems support only arithmetisations over fixed finite fields
whose characteristics have bit lengths typically between 30 and 50. Such fields are poorly
suited to the bitwise operations prevalent in standard hash functions, which are more
naturally expressed over F2 [DP25]. Akita provides a route to proving such computations
through its integration with the Jolt zkVM [DBK+ 26, GTTZ26]. In this setting, however,
the total prover runtime also includes the overhead of representing and executing the
computation within the zkVM. Meaningful comparisons must therefore account for the
full cost of proving the target computation, including arithmetisation, commitment, and
opening.
    The goal of this work is to substantially reduce the concrete prover runtimes of lattice-
based proof systems while enabling flexible arithmetisations that are optimal for real-world
tasks, without incurring any overhead.

Overview of lattice proofs. State-of-the-art lattice-based SNARK prover back-ends,
e.g. RoKoko [KLN+ 26], can be divided into three main phases, similarly to non-lattice
constructions. First, the prover commits to the witness using an algebraically structured
commitment scheme. Then, in the sumcheck phase (typically in the literature captured
abstractly as PIOP), the relations in the arithmetised proof statement are aggregated
and reduced to a random evaluation of the witness when viewed as a large multilinear
polynomial. More specifically, the polynomial interpolates the mr ring elements of the
witness on the hypercube points {0, 1}log(mr) . Finally, the random evaluation is proven,
which entails an opening proof for the commitment.
    A lattice commitment is a simple high-dimensional linear map over a cyclotomic ring
Rq of degree φ. The witness, consisting of mr ring elements with mrφ integer coefficients
in total, is formatted as a matrix W ∈ Rm×r with column vectors wi ∈ Rm . Then, the
commitment is given by Y = F W with subcommitments yi = F wi , where F is a (fixed)
public uniformly random matrix over Rq . The commitment is binding under the SIS
assumption if the ℓ2 -norm of the witness is sufficiently small. Such a relation instead of
being inspected directly may (and should) be recursed for better compression and verifier
efficiency.
    The sumcheck phase reduces low-degree relations on the witness to a polynomial
evaluation. For compatibility with the lattice commitments the relations do not operate
on the individual integer coefficients but instead on the ring elements inside the wi .
In the opening and evaluation phase, the prover sends a random linear combination
v = W c = c0 w0 + · · · + cr−1 wr−1 of the wi which is called the amortised opening.
Succinctness stems from the fact that v is essentially r times smaller than the witness.

Algebraic setup. In concrete instantiations of the commitment in the literature, the
cyclotomic ring is chosen to be a power-of-two cyclotomic Rq = Zq [x]/(xφ + 1) modulo a
rational prime q. More precisely, the degree φ is a small power of two in the range between
64 and 1024 and the size of the modulus q is in the range of 30 to 50 bits. A minimal
modulus size of about 20 bits is needed for SIS-hardness, and slightly bigger sizes are
chosen so that a negligible soundness error can be achieved more easily. Depending on
the splitting behaviour of q, i.e. the factorisation of xφ + 1 modulo q, Rq is isomorphic
under the Chinese Remainder Theorem to a direct product of φ/l copies of the finite field
Fql . That is, Rq ∼

This is the case when q = 1 mod f, where f is a conductor of a cyclotomic, so that there
are roots of unity of order f in Zq . Fully splitting primes are widely used in basic schemes
such as ML-KEM [SAB+ 22], ML-DSA [LDK+ 22] and FN-DSA [PFH+ 22].
    Unfortunately, up until now this high-performance configuration was believed to not
be possible for lattice-based proof systems. The main reason is as follows. For extracting
the j-th witness column wj from a successful prover, the extractor obtains two accepting
transcripts whose challenge vectors c differ only in the j-th coordinate. Then the difference
v̄ of the two corresponding amortised openings is expected to be c̄j wj for the difference c̄j
of the two j-th challenges. In the fully-splitting case the NTT coefficients of the challenges
in the residue fields Zq do not have enough entropy to force all NTT coefficients of the
difference c̄j to be non-zero. In particular, the challenge difference c̄j ∈ Rq might not be
invertible. Hence, the extractor might not be able to compute the full witness. Moreover,
the sumcheck phase, which works with the same arithmetic, needs large residue fields as
well so that Schwartz–Zippel and the sumcheck protocol have negligible soundness error.
This also prohibits fully-splitting primes in current lattice proof systems.
    As mentioned, the performance of the arithmetisation and sumcheck depends on how
efficiently their arithmetic can handle the arithmetic demanded by the input statement.
In the important case where the input statement performs binary arithmetic, lattice proof
systems need to emulate binary arithmetic in their much larger characteristic. This requires
in particular lifting the binary arithmetic to Z and making the modulo-2 reduction explicit.
And, more severely, it requires to prove that the committed witness is in fact binary.
    This raises the following question:

  Can we construct a lattice-based polynomial commitment scheme that simultaneously
  enables:
      (i) Fast commitments over NTT-friendly cyclotomic rings,

   (ii) Native binary-field evaluation, without explicit modulo-two reductions or separate
        bitness checks,
  (iii) Efficient proofs of standard hash computations, with compact proofs through
        recursive compression?


1.1     Our Contributions
We answer this question in the affirmative. The contributions in this work are summarised
as follows:

   1. We propose a new lattice-based polynomial commitment scheme (PCS) where the
      arithmetic of the evaluation is decoupled from that of the commitment. This allows
      us to instantiate the commitment in a configuration that offers superior performance
      over previous commitment instantiations in lattice proof systems. Moreover, it allows
      the evaluation proof to work over binary extension fields without any overhead.
      The core technical contribution lies in an extraction strategy that avoids reduction
4                                                                                  LaBinius


      modulo the commitment modulus q and amounts to an extraction over the number
      field.

    2. We provide a concrete instantiation of the binary PCS that is primarily optimised for
       maximum prover efficiency, and secondarily tuned for small proof sizes. We combine
       our binary PCS with several state-of-the-art arithmetisation front-ends. To this
       end, we provide a field-switch reduction that carries evaluation claims over the more
       standard F2128 to our binary extension field F2162 .

    3. We have implemented our PCS and introduce new optimisation techniques on
       AVX-512 platforms. We use our implementation for proving various standard hash-
       functions. Our preliminary results show that lattice-based proof systems can compete
       with state-of-the-art code-based proof systems such as Binius [Irr26, DP25, DP26]
       and Flock [BRW26].

    4. We explain how our PCS can easily be combined with the LaBRADOR proof system
       [BS23] in order to compress the proof size. This yields the smallest currently known
       quantum-safe proofs for standard hash functions.

1.2     Related Work
Lattice-based arguments and commitments. A central problem in lattice-based
proof systems has been to establish that the committed witness has small norm or is
otherwise binding. One line of work, including constructions of [AL21, CLM23, CMNW24,
AFLN24, KLNO24], controls norm growth during extraction through challenge sets with
short inverses of pairwise differences. Another uses random projections [LNP22, NS24,
KLNO25, KLOT25, KLN+ 26, BBL+ 26b] or range-proofs [DBK+ 26, NOZ26] to certify
shortness. The same or very similar norm-control techniques also appear in lattice-based
folding, e.g. LatticeFold and LatticeFold+ [BC25a, BC25b, GLLO26] use range proofs and
decomposition, while [Osa26a, KLOT25, Osa26b] rely on random projections. When the
language domain does not require the extracted witness to be short, these norm-control
techniques can be bypassed on the initial levels [NS24, KLN+ 26], however the advocated
extraction strategy of those protocol still requires (at least statistical) invertibility of
challenge differences, which constrains the choice of the commitment modulus. Except
of trivial attempts of proving statements with small modulus over larger modulus with
shortness guarantees (cf. [LSS24, BBL+ 26a]), none of those protocols attempted to
decouple the evaluation or constraint domain from the commitment module.
    Sole exceptions are CELPC [HSS24] and Jindo [HLSS26]. They commit to elements of
a field Zp with p = bγ + 1 by encoding each element as γ ring coefficients of size about b,
via the Z-module isomorphism R/(xφ/γ − b) ∼
                                                  φ/γ
                                              = Zp . Nevertheless, this algebraic trick is
applicable only to prime fields and is not transferable to binary fields.

Extension fields and general-purpose front ends. A related observation is made in
Hachi [NOZ26], which reduces evaluation claims over extension fields to cyclotomic-ring
statements of the same characteristic. Akita [DBK+ 26] also supports prime fields and
extensions, and its integration into Jolt provides a lattice-based route to proving general
RISC-V computations [DBK+ 26, GTTZ26]. Jolt [AST24] can express bitwise operations
through lookups, and SuperSpartan [STW23] supports general algebraic constraint systems.
Such front ends allow a PCS over an odd-characteristic field to prove binary computations,
but the resulting arithmetisation still represents the bitwise operations in that field.

Hash-based commitments and binary proof systems. Code-based polynomial com-
mitments provide a route to flexible evaluation fields. Brakedown [GLS+ 23], Blaze [BCF+ 25],
Michał Osadnik, Gregor Seiler                                                                           5


Bolt [GNR26], and Lightning [QGZ26] improve prover efficiency through fast encodings,
while BaseFold [ZCF24] and WHIR [ACFY25] provide polylogarithmic verification. Liger-
ito [NA25] and Quasar [JLX+ 26] further develop this approach with different choices of
codes and reductions. The strong asset of hash-based schemes is that they are often
field-agnostic, i.e. relying solely on standard field properties, they do not require any
adaptation of the commitment scheme to the desired field, including binary fields. The
price is paid in proof size. Soundness of a hash-based commitment relies on random spot
checks of a Merkle-committed codeword. At regular levels of security this amounts to a
few hundred authenticated openings per proof, and proofs of a few hundred KiB even for
the most compact schemes.

Arithmetisations over binary fields. At the arithmetisation level, Binius [DP25, Irr26],
Flock [BRW26], and BinarySpartan [Set26] target computations with binary structure,
including standard hash functions. Practically, those arithmetisations turn out to be
extremely efficient due to the efficiency of binary field operations and of sumcheck over
them. Concretely, as of today, these proving front ends incur an overhead of only around two
orders of magnitude compared to native CPU hash execution. An immediate application
of such a large throughput is the emerging need for the post-quantum transition of the
Ethereum blockchain [eth26]. Two components of Ethereum rely on quantum-vulnerable
cryptography, the Verkle state commitments planned for stateless clients and the BLS
signatures of the consensus layer. The proposed replacements for both are hash-based, so
verifying them reduces to evaluating a hash function many times. A stateless client holds
only the state root and checks a block against a witness of Merkle branches for every state
access, and over a binary hash tree this witness reaches about 10 MB per block, too large
to broadcast every slot. A proof that the branches exist and hash to the root replaces
the witness by a few hundred kilobytes, and the prover has to hash about 660 000 branch
nodes in the worst case. Since the proof must be ready within a few seconds of the 12
second slot, a prover rate of about 200 000 hashes per second is estimated to suffice [But24].
Aggregating hash-based signatures [DKKW25] of the roughly 28 000 attestations per slot
inside a succinct proof costs a rate of the same order, and both figures appear to be within
the reach of binary-field provers on a single machine [BRW26, Set26].
    The choice of arithmetisation also matters for lattice-based proof systems. To our
knowledge, neither RoKoko nor Greyhound has been benchmarked on standard hash function
evaluations, and their implementations use fixed finite fields whose characteristics have bit
lengths between 30 and 50 [KLN+ 26, NS24]. Expressing the bitwise operations of standard
hashes over such fields can introduce substantial overhead compared with arithmetisations
over binary fields [DP25].
    For Keccak-256, Akita [DBK+ 26] provides a lattice-based proving route through its
integration with Jolt [DBK+ 26, GTTZ26], which includes an optimised Keccak-256 imple-
mentation [Jol]. A 64-byte Keccak-256 input requires one permutation [BDH+ ]. For such
inputs, extrapolating from Jolt’s published cycle counts and LatticeJolt’s reported proving
rates gives rough estimates of 600 hashes/s on a MacBook CPU and 3,000 hashes/s with
GPU acceleration [Jol, GTTZ26].1 The cost of expressing Keccak-256 in the zkVM must
therefore be included when comparing against dedicated binary-field arithmetisations.

1.3     Technical Overview
Throughout, let R = Z[x]/(Φf ) be the ring of integers in a cyclotomic number field
K = Q[x]/(Φf ) of conductor f and degree φ = φ(f), where φ(·) is Euler’s totient function.
We use two rational moduli: a commitment modulus q, which need not be prime, and an
evaluation prime p. We write Rq = R/qR and Rp = R/pR, and choose p inert in R so
that Rp is a field.

1.3.1   Decoupling the commitment and evaluation proof algebra
Existing lattice-based proof systems use the commitment ring Rq also for arithmetisation
and sumcheck. This couples two different requirements: the commitment modulus should
support efficient arithmetic and SIS hardness, whereas the evaluation field should suit the
computation being proved and provide sufficient soundness. Our approach separates these
choices. We commit to a short matrix W ∈ Rm×r through Y = F W mod q, where F is
a public uniformly random matrix over Rq , but perform arithmetisation and sumcheck
modulo a different prime p. The central challenge is to ensure that these two parts of the
proof refer to the same witness, despite operating in different characteristics.
    The connection is provided by the evaluation and opening proof. Write wi for the i-th
column of W and yi for the i-th column of Y , so that yi = F wi mod q. The evaluation
claim concerns the multilinear extension of the coefficients of W , whose variables split
into row variables, indexing within a column, and column variables, indexing the columns.
Let α and β be the vectors of multilinear interpolation coefficients at the row and column
part of the evaluation point, so that the claimed value is αT W β. For the evaluation proof
the prover first sends a partial evaluation where all row variables that interpolate between
the rows of W are filled. It means the prover sends a row vector uT = αT W that is called
the left opening. This partial evaluation can be completed by the verifier to obtain the
full evaluation uT β = αT W β. At the same time the verifier  P can check u by testing

lives over Rq as it is used to open the commitment Y over Rq , which in turn means the
left opening and partial evaluation can only be checked over Rq .
    A first observation when trying to break this connection is that in the honest execution
the witness vectors wi , as well as the challenge polynomials ci , are short, and so in the
computation of v there is no wrap-around modulo q. Hence, the prover effectively computes
v over the ring of integers R. The problem is that we do not know that the prover is
honest. In fact, that v is short does not necessarily imply that all the wi are short. A
simple counterexample for power-of-two cyclotomics Zq [x]/(xφ + 1) is given by a coefficient
w of some wi whose coefficients are all ±(q − 1)/2 and a challenge ci that has an even
number of nonzero coefficients in {−1, 1}. Note that challenge polynomials are usually
sampled to be low-weight ternary.
    A natural idea to circumvent this problem is to require the prover to provide a proof
that all wi are in fact short. But, first, such a range proof is expensive, and second, the
range proof needs to be verified over Rq . Our goal, however, is specifically to not verify
witness relations over Rq , so that does not get us far.
    Instead, we propose the following technique. Recall how a witness is extracted from
amortised openings. The extractor forks the prover on the j-th coordinate of the challenge
c and obtains two accepting right openings v and v ′ for challenges that differ only in cj .
Subtracting them gives v̄ = v − v ′ and c̄j = cj − c′j with F v̄ = c̄j yj mod q, and for the
honest prover v̄ = c̄j wj over R. The extractor then divides by c̄j to obtain a candidate
witness wj∗ with v̄ = c̄j wj∗ . We do not let this division be performed in Rq , but instead
over the number field K. Notice that the non-zero c̄j is always invertible in the field K.
    In the honest execution this still gives the correct wj . But of course the objection
from before does not go away. The prover can still commit to long vectors. Concretely,
in the example above, we are now extracting a witness vector that is a proper rational
vector with twos in the denominators. Moreover, the extracted vector might not even be
a preimage for the commitment. We do not know if it has a prime ideal above q in the
denominator and so its reduction mod q is not well-defined. But it turns out this is all
fine and we can prove two properties:
   i) The commitment is binding with respect to extracted rational vectors.
  ii) The reduction modulo the prime p which we want to use for the arithmetisation is
      well-defined.
    So why is a (sub-)commitment yj binding with respect to the extracted rational vectors
although yj was computed over Rq and its opening is checked over Rq ? Observe that
what we get from the extraction is a candidate rational opening wj∗ over the number field
K, together with a polynomial c̄j ∈ R that is both integral and short. Moreover, we have
that v̄ = c̄j wj∗ is also both integral and short, and

                                           c̄j yj = F v̄ mod q.

Normally, the consistency check is performed over Rq by the verifier as well, and hence the
last equation also only holds over Rq . Instead we let the consistency be checked modulo p
over Rp , and the prover also computes uT = αT W modulo p. Next, we want to substitute
v̄ = c̄j wj∗ again, but need to make sure that this makes sense modulo p. In the case where
c̄j is invertible modulo p, i.e. it is coprime to p, the valuation of wj∗ is non-negative at all
the prime ideals above p since v̄ is integral. So in this case the reduction of wj∗ modulo p
is well-defined. We ensure this invertibility by choosing the challenge set so that reduction
modulo p is injective. In particular, if Rp is a field, any two distinct challenges then have
an invertible difference modulo p. For example, the elements whose coefficients in the
power basis lie in {0, . . . , p − 1} map bijectively onto Rp ; a suitable subset can be used to
control challenge norms. This invertibility condition comes essentially for free: we already
choose Rp to be a sufficiently large field for sumcheck and polynomial identity testing, and
injectivity of the challenge set supplies the remaining condition. No additional invertibility
requirement is imposed on the commitment modulus q.
     We briefly mention that the previous paragraph hints at a slightly more general
observation. If the challenges have high min-entropy modulo some prime ideal p and in
particular if they are almost uniformly random modulo p, then the extracted rational
opening wj∗ will not have a negative valuation at p. Put differently, negative valuations
will only occur at prime ideals of small norm.
     In this work, we focus solely on p = 2, as this regime is relevant to the main application
of this work, which is to prove the execution of standard hash functions. The extracted
witness over K need not itself have binary coefficients: its reduction modulo 2 defines the
binary coefficients to which the arithmetised statement refers. We therefore avoid a separate
proof that the committed witness is binary. To obtain a large binary residue field, we choose
a power-of-three cyclotomic in which 2 is inert. Concretely, R = Z[x]/(x162 + x81 + 1) gives
R2 ∼ = F2162 . The same extraction strategy applies to other choices of p and R satisfying
these conditions.

1.3.2   Efficient commitments and folding
Freedom in selecting the commitment modulus q allows us to choose a product of small
fully-splitting primes instead of a single large prime. We do not stop at this, but instead
further structure the commitment matrix F over a larger superring S ⊃ R. For our
concrete instantiation, we choose S ∼  = Z[x]/(x648 − x324 + 1) of conductor 23 · 35 . Such a
ring is particularly favourable for the NTT implementation, as it allows for radix-2 and
radix-3 splittings. There are plenty of small primes that are NTT-friendly in S, including
3889 and 9721, which fit in 14 bits, 17497 and 19441, which fit in 15 bits, and 2917, 4861,
and 12637, which fit in 14 bits but split only into quadratic factors. These primes exhibit
various tradeoffs, but all enjoy highly efficient NTT implementations. We use two or three
of these primes in the product for the commitment modulus q to obtain the target SIS
security level. Furthermore, since the witness is short (here, binary), folding the witness
matrix W is done over only one fully-splitting prime. The coefficient bounds ensure that
the folded result can be recovered without wraparound. Therefore, even though both
commitment and folding require a single pass over the witness matrix, in practice folding
is three to four times faster than commitment.

1.3.3   Controlling precisely the security level
With the ring dimensions and norm bounds fixed, restricting q to products of small
primes makes security tuning coarse: adding or removing one prime changes a modulus
substantially. We obtain finer control using bit-dropping, inspired by public-key compression
in Dilithium [LDK+ 22] and adapted in [LNP22]: we omit low-order bits of the commitment
and let the verifier tolerate the resulting bounded error. This reduces commitment size at
the cost of increasing the norm bound in the underlying SIS problem, allowing us to trade
excess security for smaller proofs without changing the modulus.

1.3.4   Recursion with LaBRADOR
A significant advantage of our protocol, and lattice-based proofs in gerneral, is that, due to
the simple algebraic structure of the commitment, the evaluation proof verifier only has to
perform cyclotomic ring arithmetic apart from Fiat-Shamir hashes. This allows for straight-
forward recursive proofs of the verifier with a further lattice proof system. We exemplify
this using LaBRADOR [BS23]. More precisely, for a witness containing N = mr ring
elements,

1.3.5   Integration with Binius and Flock
We integrate our PCS with the PIOPs underlying Binius [Irr26, DP25, DP26] and Flock [BRW26]
to prove standard hash computations. These protocols produce evaluation claims over
F2128 , whereas our PCS operates over F2162 . A field-switch reduction inspired by [DP26]
connects the two without modifying the front-end arithmetisation. We pack the 128 binary
coordinates of each source-field element into an F2 -subspace of F2162 . To connect the
evaluation claims, the prover sends partial evaluations whose recombination in F2128 must
equal the claimed value. The verifier then randomly aggregates their packed versions,
and a sumcheck over F2162 reduces their consistency with the packed witness to a single
evaluation claim for our PCS.
    The resulting systems retain the front-end constraint reductions while replacing their
commitment and opening layers with our Ajtai commitment and evaluation proof. Our
AVX-512 benchmarks in Section 5 show competitive non-recursive prover and verifier times,
while the LaBRADOR wrapper produces substantially smaller proofs than the compared
Binius and Flock configurations. In the most favourable non-recursive configuration, we
prove over 67,000 BLAKE3 compression calls per second on a single CPU core, corresponding
to a throughput of about 4.3 MB of data per second.

1.4     Perspective
Our construction shifts attention to the cost of recursive compression. Wrapping our PCS
in LaBRADOR [BS23] gives small proofs, but leaves room to improve verification time.
A natural next step is to explore a wrapper based on RoKoko [KLN+ 26], or a dedicated
argument tailored to the verifier of our PCS. Such a construction could retain the benefits
of our commitment and evaluation protocols while reducing the cost of verifying the final
proof.
    A second direction is to extend our construction to zero knowledge. A possible
approach is to hide the commitments and openings inside a zero-knowledge argument such
10                                                                                       LaBinius


as LaBRADOS [BBL+ 26a], and to blind the sumcheck messages using techniques similar to
those of [XZZ+ 19]. Turning this approach into an efficient construction requires ensuring
that the entire composed protocol hides the witness, including the information revealed by
the evaluation proof.
     A longer-term application of our work is incrementally verifiable computation (IVC) [Val08,
KST22], where each step certifies both a new computation and the validity of the preceding
proof. Recent lattice-based folding schemes provide promising building blocks for this
task [BC25b, BC25a, KLOT25, NS25, NS26, GLLO26, Osa26a, Osa26b]. Their use of Aj-
tai commitments parallels the role of Pedersen commitments [Ped92] in discrete-log-based
folding schemes: the verifier can form a commitment to a linear combination of witnesses by
taking the same linear combination of their commitments, without accessing the witnesses
themselves. This homomorphism is what allows the commitments to be folded alongside
the witnesses. Turning these folding schemes into IVC also requires certifying the hash
computations used by the Fiat–Shamir transform, which are not directly captured by this
algebraic folding. Our construction brings these two requirements together: it supports
efficient proofs of standard hash computations while retaining an Ajtai commitment to the
witness. Realising this integration remains an open direction.

4    Recursion into LaBRADOR

At the end of Πfold , the prover would ordinarily reveal the folded witness v, commitment
matrix Y , and left expansion u, which satisfy the folded equations and norm bound


                    F v = Y c mod q,             Bv = uT c mod 2,            ∥v∥ ≤ β ′ .


Our recursive variant replaces this large final message with Ajtai commitments to Y and
u, followed by one LaBRADOR proof that opens them and proves the folded relation. The
commitments are fixed before the folding challenge, so the prover cannot choose either
object after seeing the coefficients c.
   The main technical obstacle is that the folded equations use Rq and R2 , whereas
LaBRADOR works over the smaller power-of-two ring R′Q with its separate modulus Q.
We bridge the rings by cutting every witness ring element into coefficient chunks and
every public multiplier into blocks small enough that their products are computed in
R′Q without wraparound. The resulting block equations are connected by short carry
ring elements, including two correction terms that implement reduction by Φ3κ (x). An
RNS encoding handles the equations modulo q, while an integer lift followed by reduction
modulo 2 handles the binary equation and the claimed multilinear evaluation. Quotients
and carries are gadget-decomposed into bounded digits so that all auxiliary witnesses
meet LaBRADOR’s norm requirements. Random linear tests enforce that chunks have zero
coefficients outside their prescribed supports, and an explicit no-wrap bound turns the
identities proved modulo Q back into integer identities.
    These checks form a standard LaBRADOR relation and give the completeness and
knowledge-soundness guarantees of Theorem 2, and Appendix A presents the full details
of this mechanical translation.

5.1       Implementation
We implement the commitment, the fold, and their verifier in Rust,3 with the arithmetic
kernels written for AVX-512. The prover and verifier use the same transforms and inner-
product routines, with the verifier applying them to the shorter folded opening. All kernels
use one thread. The implementation supports clear openings, bit-dropped commitments,
and recursive openings through LaBRADOR.

5.1.1      Binary Extension Field
For f = 35 = 243, the binary residue field is

                                 F = R2 ∼
                                        = F2 [x] / (x162 + x81 + 1).

An element of F has 162 binary coefficients and is stored in three machine words. Within
each word, the bits are the coefficients of consecutive powers of x. For example, the bit
string 10 represents x, and multiplying 10 by 10 gives 100, which represents x2 . When
several terms contribute to the same coefficient, they are combined modulo two by exclusive-
or. The word-sliced SIMD layout places the first word of several field elements in one vector
register, their second word in another register, and their third word in a third register.
Write two elements as a0 + a1 x64 + a2 x128 and b0 + b1 x64 + b2 x128 . We apply VPCLMULQDQ
carry-less multiplication instruction to the three diagonal products ai bi and the three cross
products (ai + aj )(bi + bj ) in parallel across vector lanes. These six products determine
all coefficients of the unreduced product. Reduction uses the identity x162 = x81 + 1 in
F and folds the high coefficients back twice. An inner product can therefore accumulate
the six unreduced product components for every summand without coefficient growth and
perform this reduction only once at the end.
    The source field T = F2128 is represented in the GHASH basis, with modulus x128 +
x + x2 + x + 1. We use the F2 -linear packing map

5.1.2      Ring Arithmetic and NTT Transformation
The committed ring elements are represented in the cyclotomic superring S = Z[ζ8f ]. The
embedding R → S maps ζf to −ζ8f    4

We pack four consecutive elements of F into one element of S by interleaving their bits
across the four module components. Multiplication by an element of R, including every
folding challenge, acts identically on these components. The commitment and fold can
therefore process the packed elements without separating them. The analysis of Section 3
carries over verbatim: W lives in S m×r and F in Sqn×m , the challenges and slacks stay

in R and act R-linearly on the four components, and the binary claims are read on each
component separately, as 2 ramifies in S and S/2S is not a field. The SIS instances
estimated below are accordingly over S.
    Each residue occupies one signed SIMD lane, and a vector register holds the same
coefficient of several independent ring elements. The NTT follows the mixed-radix fac-
torisation of the superring conductor. For fully splitting primes, the transform ends in
linear slots. We also support primes for which the final factors are irreducible quadratics.
Multiplication in these slots uses a Karatsuba formula, and the output is converted to
the same public module layout as in the fully splitting case. The reduction schedule is
selected for each modulus from the available signed-lane headroom. Smaller moduli admit
lazy reduction across several levels. The supported primes are 3889, 9721, 17497, and
19441 for fully splitting transforms, and 2917, 4861, and 12637 for transforms ending in
quadratic factors. We select two or three primes for the commitment modulus according
to the target SIS security level.
    The binary input permits a specialised forward transform. The upper levels and the
following twiddle factors form a linear function of a small group of input bits, so we
replace them with byte-table lookups. The remaining butterflies use signed Montgomery
arithmetic. For fully splitting small primes, the final levels are hand-scheduled in assembly
so that an entire transform block remains in vector registers. The verifier uses a generic
transform with the same tree for the non-binary folded opening.

5.1.3   Commitment and Fold
The Ajtai commitment is one inner product per NTT slot and RNS limb. The public
matrix F is stored in the transform’s centred slot layout and read once per commitment.
Vector dot-product instructions accumulate signed lane products into wider lanes without
reducing each product.
    The commitment retains the transformed witness for the single RNS limb. The prover
computes v = W c within this limb. Only the folded result is transformed back to its
centred coefficient representation. The fold therefore reads the retained transform once
and uses only the base limb. The verifier transforms v modulo every limb, recomputes F v
with the commitment kernel, and compares it with Y c. It also checks the coefficient norm
of v and its evaluation claim.
    The challenge set consists of signed elements of R with fixed Hamming weight, nonzero
coefficients in {−1, 1}. We sample the support uniformly with a partial Fisher-Yates
shuffle[FY38, Dur64]. The signs are deterministically derived by a keyed hash of the
support. Since −1 = 1 mod 2, signing preserves the image of the challenge in F while
reducing the average norm of sums of challenges. To control the operator norm, we evaluate
the squared magnitude of the challenge in Fourier slots. The slot energy gives an upper
bound on the operator norm.

5.1.4   Bit Dropping Optimisation
We reduce a clear commitment by omitting d low bits. The prover first applies the Garner
algorithm to convert the RNS residues into mixed-radix digits. Coefficientwise, write
Y = Y0 + 2d Y1 , where Y0 is the rounding error. The prover sends Y1 together with the
remaining mixed-radix digits. The verifier uses these digits to reconstruct the rounded
commitment and computes z ′ := F v − 2d Y1 c = Y0 c mod q. It transforms only this residual
back to coefficient form. The Garner algorithm then reconstructs the centred integer
residual coefficientwise from its RNS limbs and accumulates its squared norm. The identity
(F I)(v, −z ′ ) = 2d Y1 c mod q is a normal-form SIS relation. The residual −z ′ is the
component corresponding to the identity block, and the extracted preimage is p

5.1.5    Connection with Binius and Flock
We use the lattice commitment as the polynomial commitment layer beneath both Binius
[Irr26] and Flock [BRW26]. For Binius, we retain its constraint reductions and packed
binary witness, but replace its Merkle-tree commitment and BaseFold opening with our
Ajtai commitment and folding protocol. These reductions produce an evaluation claim
over T, which the field-switch protocol converts into a claim over F for our commitment to
open. For Flock, we likewise retain its R1CS zero-check and linear-check, bind the lattice
commitment into its transcript, and pass the resulting evaluation claim through the same
field switch. Both front ends end their reductions on the bits of the packed trace rather
than on the words, with ν multilinear coordinates over the words and, within a word, ι
multilinear coordinates for Binius and a univariate skip over six of them for Flock. Instead
of first reducing this to a claim on the words over T by the ring switching of [DP25], and
then switching to F via Figure 2, we observe that the within-word part of the claim is a
                       ι
public vector ω ∈ T2 of weights on the 2ι partial evaluations t, and run the switch on
the bit-level claim directly, with ω T t in place of θ T t in the verifier’s check. This saves one
sumcheck of ν rounds and one message of 2ι elements of T. Thus the two integrations
share the commitment and opening implementation while retaining their respective outer
protocols. The two statements differ in shape. For Binius the circuit is a single hash of
one long message, so its compressions form one chain and each consumes the state the
previous one produced. For Flock the statement is a batch of independent compression
calls with no chaining between them. We could not integrate BinarySpartan [Set26], as its
code was not available at the time of writing.
    We remark that the codebases of Binius and Flock differ significantly from those
described in the publications [DP25, BRW26] (e.g. Binius got enriched with some ideas
from Flock, including univariate skip, and Flock does not support Keccak-256 proofs).
They are under active development4 . We made a few modifications to the Flock prover to
ensure that our comparison is fair. First, Flock uses Ligerito in a default profile targeting
about 128 bits of security. We modify it to aim for 100 bits of security at rate 1/2. Second
Flock defaults to BLAKE3 for its Merkle trees, which is not optimal on our machine. We
therefore use hardware-accelerated SHA-256 Merkle trees for Flock and Ligerito throughout,
which is the faster choice for them on the hardware used in our experiments.

5.1.6    Comparison with Other Polynomial Commitments
We compare our implementation with the BaseFold [ZCF24], WHIR [ACFY25], and Ligerito
[NA25] polynomial commitments. All of those PCS are field-agnostic, and for fairness
of comparison we instantiate them over the GHASH field. For BaseFold and WHIR, the
implementation is taken from the Binius codebase [Irr26], and for Ligerito we use the
implementation from the Flock repository [BRW26]. Binius has since removed its WHIR
implementation. We keep WHIR in the comparison nonetheless, so the WHIR rows are built
from a commit from the day before the removal, while every other row uses the commits
stated in the citations. We use BaseFold, WHIR and Brakedown in the unique-decoding
regime with no grinding at 100 bits, and Ligerito in the two Flock profiles built for 100 bits,
which rely on the Johnson bound with out-of-domain samples and, at rate 1/4, on 16 bits
of grinding per level. All three commit with Reed–Solomon codes, whose relative distance
at rate ρ is 1 − ρ, so the rate is a free parameter, and we commit at ρ = 1/2 and ρ = 1/4. A
higher rate implies more queries, thus a larger opening proof, but faster commitment and
opening time for a prover. We also compare against the tensor commitment of Brakedown
[GLS+ 23], whose expander code encodes in a linear number of field operations rather than

5.1.7   Recursion
The recursive mode links the C implementation of LaBRADOR [BS23] as a static library
through a foreign-function interface. The prover and verifier construct the encoding of
Appendix A.2. The recursive statement is bound to the preceding transcript by a digest of
its absorbed data.

5.1.8   SIS Parameter Selection
We estimate the conventional lattice attack on each SIS instance with the Lattice Esti-
mator [APS15] and target λ = 100 bits of security. In the correctness direction we use
average-case estimates for the norms of the folded witness. For the soundness direction,
we use the worst-case bound on the extracted preimage as stated in Section 3. For bit-
dropped commitments, the length bound includes both the extracted p folded witness and
the reconstructed rounding residual through the combined bound Bv2 + Bz2′ .
    We account for the module-BKZ predictions of [DEdP25] and allow the attack to use
every relevant subfield. For conductors of the form 2a 3b , the lowest estimated cost is
obtained over Q(ζ3 ). The conversion from the coefficient length bound to the canonical
embedding assumes that a short vector has a random direction there, under which the
conversion follows from the trace-form Gram matrix. We translate the resulting block size
to its module-BKZ equivalent and evaluate it with the same reduction-cost model as the
conventional attack.

5.4    Binius and Flock as the outer proof systems
We use the codebase of Binius [Irr26] and Flock [BRW26] in semi black-box manner, i.e.
we separate its information-theoretic reductions used to turn the original statement into an
evaluation claim over T from its cryptographic commitment and opening. More precisely, we
use its reductions and the witness generation, but replace its Merkle-tree commitment over
a Reed–Solomon codeword and BaseFold or Ligerito opening with our Ajtai commitment
and PCS from Section 3. The resulting evaluation claim over T is passed through the field
switch (Figure 2) to F for our commitment to open.
   Tables 2 to 4 report Keccak-256, SHA-256 and BLAKE3 under Binius, and Tables 5 and 6
report BLAKE3 and SHA-256 under Flock. In those benchmarks, we do not distinguish
prover and commitment times as we envision that in practical applications we mainly care
about general throughput of the proof geenration. For Binius, the prover times exclude
witness generation, and for Flock we include generation as in the original codebases. For
Flock we run its single-table path, which commits the full padded witness, since that is
the path in which our integration replaces the commitment.
   For Binius, at the largest parameters we consider and for all three hashes, i.e. Keccak-
256, SHA-256 and BLAKE3, our non-recursive prover costs at most 1.2 times the original

times and our recursive verifier less than 1.4 times. At those parameters our recursive
proof is about five times shorter than the Binius proof. The Binius verifier evaluates the

A.2      Encoding Cyclotomic Arithmetic
We specialise the relations from Section 3 to the cyclotomic ring R = Z[ζf ] of conductor
f = 3κ , where κ ∈ N, so that φ = 2 · 3κ−1 and

                        RQ ∼
                           = ZQ [x] / (Φf (x)),            Φf (x) = xφ + xφ/2 + 1.

The folded constraints use the moduli q and 2. We first express them as integer identities
in R and then reduce them modulo the separate LaBRADOR modulus Q, which gives
identities in RQ . The two cyclotomic rings modulo Q do not match. Elements of RQ have
φ coefficients, while elements of R′Q have d coefficients, where d is a power of two and
smaller than φ. An element of RQ does not fit into one LaBRADOR witness element, and
a product in RQ is not a product in R′Q .
    We work with coefficients only. A witness element x ∈ RQ is cut into chunks of c
consecutive coefficients, and every chunk is stored as one element of R′Q . For a public
element g ∈ RQ , the product g x is, before reduction modulo Φf , the sum over all chunks
of the product of the chunk with a shifted copy of g. To keep every such product inside
R′Q , the shifted copies of g are cut as well, into blocks of p consecutive coefficients. The
product of a block with a chunk has at most p + c − 1 ≤ d coefficients, so LaBRADOR
computes it exactly,
                 P without reduction modulo x + 1.
                                                    d

    An identity ν gν xν = z in RQ then becomes one equation per block of p coefficient
positions, in the same way that two long integers are added digit by digit. There are
N := φ/p blocks, indexed by a ∈ [N ], and block a covers positions ap, . . . , ap + p − 1. The
equation for block a collects the block products that start at position ap. Such a product
has p + c − 1 coefficients, so it also covers the first c − 1 positions of block a + 1. This
excess is moved into the equation of block a + 1 through a carry ea : the equation of block
a subtracts xp ea , and the equation of block a + 1 adds ea . The carry out of the last block
covers positions φ and above, which do not exist in RQ . Reduction modulo Φf replaces xφ
by −xφ/2 − 1, so this last carry is instead subtracted once in block 0 and once in block
N/2. Figure 5 shows the layout.

A.5     Protocol and Security
The message f is committed as Y = F W mod q with vec(W ) = lift(ϕ(f )). The com-
mitment algorithm first fixes the RNS residue vectors and the pre-commitment TY . The
evaluation protocol then proves an opening of TY and the requested evaluation claim.
    The commitment phase is separate from the evaluation proof. The algorithm Com(pp, f )
returns TY and an opening state st containing the committed message, the Ajtai opening
data with slack 1, the matrix Y , and its RNS residue vectors. The interaction between P
and V proves that this state opens TY and that the evaluation at r equals t. The extractor
outputs an opening state satisfying Open(pp, TY , f , st) = 1 for which the decoded binary
evaluation constraint gives MLE[f ](r) = t.
