        Abstract. Sitting at the core of folding-based recursion, multi-instance
        accumulation still forces the recursive verifier to combine ℓ committed ob-
        jects, incurring Θ(ℓ) circuit-expensive commitment random linear com-
        binations (CRCs). Here ℓ denotes the number of instances accumulated
        per step, and a CRC is an in-circuit random linear combination of com-
        mitments. Eliminating the linear-in-ℓ CRC overhead is the central chal-
        lenge, and we address it with Quasar, reducing in-circuit CRCs from Θ(ℓ)
        to O(1) per step, while the unavoidable cost of reading and processing
        public inputs remains linear as usual. Realizing constant-commitment
        accumulation, Quasar’s multi-cast reduction commits to a union poly-
        nomial and verifies a random partial evaluation, yielding a reduced in-
        stance with O(1) commitments. Moreover, composing multi-cast with
        a standard 2-to-1 folding reduction yields a multi-instance accumula-
        tion scheme whose verifier uses O(log ℓ) field work and O(1) CRCs per
        step. Across instantiations with suitable polynomial commitments, our
        instantiation supports linear-time accumulation provers, plausible post-
        quantum security, and parallelizable proving at each step. Not only does
        Quasar keep CRCs constant in ℓ, it also yields a multi-instance incre-
        mentally verifiable computation with lower recursion overhead.

        Keywords: Zero-Knowledge Proofs · Recursive Arguments · Accumu-
        lation Schemes.


1     Introduction

Succinct Non-interactive ARguments of Knowledge (SNARKs) enable succinct
verification of computations across applications, including zero-knowledge vir-
tual machines [2,46], verifiable databases [58,59], and verifiable inference [45,53].
In a monolithic SNARK [50], the prover records the full execution trace and
proves correctness end-to-end using a general-purpose proof system [37,9,33].
As computations scale, monolithic proving remains costly despite substantial
prover-side optimizations [6,50,13,35,34]. For example, proving a zkVM execu-
tion, e.g., EVM or RISC-V [38,39] expressed as an arithmetic circuit with 226
gates can require around 1 hour of proving time [24] and more than 200 GB of
memory [54]. These costs motivate recursive and incremental approaches that
avoid materializing the full trace at once, but they introduce a separate per-step
verification bottleneck addressed by this work.

IVC and PCD. A standard alternative is to partition a large computation into
smaller chunks and represent each chunk as a predicate. The prover produces a
proof for each predicate instance and then recursively “squeezes” these proofs
into a single one. This avoids holding the full trace at once and enables incre-
mental progress. The sequential setting is incrementally verifiable computation
(IVC) [55], and its generalization to directed acyclic graphs is proof-carrying data
(PCD) [26]. Compared to monolithic SNARKs, IVC/PCD reduces peak mem-
ory, supports streaming proof production, and can enable prover parallelism in
several constructions (discussed below).

Accumulation and folding schemes. Traditional IVC/PCD via recursive compo-
sition invokes a SNARK at each step to verify the previous step’s proof [10,9,28],
which requires embedding a full SNARK verifier into the next-step proving cir-
cuit and is typically impractical [28,25]. Accumulation (or folding) schemes in-
stead aggregate per-step proofs into a succinct accumulator [14,19,18,12,42], so
the recursive circuit verifies only an accumulation step. A final decider checks
the terminal accumulator in time independent of the number of steps, enabling
practical recursion.

1.1    Problem Statements
While IVC/PCD improves memory scalability, the per-step recursive circuit can
still dominate cost. In Nova-style frameworks [42,43,41], the per-step circuit typ-
ically splits into two components.
• The trace for computing a new predicate input (the computation overhead ).
• The trace for verifying the accumulation between the new proof and the run-
  ning accumulator (the recursion overhead )4 .
Empirically, for R1CS, the recursion overhead for a single accumulation can
consume around 10,000 constraints [44], which is 2–3× larger than the compu-
tation overhead [49]. This cost limits the number of recursive steps that can be
instantiated in practice.
    A common optimization, used in ProtoGalaxy [30] and in PCD solutions [18,50],
is to process multiple predicate instances or accumulators per recursive step. We
refer to these as multiple accumulation schemes of arity ℓ, following prior termi-
nology [41,23]. This reduces the number of recursive steps by a factor of roughly ℓ,
but it does not necessarily reduce the dominant cost inside each recursive step.

    The core issue is that recursion overhead is often dominated by commitment
random linear combination (CRC) operations, which are circuit-inefficient. For
curve-based commitments, CRC typically includes non-native elliptic-curve oper-
ations. For code-based commitments, CRC typically includes Merkle path verifi-
cation and related hashing. Existing multiple accumulation schemes [19,18,12,42,23]
require the verifier to perform Θ(ℓ) CRC operations per step.
    Formally, let CRC denote the cost of computing one random linear combina-
tion C := r1 · C1 + r2 · C2 . To prove N computation chunks, an IVC protocol
employing a 2-accumulation (left of Figure 1) requires N steps and a total of
N · t CRC operations in the recursive circuits, where t is the maximum number of
commitments in an accumulator. Thus, though multiple accumulation (right of
Figure 1) reduces the number of recursive steps, it does not remove the dominant
in-circuit CRC work, since existing ℓ-ary verifiers still incur Θ(ℓ) such operations
per step. In recursive settings where CRC is circuit-expensive, this leaves the
dominant recursion overhead asymptotically unchanged in ℓ.
    These considerations motivate the following question.

Motivation. Can we design a multiple accumulation scheme whose recursive
verifier uses a constant number of in-circuit CRC operations in the arity ℓ?
Equivalently, can we make the dominant in-circuit commitment-combination
cost scale as O(1) in ℓ, while treating the linear cost of reading and processing
public inputs as orthogonal and unchanged?

1.2   Contributions
Quasar is a multiple accumulation scheme whose recursive verifier uses a con-
stant number of CRC operations in the arity ℓ. Quasar answers the above question
by keeping the dominant in-circuit commitment-combination cost constant in ℓ.

New interactive oracle reduction. Building on oracle batching [22], we iden-
tify a recursion-friendly reduction interface—multi-cast—that compresses ℓ in-


                                        3
stances into a constant-commitment derived instance suitable for folding. At a
high level, multi-cast reduction transforms ℓ input instances into a single derived
instance while ensuring that the output instance-proof pair contains only a con-
stant number of polynomial commitments. Technically, Quasar achieves this via
an efficient polynomial partial evaluation check that lets the verifier validate the
required commitment relations with O(1) CRC operations, plus O(log ℓ) field
work. This targets the dominant circuit cost in recursive settings, while leav-
ing the linear cost of reading and processing public inputs unchanged. The key
structural point is that multi-cast outputs a constant-size committed interface
that is preserved under folding, rather than merely providing a batched check
over ℓ instances.

Accumulation schemes. We propose a framework for constructing accumu-
lation schemes from (i) a multi-cast reduction and (ii) a 2-to-1 reduction for
special-sound protocols (SPS) [16]. Main Theorem 1 formalizes the composition
and isolates the ℓ-dependence to O(log ℓ) explicit field work, while CRC remains
independent of ℓ. For simplicity, we focus on the setting where each recursive
step takes ℓ NARK (Non-interactive ARguments of Knowledge) proofs and one
running accumulator as inputs. In this setting, each fold level uses O(1) CRC
operations and the O(log ℓ) levels contribute O(log ℓ) explicit field work, ex-
cluding linear public-input processing. Since our construction is phrased over
polynomial oracles, it is compatible with a variety of polynomial commitments,
and can achieve plausible post-quantum security based on cryptographic hash
functions or lattice-based assumptions [22,11].

Instantiations and applications. To demonstrate applicability, we construct
a multi-instance IVC protocol that, at each step, takes ℓ predicate instances and
a single running accumulator as inputs. We instantiate the underlying polyno-
mial commitment scheme (PCS) using linear-time encodable codes [35,56], and
employ a NARK for multivariate Plonkish constraint systems [24], yielding a
post-quantum IVC with a linear-time prover. Although this multi-instance IVC
does not have the tree structure of PCD [50], it enables parallel execution of the
accumulation prover within each step. Applying the same technique to PCD can
reduce the recursion costs by a constant factor; however, achieving a PCD with
an accumulation verifier that is sublinear in ℓ in the strong sense remains open.

1.3   Performance Analysis
We analyze the recursive verification cost by separating (i) in-circuit commit-
ment combinations and (ii) public-input processing. Throughout, ℓ denotes the
accumulation arity. We write CRC for one in-circuit commitment random linear
combination, e.g., computing C := r1 · C1 + r2 · C2 . In curve-based instantiations,
CRC induces non-native elliptic-curve arithmetic inside the recursive circuit. In
hash-based instantiations, CRC induces hashing work, e.g., Merkle-path verifi-
cation. Quasar reduces the in-circuit CRC cost from Θ(ℓ) to O(1) per recursive

1.4   Scope

Although folding/accumulation schemes enable practical IVC/PCD, key deploy-
ment requirements remain orthogonal to the bottleneck studied here. First,
zkVM-style workloads (e.g., EVM, RISC-V, Wasm) often yield heterogeneous
circuits, whereas many recursive systems are optimized for more uniform con-
straint structures. Second, many IVC/PCD constructions target a single prover
and do not provide per-step zero-knowledge for intermediate witnesses (e.g.,
Nova [42]), limiting use in privacy-sensitive settings.
    Our goal is to introduce a clean primitive (multi-cast reduction) and a mod-
ular composition theorem that yields O(1) CRC per recursive step. Accordingly,
our evaluation is scoped to validating the circuit footprint implied by the cost
model, rather than end-to-end system throughput. In particular, we measure
constraint counts (and related resource usage) for the accumulation-verification
logic embedded in a recursive circuit under varying arity, and we treat these
results as sanity-check datapoints for the predicted ℓ-dependence. We do not
report end-to-end multi-instance IVC performance, since a complete prototype
would require a recursive wrapper together with an in-circuit verifier for each
PCS backend, and these engineering components are orthogonal to the primi-
tive and composition results established here. Concrete wall-clock performance

2        Technical Overview
We summarize the recursive-accumulation bottleneck and how Quasar removes
it. The core idea is to use polynomial partial evaluation so that ℓ instances can
be mixed while committing to only O(1) objects. We then present Quasar as the
composition of two primitives, a multi-cast reduction and a 2-to-1 reduction,
which together yield a multi-instance accumulation scheme. Finally, we outline
the resulting multi-instance IVC application.

2.1      New Accumulation Idea
We begin by recalling the accumulation bottleneck. An accumulation scheme
incrementally aggregates NP statements into a succinct accumulator such that
each step is efficiently verifiable. Most practical constructions [14,19,18,12,42,16]
rely on homomorphic commitments, which force the verifier to compute random
linear combinations of commitments. In recursive settings, these commitment
random linear combination (CRC) operations are typically circuit-inefficient.

2.2   Main Result: Multi-instance Accumulation with Constant CRC

We adopt the accumulation framework of Bünz et al. [22,17], which builds accu-
mulation schemes from interactive oracle reductions (IOR; see Section 3.3). At a
high level, the framework uses:

• a cast IOR from an NP relation R to a relation Racc , and
• a many-to-one IOR from (Racc )ℓ to Racc .

The bottleneck in prior multi-instance designs is that the many-to-one step com-
bines ℓ committed objects, forcing Θ(ℓ) in-circuit CRC operations in the recursive
verifier. Quasar shifts this combination from the folding stage to a cast stage that
takes non-committed inputs, so that the recursive verifier pays only a constant
number of CRC operations.
    To better accomodate to existing proof systems, we follow Protostar [16]
and adopt our accumulation to special-sound protocols (SPS; see Section 5.1).
The result is formalized as Main Theorem 1. We measure recursive-verifier cost
by separating (i) circuit-expensive CRC and (ii) standard processing of public
inputs. A CRC is one in-circuit operation computing a random linear combination
of two committed objects, e.g., C := r1 ·C1 +r2 ·C2 . All bounds below exclude the
unavoidable linear cost of reading and processing the ℓ public instances, which
remains linear as usual.
Theorem 1 (Composing multi-cast with 2-to-1 folding). Let ΠSPS be
a special-sound protocol for a relation R. Assume we are given: (i) a multi-cast
reduction IORcast-SPS (and its Fiat–Shamir compilation NIRcast-SPS ), and (ii) a 2-
to-1 folding reduction IORfold-SPS (and its Fiat–Shamir compilation NIRfold-SPS ),
both instantiated with an extractable PCS and compiled in the random-oracle
model. Then the construction in Section 6 yields: (a) a non-interactive argument

                                         8
NARK for ℓ statements of R, and (b) a multi-instance accumulation scheme ACC
for NARK, satisfying perfect completeness and round-by-round (RBR) knowledge
soundness in the random-oracle model. Moreover, each fold level invokes the un-
derlying batching verifier a constant number of times (independent of ℓ), and the
O(log ℓ) fold levels contribute only explicit field work (e.g., sum-check / partial
evaluation), excluding linear public-input processing. (Each batching invocation
combines two committed oracles into one; see Fig. 11.)

Proof idea. The multi-cast reduction compresses ℓ input instances into a single
accumulator instance while keeping the accumulator interface constant-size (a
constant number of oracle polynomials). The folding reduction then combines
two such accumulator instances into one without increasing that interface, since
it only requires a constant number of batching checks. Therefore, the number
of commitment-combination checks in the recursive circuit is independent of ℓ,
while the only ℓ-dependent work introduced by multi-cast is explicit field work
(sum-check / partial-evaluation), and linear public-input processing is orthogo-
nal and excluded from the metric.

Why this is not immediate. It is well-known that sum-check and partial-evaluation
tests can certify polynomial identities and batched evaluation claims. In partic-
ular, prior multi-instance designs typically incur Θ(ℓ) commitment-combination
work per step in the recursive verifier, even when their arithmetic can be orga-
nized as an MSM. Our main contribution is not the existence of these checks, but
an accumulation interface that makes them compatible with recursion at arity
ℓ. Concretely, a naı̈ve “batch ℓ instances and sum-check” approach still leaves
the recursive verifier facing Θ(ℓ) committed objects (or Θ(ℓ) commitment com-
binations) somewhere in the pipeline, so the in-circuit commitment-combination
cost does not disappear. Quasar’s multi-cast reduction is designed to output
a constant-size committed instance (a constant number of oracle polynomials)
whose verification requires only O(1) in-circuit CRC, independent of ℓ. The com-
position theorem then shows that this constant-size interface is preserved under
2-to-1 folding, so Θ(ℓ) committed objects do not reappear across recursive steps.
This interface-preservation property, together with a clean cost separation that
excludes linear public-input processing, is the structural novelty that yields con-
stant in-circuit CRC in ℓ. We further illustrate this framework in Figure 2.

2.3    Applications to IVC
We next apply Quasar to IVC. We use the notion of multi-instance IVC (formal-
ized in Definition 4), where each recursive step processes ℓ predicate instances

together with one running accumulator. This captures settings where the com-
putation remains sequential (as in standard IVC), but batching ℓ instances per
step reduces the number of recursive steps and exposes parallelism inside the
accumulation prover.
   Figure 3 depicts the resulting workflow. The prover updates the running ac-
cumulator until ℓ chunk instances have been generated, and then performs a
multi-instance accumulation step. This enables parallel execution of the accu-
mulation prover within each step, which is useful when the per-step circuit is
dominated by commitment-combination logic. It remains an open problem to
achieve a sublinear accumulation scheme for multiple accumulators (which is
required for fully general PCD).
   We emphasize that a multi-instance IVC that accumulates ℓ chunk proofs
per step is distinct from an IVC that treats one chunk of size ℓ times larger
as a single instance. In the latter, the final statement and proof typically grow
proportionally with the enlarged instance, whereas multi-instance IVC keeps the
per-chunk interface and accumulates across chunks.

tial evaluation at a random point) also appears outside accumulation, e.g., in
HydraProofs [51] for optimally computing all proofs in a vector commitment.
Quasar’s contribution is not this check in isolation, but using it to build a multi-
cast IOR that outputs a constant-commitment reduced instance, preventing Θ(ℓ)
committed objects from reappearing in subsequent folding. This structural shift
is what yields O(1) CRC operations per recursive step when composing multi-
cast with a standard 2-to-1 folding reduction.

3.3   Interactive Oracle Reductions

Interactive oracle reductions (IOR) [7,22] are information-theoretic proof systems
that generalize interactive oracle proofs to the language of reductions. An IOR
verifier may output claims about oracle strings without reading them in full.
    We consider public-coin polynomial-time IORs from an oracle relation R to
an oracle relation R′ with µ rounds of interaction. In round i ∈ [µ], the prover
sends either a standard message or an oracle string, the verifier responds with
a uniformly random challenge, and the verifier may query previously received
oracles.

Definition 1 (Interactive oracle reductions [22]). A public-coin interac-
tive oracle reduction from oracle relation R to R′ is a tuple of polynomial-time
algorithms IOR = (I, P, V) with the following syntax.

• The indexer I is deterministic. On input an index i for R, it outputs a short
  index ι, an index oracle I, and a new index i′ for R′ .
• The prover P is interactive. On input (i, x, y, w), it engages in µ rounds. In
  round i, it sends either a non-oracle message mi or an oracle πi , and then
  receives a challenge ri .
• The verifier V is interactive. On input (ι, x) and query access to y and I,
  it engages in µ rounds. In round i, it receives either mi or query access to
  πi , samples a uniformly random challenge ri from a specified challenge space
  (typically F or {0, 1}ρi for some ρi ∈ N), and sends ri . After the interaction,
  it queries I, y, and π, and then either rejects or outputs a new statement
  (x′ , y′ ).
• The prover outputs a witness w′ such that (i′ , x′ , y′ , w′ ) ∈ R′ .

     We assume perfect completeness and soundness with proximity radius δ ∗ ∈
[0, 1], as defined in Definitions 18 and 19 in Appendix D.5. To justify Fiat–
Shamir in the ROM (in particular, to rule out state-restoration attacks), we use
the stronger notion of round-by-round (RBR) knowledge soundness [8]. We refer
to Definition 21 in Appendix D.5 for formal definitions.


4     New Technique: Multi-Cast Reduction

This section presents the multi-cast reduction, the core device behind Quasar’s
constant-CRC recursion overhead. As discussed in Section 2.1, existing multi-
ple accumulation schemes [16,41,22] require the recursive verifier to compute
a random linear combination over the commitments of all ℓ input instances,
i.e., Θ(ℓ) CRC operations. In recursive circuits, CRC is often the dominant cost
for curve-based and Merkle-hash-based commitments, whereas in lattice/module
settings the dominant cost may shift. Quasar avoids Θ(ℓ) in-circuit commitment
combinations by moving the ℓ-way combination into field-only polynomial rela-
tions and outputting only a constant number of committed objects. Concretely,
our multi-cast reduction reduces the number of committed objects that must


                                       14
be combined from Θ(ℓ) to O(1), while the reduction verifier’s non-linear work
is O(log ℓ) field operations (excluding the linear-time public-input processing
discussed in Section 2.1).
    We formalize the technique as a multi-cast interactive oracle reduction in
Section 4.1. We then prove round-by-round (RBR) knowledge soundness in Sec-
tion 4.2.

6    Quasar: Accumulation for SPS

This section instantiates Quasar as a multi-instance accumulation scheme for
special-sound protocols (SPS) and proves Main Theorem 1. We first present the
composed construction that combines our multi-cast reduction with our 2-to-1
folding reduction, and state its formal guarantees as Theorem 2. We then ex-
plain how the construction is compiled under different polynomial commitment
schemes (PCS) and where the PCS choice affects the verifier and extractor. Fi-
nally, we give the concrete NARK and accumulation algorithms induced by the
two non-interactive reductions from Section 5.

We recall the syntax of an accumulation scheme (formalized in Appendix D.4).
An accumulator is a tuple acc = (acc.x, acc.w) such that (i, acc.x, acc.w) ∈ Racc
for the target accumulator relation Racc . The definition below fixes the syntax.

Definition 3 (Accumulation scheme (informal)). An accumulation scheme
ACC = (G, I, P, V, D) for a non-interactive argument NARK = (G, I, P, V) con-
sists of:

• ACC.G(1λ ), which outputs public parameters pp.
• ACC.I(pp, i), which outputs keys (apk, avk, adk).
• ACC.P(apk, {x(k) }k∈[ℓ] , π, acc(0) ), which outputs an updated accumulator acc

Where the PCS matters. In our construction, the PCS choice only affects the
instantiation of the oracle batching protocol IORbatch (Definition 2) and the re-
sulting costs of PCS.Open and PCS.Verify for the induced evaluation claims.
Quasar’s multi-cast step reduces the number of committed objects that must be
combined across recursion, so the recursive overhead is governed by the batching
interface rather than by ℓ-way commitment combinations. Accordingly, the key
requirement on the PCS instantiation is that the batching protocol is succinct
in the sense of Definition 2, i.e., its proof size and verification time are sublinear
in the underlying polynomial length.
    For homomorphic PCS, batching is direct because commitments can be lin-
early combined in the commitment group. For non-homomorphic PCS (e.g., code-
based commitments [5,35,56,57]), recent work develops succinct batching IORs
and their Fiat–Shamir instantiations [22,17]. We summarize representative in-
stantiations in Table 3, and provide additional details for code-based instantia-
tions in Appendix A.

7    Conclusion
Multi-instance recursion is attractive because it reduces the number of recursive
steps while exposing parallelism within each step. Its main practical bottleneck is
that existing designs often pay a linear number of circuit-expensive commitment-
mixing operations as the number of inputs grows. Quasar addresses this bot-
tleneck by introducing a multi-cast reduction that compresses ℓ commitment-
combination checks into a constant-size committed interface. As a result, the re-
cursive verifier incurs O(1) in-circuit CRC operations per step (i.e., commitment


                                               28
random linear combinations), while public-input processing remains a separate
linear pass as usual.This separation provides a clean cost model for reasoning
about recursion overhead in settings where commitment mixing is the dominant
in-circuit expense. We instantiate Quasar with multiple PCS backends and re-
port circuit-level evidence that the CRC layer can be made substantially cheaper
than in prior multi-instance folding baselines.
    Quasar is intentionally scoped to reduce recursion overhead inside the circuit,
rather than to eliminate the inevitable linear work of reading and validating
public inputs. When CRC is not the dominant in-circuit cost for a given backend,
the end-to-end benefit can be correspondingly smaller, and our analysis makes
this dependency explicit. A natural next direction is to integrate Quasar into
fully end-to-end recursive IVC stacks across more backends and to tighten the
constant factors in the RO and field-work components. Another direction is to
extend the multi-cast perspective to workflows that maintain multiple running
states, where interface design and accumulation structure become more subtle.
Overall, Quasar reframes multi-instance recursion around a simple principle,
namely, preserve a constant-size committed interface under composition, and
provides a concrete route to constant-CRC recursion overhead in practice.

A     Multi-instance IVC

In this section, we instantiate multi-instance IVC from our sublinear accumu-
lation scheme. We first define multi-instance IVC and its security properties.
We then sketch the standard black-box route from a NARK plus an accumula-
tion scheme to (multi-instance) IVC/PCD. Finally, we instantiate the required
components using a multilinear plonkish constraint system (HyperPlonk) and a
code-based PCS.

As stated in Theorem 3, there is a generic construction of (multi-instance) IVC

B    Related Work
Recursive compositions. Recursive composition enables proving statements
whose verification includes checking a prior proof, formalized by Bitansky et al. [10].
A recursive composition must preserve knowledge soundness, so that valid re-
cursive proofs certify an efficiently computable witness at each step [10,29,28].
It must also keep the per-step verifier work small (ideally sublinear in the state-
ment size), since otherwise the cost of verifying the composed computation grows
prohibitively with the recursion depth.

Halo-style accumulation. Halo introduced accumulation schemes and sparked
a line of practical recursive proof systems [14]. At a high level, these schemes
avoid verifying a full SNARK/NARK proof at every recursive step by maintain-
ing a running instance that represents deferred verification obligations [20,12,18].
The prover updates this running instance each step, and the recursion checks
only the local update rather than re-checking all prior proofs. A single final ver-
ification then discharges the accumulated obligations, yielding end-to-end veri-
fication comparable to a single step.

Nova-style folding. Nova introduced folding schemes as an alternative accu-
mulation paradigm for IVC [42]. Rather than accumulating already-compiled
SNARK/NARK proofs (as in Halo-style approaches, often instantiated via PCS
such as KZG [40] or Bulletproofs [15]), Nova folds underlying constraint instances
using (typically) random linear combinations. This shifts work from verifying
cryptographic proof objects to verifying lightweight consistency checks of folded
instances, reducing the dominant overhead inside the recursion. Protostar [16]
and Protogalaxy [30] further extend and systematize folding-based IVC, includ-
ing variants based on special-sound protocols [3].


                                         37
Hash-based accumulation. Recent work develops accumulation schemes whose
dominant verification reduces to hash computations rather than PCS checks. A
general construction for error-correcting codes achieves accumulation but is not
distance-preserving, with relative distance degrading from δ to δ − ϵ, and it sup-
ports only bounded-depth accumulation [21]. A subsequent construction gives
a distance-preserving accumulation scheme for Reed–Solomon codes [22]. This
approach is later extended to (classes of) locally decodable codes, yielding a
linear-time accumulation prover [17].

Holographic proofs and preprocessing SNARKs. A recurring goal in
SNARK design is to reduce online verification cost. In preprocessing SNARKs,
the verifier performs an offline phase that preprocesses the circuit into a suc-
cinct summary, and then uses this summary to verify many instances efficiently
in an online phase. This paradigm underlies several practical systems, including
Sonic [48], PLONK [33], and Spartan [52]. Marlin [27] connects preprocessing
SNARKs to the information-theoretic notion of holographic proofs [4], in which
the verifier queries an encoding of the input rather than reading it in full. This
viewpoint clarifies the role of preprocessing and query access in achieving suc-
cinct verification.

C     Implementations and Evaluations
We evaluate Quasar by measuring the cost of accumulation verification logic em-
bedded in a recursive circuit. We report circuit constraint counts, native prover
running time, and peak memory usage for the accumulation prover under vary-
ing arity. We do not report end-to-end recursive IVC performance, because our
prototype does not yet include a recursive wrapper (e.g., HyperNova) together
with an in-circuit verifier for the code-based PCS.
    All experiments are conducted on a MacBook Air (2025 model) running
macOS Sequoia 15.6, equipped with an Apple M4 chip (8 cores) and 16 GB
RAM. The codebase is compiled in --release mode using rustc version 1.92.0
with default optimization flags.
    For the curve-based polynomial commitment scheme (PCS), we use Mer-
cury PCS [31] instantiated over BN256. For the code-based PCS, we use FRI [5]
with Merkle commitments instantiated via Poseidon [36]. Our implementation
is structured to admit future linear-time PCS backends, such as Brakedown [35],
as drop-in replacements for the PCS layer.
    Due to missing toolchain components, notably (i) a recursive construction
compatible with our code-based backend and (ii) an in-circuit encoding of the
FRI verifier, we leave a full multi-instance IVC prototype as future work. In
this paper, we therefore focus on simulating the accumulation verification logic
within the circuit and quantifying its constraint footprint. To estimate constraint
counts, we use the bellpepper crate5 for circuit synthesis and constraint system
instrumentation.

Unless stated otherwise, we set the security parameter to λ = 128. We fix the
verifier polynomial degree parameter to d = 2 and vary the accumulation arity
as ℓ ∈ {2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64}. For code-based experiments, we use a Reed–Solomon
code with message length k = 28 and rate ρ = 1/16, hence codeword length
n = k/ρ = 212 .
    We evaluate prover performance under different arity settings, including: (i)
the constraint count of the recursive circuit for accumulation verification, (ii) the
running time of the accumulation prover (in seconds), and (iii) the peak memory
consumption of the accumulation prover (in MB). As a representative workload,
we use a synthetic predicate circuit that incrementally computes a hash chain.
    Figure 12a reports results for the curve-based PCS instantiation, comparing
Quasar against HyperNova under identical arity and degree settings. Across all
arities, Quasar yields lower constraint counts, lower prover time, and lower peak
memory in our prototype. The dominant source of savings is the reduced number
of in-circuit CRC operations. Concretely, for ℓ ≥ 16, CRC accounts for more than
half of the total accumulation-verification constraints in our HyperNova baseline,
while it remains below one quarter in Quasar.
    Figure 12b reports results for the code-based PCS instantiation, comparing
Quasar against Arc under identical arity and degree settings. In our measure-
ments, Arc incurs a 3–5× higher prover cost than Quasar. This gap is primarily
driven by the overhead of accumulation in code-based settings, where batching
and proximity checks dominate the recursive circuit. We view Quasar as a step
toward making accumulation practical for code-based recursive proof systems.

IOR to a non-interactive reduction NIR with compilation in two steps:

• replace every oracle in IOR with a polynomial commitment PCS to obtain
  IORPCS ;
• apply the Fiat–Shamir transform to IORPCS to obtain NIR := FS[IORPCS ].

D.6     Definitions for Special-Sound Protocols

For an N P relation R := {(x; w)}, an interactive proof for R allows a prover P
to convince a verifier V that x admits a witness w. In a (2µ − 1)-move protocol,
the prover and verifier exchange µ prover messages and µ−1 verifier challenges. If
V samples each challenge uniformly at random and independently of the prover’s
messages, the protocol is public coin. Public-coin protocols can be transformed
into non-interactive protocols via Fiat–Shamir [32].

messages (possibly empty). Each node at depth i has ai children, corresponding
to ai distinct challenges. Each transcript corresponds to a unique root-to-leaf
path.
