    SALSAA: Sumcheck-Aided Lattice-based Succinct Arguments and
                           Applications

                  Shuto Kuriyama, Russell W. F. Lai, Michał Osadnik, and Lorenzo Tucci

      Abstract. The state-of-the-art lattice-based fully-succinct argument framework of “RoK, paper, SISsors
      (RPS)” and “RoK and Roll (RnR)” [ASIACRYPT’24,’25] comprises a suite of atomically composable
      reductions of knowledge (RoK) for bounded-norm tensor-structured linear equations. While the framework
      can be used to build polynomial commitment schemes (PCS), hence succinct non-interactive arguments
      of knowledge (SNARK), the bounded norm guarantee is abstracted away from higher-level applications.
      Furthermore, RPS/RnR’s lazy batching RoK only reduces the proof size but not time, and their
      (ℓ2 -)norm-check RoK has a Oλ (m log m)-time prover, a concrete bottleneck in applications.
      We present SALSAA, which incorporates the sumcheck protocol into the RPS/RnR framework to addi-
      tionally support verifying sumcheck claims over bounded-norm witnesses of structured linear relations.
      This grants the flexibility of sumchecks, exposes the norm guarantee to higher-level applications and,
      perhaps counter-intuitively, leads to efficiency improvements in the framework itself. We use SALSAA
      to construct the following: (i) An improved (ℓ2 -)norm-check RoK with an Oλ (m)-time prover, and an
      improved batching RoK that also reduces prover time. These combine to yield a concretely efficient
      SNARK for structured linear relations and a PCS, with half the proof size of RPS and verification in
      under 7 ms for witnesses of up to 230 Zq elements. (ii) Improved RoKs for checking binariness and
      staircase-structured linear relations, which in turn yield a more practical lattice-based verifiable delay
      function (VDF). (iii) A folding scheme for bounded-ℓ2 -norm (semi-)structured linear relations, with
      sub-millisecond verification and proofs of roughly 60 KB per folding step. (iv) A RoK for the Algebraic
      Intermediate Representation (AIR) relation which, through (i) and (iii), yields a lattice-based succinct
      argument and a folding scheme for AIR.
      Finally, we provide modular implementations of (i)–(iii) in one codebase relying on AVX-512-accelerated
      cyclotomic ring arithmetic.

      Keywords: post-quantum cryptography, lattice-based cryptography, succinct arguments, sumcheck,
      folding schemes, verifiable delay functions



1   Introduction

Succinct argument systems [Kil92,Mic94] are cryptographic protocols that enable a prover to convince a
verifier of the validity of a mathematical statement with communication much shorter than the size of the
statement. Unlike group-based constructions (e.g. [PHGR13,Gro16,BBB+ 18,CHM+ 20,KZG10]), lattice-based
argument systems promise post-quantum security and exploit rich algebraic structures that may lead to greater
efficiency and expressiveness compared to hash-based approaches (e.g. [BBHR18,BCR+ 19,GLS+ 23,AHIV23]).
Furthermore, lattice-based succinct arguments can be used to natively prove relations that arise from lattice-
based cryptographic constructions, which are predominant in the post-quantum setting, without requiring
translations between mathematical structures that induce extra overhead. This work contributes to the
ongoing effort to realise the latter potential in practice.
     Succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (SNARKs) are a central component in application
scenarios requiring efficient verification, such as verifiable delegation of computation [KP16,Lee21,GSW23],
verifiable fully homomorphic encryption [GBK+ 24,ABPS24,LLZ+ 25,ZLW+ 25], aggregate signatures [BK20,
JRS23,JRS24,AAB+ 24,DKLW25], and verifiable delay functions [LM23,OKC+ 25]. Folding schemes [KST22,
BC25a,BC25b,FKNP24,NS25,GLLO26] are closely related to SNARKs and allow a prover to fold multiple
instances of a relation into fewer ones, which leads to constructions of incrementally verifiable computation
(IVC) [Val08] and proof-carrying data (PCD) [CT10].
 1.1   Lattice-based Succinct Arguments

Lattice-based succinct arguments can be divided into semi- and fully-succinct systems. Semi-succinct systems
such as LaBRADOR [BS23] and Greyhound [NS24] already achieve polylogarithmic-size proofs, but their ver-
ifier work remains super-logarithmic. Fully-succinct systems [CLM23,AFLN24,CMNW24,KLNO24,KLNO25]
feature polylogarithmic-time verifiers. The frameworks of “RoK, paper, SISsors” (RPS) [KLNO24] and “RoK
and Roll” (RnR) [KLNO25] are the most practically relevant in the fully-succinct line.
     The RPS/RnR line of work provides a modular framework, described in the language of reductions of
knowledge (RoKs), for constructing succinct arguments. A RoK generalises a traditional proof system: It
allows a prover and a verifier to interactively reduce an instance of a starting relation to an instance of an
often simpler target relation. The RPS/RnR framework in particular provides RoKs between bounded-norm
structured linear relations Ξ lin-⊗ with different parameters. For example, folding reduces the witness dimension,
norm checking reduces an exact norm claim into a relaxed norm claim, and batching reduces the number of
constraints. These atomic RoKs compose into succinct arguments with different trade-offs, which can further
be turned non-interactive using the Fiat-Shamir transform. The modularity makes it possible to examine and
remove individual bottlenecks without rebuilding the rest of the framework.
     The RPS/RnR framework suffers from one main bottleneck: a lack of expressiveness. The only supported
relation is the structured linear relation Ξ lin-⊗ , which captures inner products between the witness and public
vectors, where the public vectors must be tensor-structured. Although a SNARK for Ξ lin-⊗ immediately
gives a polynomial commitment scheme (PCS), through which one can compile polynomial interactive
oracle proofs (PIOP) to obtain SNARKs for various expressive relations, such as Algebraic Intermediate
Representations (AIR) or Rank-1 Constraint Systems (R1CS), the compilation abstracts away the norm bound
guarantee of the RPS/RnR framework which is potentially useful for higher-level applications. Indeed, relations
expressed as bounded-norm satisfiability of polynomial equations naturally arise in lattice-based cryptographic
constructions, such as verifiable FHE, proof of correct encryption/decryption or aggregating lattice-based
signatures. Expressing these bounded norm constraints via AIR/R1CS/etc. would be highly inefficient. This
overhead is in principle unnecessary if the lattice-based RPS/RnR framework is used as the backend.
     Somewhat counter-intuitively, the limitation also affects the internals of the framework. In particular,
the norm-check RoK of RPS/RnR is a key component for solving the soundness gap problem common in
lattice-based arguments. Since only a linear Ξ lin-⊗ interface is exposed, the norm check is proven via an
auxiliary commitment to the convolution of the witness with itself, which takes Oλ (m log m) ring operations
for a m-dimensional witness and dominates the prover’s cost. This cost is inherited by applications built on
the framework, such as the verifiable delay function Papercraft [OKC+ 25].


 1.2   Our Contributions

We identify the above as an interfacing problem between the RPS/RnR framework and higher-level applications.
To solve it, we extend the framework to expose a compatible sumcheck relation Ξ sum as a reduction target for
other RoKs, by integrating the machinery of the sumcheck protocol [LFKN90]. An Ξ sum instance is simply
an Ξ lin-⊗ instance together with a list of sumcheck claims, which can encode a wide range of non-linear
relations. The technical core of our work is a sumcheck RoK that reduces Ξ sum to a multilinear extension
(MLE) relation Ξ mle , which in turn reduces to Ξ lin-⊗ , all with the prover performing Oλ (m) ring operations.
Our sumcheck is run directly over cyclotomic ring elements rather than over their coefficients, which avoids
the large modulus or extension field that a coefficient-wise sumcheck [BC25a,NS25,GLLO26] would require.
Equipped with a RoK from Ξ sum to Ξ lin-⊗ , we obtain a suite of new RoKs from various relations to Ξ lin-⊗ :

  (i) An improved norm-check RoK with a Oλ (m)-time prover in ring operations. It is a drop-in replacement
      for the Oλ (m log m)-time norm-check of RPS [KLNO24].
 (ii) An improved batching RoK, which compresses the many constraints of a Ξ lin-⊗ instance into a single one.
      This improves upon the “lazy batching” RoK of [KLNO24] which reduces only the proof size but not the
      prover time.
(iii) A binariness RoK, which certifies that the witness of a Ξ lin-⊗ instance is binary. This replaces the binariness
      RoK in RPS/Papercraft which inherits the Oλ (m log m) time cost from the norm-check of RPS and which
      requires the witness to live in a “tensor subring”.


                                                          2
 (iv) A staircase RoK, which supports constraints represented by block-bidiagonal matrices, i.e. two blocks
      repeating along the diagonal, as arises when the witness is the trace of a computation repeating the same
      step. Compared with its analogue in Papercraft, our staircase RoK is more flexible in that it decouples the
      block width of the block-bidiagonal matrix from the tensor-structure of the underlying Ξ lin-⊗ instance.
  (v) An “Algebraic Intermediate Representation” (AIR [BBHR19,Sta21]) RoK, which asserts that a Ξ lin-⊗
      witness satisfies some given AIR arithmetisation constraints. There, the witness is the execution trace of
      a computation, a low-degree transition function constrains every pair of consecutive rows of the trace,
      and boundary conditions fix the initial and final states. This RoK interfaces the framework with the
      arithmetisation emitted by standard SNARK front ends.

  Each of these RoKs reduces to Ξ sum with different sumcheck claims and ultimately to Ξ lin-⊗ , the last two
  steps being shared by all of them:

     By virtue of the modularity of the RPS/RnR framework, composing the above new RoKs enables new
  applications. We construct some of them below:

  (vi) Improved SNARKs for the structured linear relation Ξ lin-⊗ , which immediately gives improved polynomial
       commitment schemes (PCS). The PCS is immediate because a commitment to the witness doubles as
       a commitment to its multilinear extension, so an evaluation claim is an instance of Ξ mle . These take
       advantage of the new norm-check and batching RoKs.
 (vii) Improved verifiable delay functions (VDF), following the blueprint of [OKC+ 25]. In brief, the execution
       trace of the delay function can be expressed as the binary-satisfiability of a structured linear equation
       represented by a block-bidiagonal matrix. The improved staircase and binariness RoKs reduce this to
       Ξ lin-⊗ where the SNARK in item (vi) applies.
(viii) Folding schemes for bounded-ℓ2 -norm semi-structured linear relation Ξ lin- , which differs from Ξ lin-⊗ in
       that it admits unstructured commitment keys.
  (ix) By composing the AIR RoK with a SNARK for Ξ lin-⊗ or a folding scheme for Ξ lin- , we obtain a
       SNARK/folding scheme for AIR, respectively.

  To demonstrate practicality, we implement applications (vi)–(viii) above and report their concrete performance
  in Section 8. For instance, our implementations offer the most efficient verification of any reported lattice-
  based SNARK construction to our knowledge, at 5.64 ms for a witness of 228 Zq elements, even faster than
  RoKoko [KLN+ 26], which in exchange achieves smaller proofs and a faster prover. The VDF verifies in roughly
  10 ms, two to three orders of magnitude below the delay itself for considered parameters, while proving costs
  a constant multiple of the delay. One folding step verifies in under half a millisecond and communicates about
  60 KB, independently of the witness size.


  1.3   Related Work

 The line of works most relevant to SALSAA is initiated in [CLM23] and refined in RPS [KLNO24] and
 RnR [KLNO25]. These schemes rely on the vSIS assumption, a structured variant of standard SIS, to achieve
 polylogarithmic verifier time.
     The sumcheck protocol [LFKN90] is by now a standard tool for constructing succinct arguments [CHM+ 20,
 Set20,CBBZ23,XZZ+ 19,Che25], especially those obtained by compiling a polynomial interactive oracle proof
 (PIOP) with a polynomial commitment scheme (PCS) [XZZ+ 19,BCG+ 19,GWC19,BFS20,CHM+ 20,Set20,
 BCHO22,CBBZ23]. Our use is different: rather than compiling a standalone PIOP with a polynomial
 commitment scheme, we integrate sumcheck into the RPS/RnR framework, and we run it directly over
 cyclotomic ring elements rather than over their coefficients. Such a sumcheck is more concretely efficient
 and yields a similar communication cost thanks to batching over NTT (number-theoretic transform) slots as
 observed in [BC25b].
     For lattice-based folding schemes, a linear-time prover and expressiveness are typically obtained via
 sumchecks [BC25a,BC25b,NS25,GLLO26]. In none of these works, however, is the sumcheck exposed as a
 relation in its own right that other reductions can target: it is applied, in a rather ad hoc manner, inside a


                                                           3
monolithic protocol for one fixed arithmetisation. Most of these works also share a common template: first
reduce a CCS/R1CS instance to a linear relation, then fold several instances of that linear relation into fewer
ones.1 We contribute to both steps. For the first, our AIR RoK reduces AIR constraints to Ξ lin directly, without
routing them through CCS. For the second, we build an efficient folding scheme for Ξ lin itself by composing
our improved norm-check and batching RoKs with those of RPS/RnR.
    The Algebraic Intermediate Representation (AIR) [BBHR19] originated from the hash-based STARK
line [BBHR18,Sta21]. AIR is particularly useful when verifier succinctness is a priority, as the instance descrip-
tion is as small as the description of the transition function. Other popular arithmetisation languages include
rank-1 constraint systems (R1CS) [GGPR13,BCG+ 13], Plonkish arithmetisation [GWC19], and customisable
constraint systems (CCS) [STW23], the last of which can be instantiated to recover R1CS, Plonkish and AIR
as special cases. In R1CS and CCS the dimensions of the constraint matrices are as large as the computation
description, although the matrices are typically sparse. Proof systems that do not exploit this sparsity typically
require the verifier to preprocess the matrices to achieve succinct verification.
    Lattice-based arguments and folding schemes that support a general arithmetisation have so far targeted
CCS or R1CS [BC25a,BC25b,NS25,GLLO26], which leaves AIR constraints reachable only by embedding AIR
into CCS, reintroducing the large matrices and the verifier cost that AIR avoids. To our knowledge, our AIR
RoK is the first direct treatment of AIR constraints in a lattice-based argument system.


1.4   Subsequent Work

The subsequent work RoKoko [KLN+ 26] adopts and extends our integration of sumcheck into the RPS/RnR
framework: each round alternates between generalisations of Ξ lin-⊗ and Ξ sum , where the latter expresses all
constraints–linear constraints, well-formedness of recursive commitments, exact norm check, etc.–as sumcheck
claims. While SALSAA optimises verification time, RoKoko trades a slight increase in verification for a significant
reduction in proof size and prover time. Achieving the best of both is an interesting open problem. Furthermore,
while RoKoko focuses on improving the underlying argument system, a core part of SALSAA is to interface
various applications, which is transferable to RoKoko.

2.1   A Toolkit of Sumcheck(-aided) RoKs

The framework of [KLNO24,KLNO25] exposes a single interface, the linear relation Ξ lin-⊗ , to higher-level
applications, and using it as a PCS to compile a PIOP drops the handle on the norm bound that the framework
maintains internally. We want instead a Ξ sum relation that carries non-linear constraints on the very same
committed, bounded-norm witness, so that a higher-level application can express bounded-norm satisfiability
of non-linear relations.

over the hypercube of its multilinear extension (MLE) MLE[w] ∈ Rq [x0 , . . . , xµ−1 ], the unique multilinear
polynomial s.t.

Algebraic Intermediate Representation (AIR) constraints. Finally, our most advanced RoK, Π air
(Section 4.7), proves that the witness W satisfies an AIR constraint, where W is the trace of some computation
and the constraint is a set of polynomial equations on the trace. In more detail, each row of W is the state of
the computation at some time step and the columns of W correspond to the registers of the computation,
while the AIR constraint is a transition function:

                                    ∀i ∈ [m − 1] f (Wi,: , Wi+1,: ) = 0 mod q.

On top of that, we also require the witness to satisfy certain boundary conditions, which are used to specify
the initial and final states of the computation.
   The transition function couples two consecutive rows of W, whereas the sumcheck interface accepts claims
about one row at a time. We therefore require the witness to be committed together with its “shifted” copy,
obtained by moving every row of W up by one and wrapping the first row to the bottom. Every row of the
concatenated matrix [W, shift(W)] but the last then holds a pair of adjacent trace rows, since the wrap pairs
the final trace row with the first one. The transition therefore becomes a condition on a single row, imposed
on all rows but the last, and fits the interface, as depicted in Fig. 1. Two further sumcheck claims complete
the check, one verifying that the second half of the concatenation really is the shifted copy of the first, and
one for the boundary conditions.

2.2   Applications
Composing the RoKs above with those inherited from [KLNO24,KLNO25] yields the applications developed
in Sections 5 to 7: an argument system Π as for Ξ lin-⊗ together with the polynomial commitment scheme that
follows from it, a verifiable delay function following the blueprint of [OKC+ 25], and a folding scheme Π fs for
the semi-structured linear relation Ξ lin- . Composing Π air with Π as gives a SNARK for AIR (Corollary 5.3),
and composing it with Π fs gives a folding scheme for AIR (Section 7.4). We implement all but the two AIR
compositions, and Section 8 reports the measurements.

3.1   Algebraic Number Theory

Cyclotomic Fields. Throughout this work, we let K = Q(ζ) be a cyclotomic field with conductor f of degree
φ = φ(f), where ζ is a root of unity of order f and φ is Euler’s totient function, and OK = Z[ζ] be its ring
of integers. For a modulus q ∈ N, we write Rq := R/qR. We always assume that q is a prime number. We
denote by R× and R×      q the sets of units in R and Rq respectively. We endow R with a geometry via the
coefficient embedding cf : K → Qφ and P   the canonical embedding σ : K → Cφ . Specifically, for a given Z-basis
b = (bi )i∈[φ] of R and an element x = i∈[φ] xi bi ∈ R, we write cfb (x) := (xi )i∈[φ] and σ(x) := (σj (x))j∈[φ] ,
where σj ∈ Gal(K/Q). We extend cf and σ to vectors by concatenation, i.e. if x = (xi )i∈[m] ∈ Rm , then
cf(x) := (cf(xi ))i∈[m] and σ(x) := (σ(xi ))i∈[m] .

the norm is defined as ∥M∥σ,p = maxi∈[n] ∥mi ∥σ,p , where mi is the i-th column of the matrix. Note that

Reduction of Knowledge We recall the notion of reduction of knowledge (RoK) and the principal relation
considered in the framework of “RoK, paper, SISsors (RPS)” [KLNO24] and “RoK and Roll (RnR)” [KLNO25].
Definition 3.8 (Reduction of Knowledge (adapted from [KLNO24,OKC+ 25])). A reduction of
knowledge (RoK) Π is defined by two interactive PPT algorithms Π = (P, V), the prover P, and the verifier
V, with the following interface:
  – P(pp, stmt0 , wit0 ) → (stmt1 , wit1 ): Interactively reduce the input instance (pp, stmt0 , wit0 ) to a new
    instance (pp, stmt1 , wit1 ) or ⊥.
  – V(pp, stmt0 ) → stmt1 : Interactively reduce the task of checking the input instance (pp, stmt0 ) to checking
    a new instance (pp, stmt1 ).
We write ⟨P, V⟩ (pp, stmt0 , wit0 ) → (stmt1 , wit1 ) for the interaction of P on input (pp, stmt0 , wit0 ) with V on
input (pp, stmt0 ), whose output consists of the statement stmt1 returned by V and the witness wit1 returned by
P, and write ⟨P ∗ , V⟩ (pp, stmt0 ) → (stmt1 , wit1 ) for the same interaction with a malicious prover P ∗ in place
of P. Let Ξ0 , Ξ0′ , Ξ1 , Ξ1′ be relations. A RoK Π is correct for Ξ0 → Ξ1 with correctness error δ := δ(pp, stmt0 )
if, for every (pp, stmt0 , wit0 ) ∈ Ξ0 ,
             Pr [(pp, stmt1 , wit1 ) ∈ Ξ1 |(stmt1 , wit1 ) ← ⟨P, V⟩ (pp, stmt0 , wit0 )] ≥ 1 − δ(pp, stmt0 ),
the probability being over the randomness of P and V. The correctness error is taken as δ = 0 if omitted.
If a RoK Π is correct for Ξ0 → Ξ1 , we say that it is a RoK from Ξ0 to Ξ1 , short Π : Ξ0 → Ξ1 . A RoK
Π is knowledge sound for Ξ0′ ← Ξ1′ with knowledge error κ := κ(pp, stmt0 ) if there is a black-box expected
polynomial-time extractor E such that, for every (pp, stmt0 ) and every unbounded malicious prover P ∗ ,

    We write Ξin → Ξout for a RoK that reduces the relation Ξin to the relation Ξout ; completeness statements
are phrased in this left-to-right direction. Knowledge-soundness statements instead use the reverse arrow:
Ξext ← Ξout reads right-to-left and denotes the extraction direction – from an accepting instance of the output

means that, given a valid instance of Ξ lin , the extractor outputs a witness for either Ξ sis or Ξ lin .

 8      Experiments
 In this section, we present the experimental results of our implementation of SALSAA. We evaluate the
 performance of all applications described in Sections 5 to 7.

 8.1     Design Choices and Experimental Setup
 For the most optimal performance, we choose to work with power-of-two cyclotomic rings R = Z[ζf ] where
 f = 2k for some integer k. Power-of-two cyclotomic rings admit efficient arithmetic using the Number-Theoretic
 Transform (NTT). We assume that the cyclotomic ring “almost splits” modulo q, i.e. Rq ∼       = (Fq2 )φ/2 . Using
 this ring allows us to use cyclotomic ring elements with binary coefficients as challenges for Π fold by applying
 the analysis from [GLLO26, appendix B.2]. Using “almost-splitting” cyclotomic rings allows us to use Fqe
 (with e = 2) as a challenge set for sumcheck and conveniently batch across the φ/e CRT/NTT slots. We
 select modulus q ≈ 250 so that the operations’ runtime is further improved using AVX-512-IFMA instructions
 available on modern Intel processors. We operate over the f-th cyclotomic ring for f = 256 (i.e. φ = 128),
 observing that the higher degree typically implies better performance, in exchange for larger proof sizes.

 Ring arithmetic. Our implementation of SALSAA relies heavily on efficient cyclotomic ring arithmetic, which
 is crucial for the performance of our protocols. We rely on the incomplete-rexl3 library developed for
 efficient multiplication in “almost-splitting” cyclotomic rings. It provides a Rust implementation of a subset
 of operations supported by Intel HEXL [BKS+ 21] and is likewise optimised for the AVX-512 instruction set.

Sumchecks. To improve concrete efficiency, we batch multiple sumcheck instances into a single protocol. In

subprotocols. Steps involving, for example, Π batch , Π norm , Π bin+ , or Π + require multiple sumcheck instances.
Our batched design executes all of them together, reducing communication and improving prover performance.

Projection arithmetic. For random projections, i.e. RoKs Π ⊗RP and Π RP [KLNO25], we rely on highly-efficient
intrinsics from the AVX-512 instruction set to implement the required matrix-vector multiplications, taking
advantage of the structure of the projection matrices (for both prover and verifier side).

Platform and portability. Due to the level of optimisations, we focused on Intel microarchitectures with
AVX-512 support. However, our implementation can be executed on any microarchitecture supported by
Rust, albeit with a significant performance degradation.

Non-interactivity and testing. To obtain non-interactive arguments, we implement the Fiat-Shamir transform
using the BLAKE3 hash function [OANWO20]. Our implementation is prepared in both single-threaded and
multi-threaded versions, allowing us to evaluate the performance of both.

8.2   Parameters selection
We select parameters to achieve λ = 128 security against known attacks listed in Lattice Estimator [APS15].
Our codebase is equipped with an “estimator” mode, which allows us to easily experiment with different
parameters. Prover, while executed in the “estimator” mode, outputs the estimated security level, accounting
for the norm growth during the extraction process. For statistical soundness, we set overall soundness error
κ ≈ 2−100 (mainly impacted by the size of NTT slots) following the industry standards.

Remark 8.1. Our implementation targets single-threaded performance. This is motivated by the protocol’s
highly interactive structure and its Fiat-Shamir compilation, which imposes sequential execution due to
synchronisation points used to derive challenges from prior-round transcript hashes. Preliminary within-
round parallelisation (between synchronisation points) indicates speedups of 3× for the prover and 10× for
commitment, using 16 threads on the machine in Table 2. Verifier parallelisation has not been attempted.


VDF We compare our VDF construction (overview in Section 6, full construction in Section 6.2) against
Papercraft [OKC+ 25], the current state-of-the-art post-quantum VDF. Table 4 reports prover, verifier,
communication, and per-step delay-function costs for delay inputs of size 228 , 230 , and 231 Zq -elements.
    We follow the parameter choice of Section 6.2, with two deliberate decisions. First, we instantiate the
delay-function SIS instance SIS∞ R,q,m ,n ,1 to be over-secure, so that a single delay step yields a meaningful
delay. Second, we fix the per-step delay granularity at 200 µs: a finer granularity would expose the inner loop
to multi-threading, which is undesirable for a delay function.
    For a VDF, the most meaningful comparison metrics are the ratios of delay time to verification time and
to proving time, since they capture the overhead the SNARK adds on top of the unavoidable sequential work.
    The Papercraft row in Table 4 is reproduced from [OKC+ 25] and serves only as a remote reference point.
The delay function of Papercraft is identical to ours, but its implementation does not exploit AVX-512,
NTT-friendly arithmetic, or cache-aware data layouts. Consequently it reports a per-step delay cost roughly
five times higher than ours (≈ 0.9 ms vs. ≈ 0.17 ms). The reported Papercraft delay times are therefore not
directly meaningful and could be brought close to ours by porting our delay-function implementation. For the
same reason, Remark 8.1 does not apply to Papercraft, so the parallelisation speedups it reports are inflated
relative to what a comparably optimised baseline would see, for both prover and verifier.

Folding scheme We present the performance of our folding scheme (Corollary 7.5) in Table 5. We do not
compare with other folding schemes as the only efficient implementation we are aware of is one of [BC24]
presented in [Net24], which reports the complete end-to-end performance of the R1CS folding scheme. Our
folding scheme is designed for the linear relation Ξ lin- and further extended to AIR in Section 7.4. Fully
implementing the folding scheme for AIR is a significant undertaking. We therefore leave it as an open
direction.

of RnR [KLNO25]. RoKoko generates most of its proof after a strongly compressing first round, so its proof
size is essentially independent of the witness size, while its verification remains slower than ours throughout.
Greyhound shows the semi-succinct profile, with verification time growing with the square root of the witness
size. Among all baselines only WHIR verifies faster, at the price of a prover several times slower than ours
and, as for all hash-based systems, without native support for bounded-norm lattice statements.
    Our VDF prover runs at a constant multiple of the delay itself, roughly 80 including commitment, as both
are linear in the input, and verification never exceeds half a percent of the delay. The per-step delay cost is
constant across sizes, so the delay grows purely through the step count. The nominal ratio of Papercraft is
lower only because it is measured against its five times slower delay steps. Normalised to our per-step cost it
is close to three times ours, its verification is slower by more than two orders of magnitude, and its proofs are
larger by more than one.
    For the folding scheme, verification time and proof size are essentially independent of the witness size: the
accumulator and the folding messages have fixed dimensions, and only the sumcheck rounds contribute a
logarithmic term. The folding prover is linear in the witness and costs about a seventh of the SNARK prover
at equal size, since one folding step runs a constant number of RoKs rather than the full composition.
