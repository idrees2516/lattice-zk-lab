         Abstract. This work introduces HyperWolf, a Hypercube-Wise Opti-
         mized polynomial commitment scheme based on Lattices over ring Fields.
         The scheme achieves succinctness with O(log N ) proof size and verifier
         time, along with linear prover cost. It supports both univariate and mul-
         tilinear polynomials under a unified framework. Inspired by the two-
         dimensional tensor structure employed in [GLS+ 21] to achieve sublinear
         efficiency, we generalize the idea to a k-dimensional tensor (hypercube)
         structure and design a k-round recursive proof protocol, where each
         round performs a dimensionality reduction, which results in an over-
         all efficiency of O(kN 1/k ). By setting k = log N , our scheme achieves
         near-optimal asymptotic performance.
         HyperWolf is fully transparent and relies only on the standard lattice
         assumption over rings. In terms of concrete efficiency, for polynomials
         with N = 225 coefficients, our scheme yields proof sizes that are 8×
         smaller than the lattice-based scheme of [CMNW24] (Crypto24), and
         over 200× smaller than SLAP [AFLN24] (Eurocrypt24). Compared to
         Greyhound [NS24] (Crypto24), our proof size is of similar magnitude,
         while achieving significantly lower verifier time.


Keywords: Lattice-based cryptography, Polynomial Commitment Scheme


1      Introduction

A Polynomial Commitment Scheme (PCS) is a cryptographic primitive that en-
ables a prover to commit to a polynomial f —which may be univariate [KZG10]
or multilinear [GLS+ 21], [ZCF24]—by publishing a compact commitment. Later,
for given public inputs x (or ⃗x in the multilinear case) and y, the prover can
generate a succinct proof π attesting that y = f (x) or f (⃗x). For practical
efficiency, the proof size must be succinct and the verification cost must be
efficient (i.e. less than sublinear in the input size). PCS are widely used in
cryptographic protocols, enabling applications such as verifiable secret sharing

nisms. Their most prominent application lies in constructing zero-knowledge suc-
cinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (zkSNARKs) [BFS20], [CHM+ 20],
[CBBZ23], [BSBHR18b], where they serve as a fundamental primitive.
    Significant research efforts have been made on PCS, however, the majority of
existing schemes depend on discrete logarithm problem assumptions [BBB+ 18],
[KZG10], [Lee21], which are vulnerable to quantum computing attacks. Although
post-quantum secure alternatives have been developed - notably those employing
error-correcting codes [BSBHR18a], [GLS+ 21] - these solutions typically com-
promise on homomorphic properties, which are essential for supporting efficient
proof composition in advanced cryptographic protocols.
    Lattice-based cryptography has emerged as a particularly promising ap-
proach for constructing quantum-resistant primitives, owing to its computa-
tional efficiency, natural homomorphic capabilities, and proven security against
quantum attacks. Despite these advantages, the development of lattice-based
polynomial commitment schemes has progressed slowly, mainly due to funda-
mental difficulties in achieving exact extractability guarantees. While recent
work has begun to address this gap, existing solutions inevitably introduce
substantial compromises. Some proposals depend on non-standard lattice as-
sumptions [WW23a], [AFLN24], whose long-term security against quantum ad-
versaries remains uncertain. Others require trusted setup procedures [HSS24],
undermining their practical applicability. Although certain constructions [NS24]
and [CMNW24] maintain transparent setup properties, they are respectively
constrained by their restriction to univariate polynomials and the computational
overhead associated with integer field operations. These limitations collectively
underscore the importance of continued research to advance lattice-based poly-
nomial commitment schemes.



1.1   Related Works


According to the underlying construction frameworks, the existing lattice-based
polynomial commitment schemes can be broadly classified into two categories:
    Constructions Based on Inner-Product Relations. These schemes
interpret the polynomial evaluation process f (x) = y as an inner-product rela-
tion ⟨f⃗, ⃗x⟩, where ⃗x = (1, x, . . . , xN −1 ) in the univariate case [ACL+ 22, dCP23,
WW23a, CLM23]. They leverage the Bulletproofs protocol [BBB+ 18] to gener-
ate succinct proofs. However, the verification cost is linear to the polynomial
degree N because the evaluation point cannot be chosen in advance to ensure
soundness. Recent works [BCS23, FLV23] address this issue by integrating the
Dory-based delegation protocol [Lee21] or introduced more structured commit-
ment scheme for fast verification, though they still necessitate a preprocessing
phase. Furthermore, most constructions do not guarantee extractability. Only a
few schemes [FLV23, ACL+ 22, CLM23] achieve this property, but they depend

                                           2
on non-standard lattice assumptions, such as the k-M-SIS assumption, which is
susceptible to being broken. 1
    Constructions Based on Recursive Approaches. Another approach to
constructing lattice-based polynomial evaluation schemes utilizes the recursive
computational framework. [FMN24] was the first to introduce the split-and-fold
technique from [BSBHR18a] into the lattice setting, enabling fast verification.
This advancement, however, came at the cost of relying on the new PowerBASIS
assumption, a variant of the BASIS assumption proposed in [WW23b], and re-
quired a large common reference string (CRS) of size quadratic in the input size.
A subsequent work, SLAP [AFLN24], addressed these limitations by reducing
the size of the CRS and mitigating the reliance on non-standard assumptions.
While this refinement improved efficiency, it introduced a quasi-linear prover
cost and still required a trusted setup.
    Most recently, Nguyen et al. [NS24] introduced Greyhound, a transparent
univariate polynomial commitment scheme building upon the Labrador proto-
col [BS23]. Their construction employs recursive techniques to achieve compact
proof sizes while incorporating bi-variate sum-check technique to enable sub-
linear verification time under standard lattice assumptions. Nevertheless, this
approach maintains two key limitations: it only supports univariate polynomi-
als and specifically operates under infinity norm bounds. Concurrently, Cini et
al. [CMNW24] presented an SIS-based polynomial commitment scheme with a
transparent setup that accommodates both univariate and multilinear polynomi-
als. While this construction achieves broader functionality, it incurs substantial
computational overhead due to its reliance on integer field arithmetic.
    Building on the discussion above, this work explores the following research
question:
    Can we design a general PCS for univariate and multilinear polynomials,
based on standard lattice assumptions, that supports a transparent setup, nearly
optimal (logarithmic level) proof size and verification time, and linear prover
time?


1.2    Our Contributions

We answer the above question in the affirmative by introducing HyperWolf, a
novel and efficient lattice-based proof system that achieves all the desired prop-
erties. We present three main contributions, each of independent interest.
    Unified Framework. First, we identify and formalize a common algebraic
structure shared by univariate and multilinear polynomial evaluations, and pro-
pose a unified framework that accommodates both cases. This framework is
flexibly parameterized, allowing it to specialize to either setting through the
adjustment of auxiliary parameters.
    Recursive-Friendly Proof System. Our second contribution is the design
of a proof system works in recursive manner for the unified framework. This

construction is inspired by the “bivariate” paradigm, which has been widely em-
ployed in prior works such as [NS24], [GLS+ 21], and [EG25] to achieve sublinear
proof sizes and verifier time. The core idea behind these schemes is to reinterpret
the witness—specifically, the coefficient vector f⃗ in a PCS—as a vector of length
N , and express the proof relation as an inner product ⟨f⃗, ⃗x⟩ = y. By reshaping
f⃗ into an n × n matrix with n2 = N , the original relation can be decomposed
to two sub-relations: first, an inner product between a length-n vector and an
n × n matrix; second, an inner product between two vectors of length n, which
can then be proved using a bivariate sumcheck protocol. Each sub-proof yields
an O(n)-size proof and supports O(n)-time verification, resulting in an overall
sublinear proof system that maintains completeness and soundness.
    In our proposed scheme, we generalize and extend this idea by reshaping
a length-N witness into a k-dimensional hypercube structure, and designing
a k-round recursive proof system where each round performs a reduction of
knowledge from a high-dimensional relation to one with dimension decreased
by one. Crucially, both the proof size and verification time in each round are
bounded by O(N 1/k ). After completing all k rounds, the total cost accumulates
to O(k · N 1/k ). Notably, by optimizing the parameter choice to k = log N , both
the proof size and verifier time can be reduced to O(log N ), achieving near-
optimal asymptotic efficiency. Furthermore, our scheme maintains linear prover
complexity. A comprehensive performance comparison between our scheme and

2.3   Interactive Proofs and Polynomial Commitment Schemes
Let R ⊆ {0, 1}∗ × {0, 1}∗ × {0, 1}∗ be a ternary relation. For (pp, x, w) ∈ R, we
denote that pp as the public parameters, x as a statement and w as a witness
for x. For fixed pp and x, we define the set R(pp, x) := {w : (pp, x, w) ∈ R}.
Definition 3. (Interactive Proof System) An interactive proof system Π =
(S, P, V) for the relation R is an interactive protocol that consists of the prob-
abilistic polynomial-time (PPT) setup algorithm S and a protocol between an
interactive PPT prover P and an interactive verifier V. This system enables
the prover to convince the verifier of the existence of a witness w for a public
statement x. Both P and V receive the statement x and the public parameters
pp generated by the setup algorithm S as public input. Additionally, P has pri-
vate input in the form of a witness w ∈ R(pp, x). At the end of the protocol,
V either accepts or rejects the prover’s claim of knowing a witness w such that
(pp, x, w) ∈ R. Consequently, we classify the corresponding transcript (i.e., the
collection of all messages exchanged during the protocol execution) as either ac-
cepting or rejecting.

Definition 10. (CWSS for Multi-Round Protocols) Let Π = (S, P, V) be
public-coin (2µ + 1)-round interactive proof system for relation R, where in each
round the challenge space of V is defined as C = S ℓ . A tree of transcripts is a
set of K = (ℓ(k − 1) + 1)µ arranged in the tree structure: The nodes in the tree
correspond to the prover’s messages and the edges correspond to the verifier’s
challenges. Each node at depth i has exactly ℓ(k − 1) + 1 children corresponding
to ℓ(k − 1) + 1 distinct challenges which, as a set of vectors, lie in SS(S, ℓ, k).
Every transcript corresponds to exactly one path from the root to a leaf node.
We say that Π is ℓ-coordinate-wise k-special sound if there is a polynomial time
algorithm that, given a statement x and the tree of transcripts, outputs a witness
w ∈ R(i, x).

into a general relation. We then design a proof system for this relation, which, by
instantiating appropriate parameters, yields concrete polynomial commitment
schemes for both cases.
