        Abstract. We present RoKoko, a new lattice-based succinct argument system that achieves a linear-
        time prover alongside polylogarithmic communication and verifier complexity. Asymptotically, our
        construction improves upon RoK and Roll (ASIACRYPT 2025), the first post-quantum SNARK with
        Õ(λ) proof size, by a multiplicative factor of Θ(log λ). Practically, our system yields proofs around 110 KB,
        while outperforming the state-of-the-art polynomial commitment scheme Greyhound (CRYPTO 2024)
        with ∼ 50× faster verification, similar prover time, and competitive proof size. Our framework natively
        supports (tensor-)structured relations, such as polynomial evaluation and sumcheck relations.
        At a high level, our construction follows the recursive split-and-fold paradigm: the prover first splits the
        witness into ρ sub-witnesses, sends the corresponding cross-terms, and then folds them into a single
        witness that is shorter by a factor of ρ using verifier challenges. Prior works typically restrict ρ = O(1)
        to preserve succinct verification and maintain the optimal Õ(λ) proof size. We overcome this “constant
        barrier”, which enables larger ρ and thereby reduces the proof size. To achieve this, we introduce the
        following technical contributions.
         (i) Committed folding. Instead of sending O(ρ) cross-terms in the clear, the prover commits to the
             messages and later proves that the committed vector satisfies the verification relations. This enables
             a larger shrinking factor, reducing the number of recursion rounds. While this strategy has been
             successfully used in LaBRADOR (CRYPTO 2023), our main novelty is to do so while preserving
             succinct verification.
        (ii) Recursive commitments. We generalise the double-commitment technique from LaBRADOR into
             a framework for recursive commitments, yielding further compression in commitment size. This
             results in concrete improvements in communication within each recursion round.
       (iii) Sumcheck-driven structured recursion. We extend the sumcheck framework from SALSAA (ePrint
             2025/2124) to prove substantially more complex constraints arising in our construction, including
             correctness of random projections, inner-product claims and well-formedness of recursive commit-
             ments, and open for future extensions. Expressing these constraints as sumcheck relations takes
             considerable effort, but the resulting protocols compose seamlessly with the structured recursion,
             yielding linear-time proving and succinct verification.


1     Introduction

Succinct argument systems [Kil92, Mic94] allow a prover to convince a verifier of a statement’s
validity using communication that is significantly shorter than the witness itself. This powerful
compression capability underlies some of the most impactful applications in modern cryptography,
including verifiable outsourced computation [KP16, Lee21, GSW23], verifiable fully-homomorphic
encryption [GBK+ 24, LLZ+ 25, ABPS24, ZLW+ 25], aggregate signatures [BK20, AAB+ 24], and
verifiable delay functions [LM23, OKC+ 25].
   As quantum computers threaten the security foundations of group-based cryptography, the
demand for post-quantum secure succinct arguments has become increasingly urgent. Lattice-based
constructions offer a compelling direction as they rely on well-studied hardness assumptions believed

1.1   Two Approaches in Lattice-Based Succinct Arguments

At the core of lattice-based arguments lies an efficient proof of knowledge of a low-norm witness

    As analysed in [AL21, ACK21, BCS21], the lattice Bulletproofs protocol suffers from several
efficiency drawbacks. Most notably, knowledge soundness critically depends on subtractive sets C, in
which the difference of any two distinct c, c′ ∈ C must be invertible in Rq := R/qR with a short
inverse. This limits C to polynomial size [AL21], implying an inverse-polynomial soundness error;
amplifying it to 2−λ then increases communication by a factor Ω(λ/ log λ). As a consequence, lattice
Bulletproofs have large communication, both in theory and in practice.
    Subsequent work in this area has diverged into two main directions. One path focuses on
constructing succinct arguments with concretely efficient proof sizes, while the other aims at
asymptotically efficient arguments with polylogarithmic verification. We elaborate on both directions
below.

Path 1: Concrete efficiency and proof size. The lattice Bulletproofs protocol was substantially
advanced by LaBRADOR [BS23], which introduces several key modifications. The first is that it
departs from subtractive sets and instead adopts the random-projection technique of [BL17, LNS21,

                                                         2
GHL22, LNP22, BS23]. To demonstrate that w is (approximately) short, the verifier samples a
random integer matrix J ∈ Zr×mφ with small entries (e.g. from {−1, 0, 1}). The prover computes the
projected vector v := Jcf(w) mod q ∈ Zrq , where cf(w) ∈ Zmφ is the standard coefficient embedding
of the ring vector w. The verifier checks that v has small norm, while the prover additionally proves
v well-formed; this is a linear relation over Rq [LNP22]. Crucially, this removes the dependence on
subtractive sets, so the protocol needs no repetitions.
    The second change is that the prover no longer transmits all ρ2 cross-terms gi,j . Instead, the
prover commits to the concatenated vector g := (gi,j )i∈[ρ],j∈[ρ] , and later proves that the committed
value satisfies the linear constraints required for verification, namely those in Equations (2) and (3).

at each round (where mi is the witness size entering round i), yielding only O(log log m) recursive
rounds.
    Finally, in LaBRADOR the witness w is committed in a double-commitment fashion4 . Specifically,
the commitment tout to a short vector w is defined by

in classical Ajtai commitments [Ajt96].
    As a consequence of the aforementioned improvements, LaBRADOR achieves concretely efficient
proof sizes, around 60 KB even for large statements (with witness consisting of ≈ 220 ring elements).
Its main drawback is the linear verification time. Indeed, even processing the random projection
matrix J already takes time Ω(m). The subsequent work Greyhound [NS24] addresses this limitation

by invoking LaBRADOR as a subroutine, where each invocation is on a witness of length O( m).
As a result, Greyhound inherits the appealing proof sizes of LaBRADOR while achieving sublinear
verification time.

Path 2: Succinct verification. Motivated by the growing interest in constructing SNARKs from
Polynomial Interactive Oracle Proofs, there has been much research on lattice-based polynomial
commitment schemes (PCS). In this direction, [CLM23] first adapted lattice Bulletproofs to sup-
port succinct verification by endowing the commitment matrix F with a tensor structure. This
modification leads to a commitment that is binding under a new assumption, called the Vanishing
SIS (vSIS) assumption. Independently, [BCS23] achieved fast verification for lattice Bulletproofs
via additional preprocessing. However, both constructions inherit all the underlying limitations of
lattice Bulletproofs itself [BCS23, CLM23].
    A different line of work [AFLN24, CMNW24, KLNO24b] develops succinct arguments with
polylogarithmic verification time by applying FRI-style [BBHR18] split-and-fold techniques. These
protocols still require repetitions due to the use of subtractive sets, but they yield more efficient
proofs than Bulletproofs, since they involve only O(ρ) cross-terms, compared to O(ρ2 ) required in
lattice Bulletproofs.
    The reliance on subtractive sets was later removed by [KLNO25], which adapts the random
projection strategy to demonstrate shortness. Directly using LaBRADOR-style random projections
is infeasible here due to the goal of achieving fast verification. To address this, the authors employ

 is a short random challenge matrix with O(λ) rows and columns. Consequently, the verifier only
 needs to operate on J′ , and with a carefully designed split-and-fold protocol, succinct verification

 obtained Õ(λ2 ) proofs. Despite this asymptotic, concrete efficiency remains limited as proof sizes
 scale to a few megabytes (with witness of ≈ 223 ring elements).
     While random projections can demonstrate that the committed witness is approximately short,
 they cannot on their own certify exact norm bounds. To address this, [KLNO24b, KLNO25] showed
 how to prove inner products between committed vectors modulo q. Provided that w is sufficiently
 short, no modular wrap-around occurs, enabling the prover to establish inner products over Z.
 Building on this, SALSAA [KLOT25] adds sumcheck protocols [LFKN92] to verify exact ℓ2 norm
 and, unlike [KLNO24b, KLNO25], enjoys a linear prover.

 Bridging the gap. LaBRADOR [BS23] achieves remarkably small proof sizes, owing to its use of
 super-constant folding, double-layer commitments, and random projections to establish shortness.
 However, its principal limitation is the linear verification time, and even with the improvement offered
 by Greyhound [NS24], the verifier remains more than two orders of magnitude slower than hash-
 based alternatives. Conversely, argument systems that support succinct verification are still far from
 concretely competitive. Although recent work incorporates structured Johnson–Lindenstrauss pro-
 jections into this setting, it remains unclear whether the other techniques underpinning LaBRADOR
 adapt to the succinct-verifier regime while preserving practical efficiency. This raises a question: can
 one design an argument system that admits polylogarithmic verification while achieving proof sizes
 and concrete performance similar to LaBRADOR and Greyhound?

 1.2   Our contributions
 We present RoKoko – a lattice-based argument system that unifies the strengths of both paths,
 achieving nearly the best of both worlds:
  (i) Succinct verification: polylogarithmic verifier runtime, matching the best Path 2 constructions;
 (ii) Optimal prover: linear-time proving, matching the lower bound;
(iii) Compact proofs: proof sizes substantially improving on prior succinct-verification work,
      narrowing the gap to the LaBRADOR line;
(iv) Rich expressiveness: native support for structured relations, polynomial commitments, and
      natural arithmetisations such as R1CS.

Technical innovations. Our main technical contribution is to adapt committed superconstant folding
– a technique pioneered by LaBRADOR [BS23] – to the vSIS-based framework that supports succinct
verification. In the LaBRADOR line, the verifier performs work proportional to the witness size, so
the folding structure can remain opaque to the verifier. In the succinct-verification regime, however,
the verifier must efficiently check consistency of the folded instances without implicitly reading
the full witness, which requires the folding to respect the algebraic structure of the underlying
commitment scheme. Concretely:

  – Structured superconstant folding. We show how to fold by a superconstant factor within
    the vSIS-based framework while preserving the structure needed for succinct verification. We

                                                    4
   extend the random projection technique of [KLNO25] to handle this more aggressive folding,
   yielding a significant reduction in the number of rounds.
 – Recursive commitments. Inspired by [BS23, BC25b], we introduce a recursive commitment
   structure where the outermost commitment – the only one the verifier inspects explicitly – has
   low rank independent of the witness size. This compresses the commitment component of the
   proof, which dominates proof size in prior constructions from Path 2 (the succinct-verification
   regime); such recursive commitments were not previously available in the structured setting.
 – Sumcheck-based linear-time proving. Building on [KLOT25], we integrate sumcheck pro-
   tocols to efficiently prove well-formedness of the recursive commitments and correctness of
   projections. The sumcheck machinery suits the recursive commitment structure well: the prover
   shows each commitment layer is correctly formed without materialising intermediate witnesses,
   achieving linear-time proving without sacrificing succinctness.
 – Implementation and evaluation. We implement RoKoko and conduct experiments, demon-
   strating that our improvements translate into practice: competitive proof sizes alongside faster
   verification compared to prior lattice-based constructions. We also give a complete asymptotic
   analysis and instantiate RoKoko as a SNARK for structured relations and as a PCS.
 – Incomplete-NTT library. As a side contribution, we provide the most efficient open-source
   standalone implementation, to date, of the “almost-splitting” cyclotomic ring arithmetic under-
   pinning our construction.

   We summarise the concrete performance of RoKoko compared to prior post-quantum SNARKs and
PCSs in Table 1. Compared to SALSAA [KLOT25], the closest prior work with succinct verification,
RoKoko produces roughly 10× smaller proofs and reduces the combined commitment and prover
time by roughly 8×, while its verifier is roughly 1.8× slower. Compared to Greyhound [NS24],
which optimises for proof size and concrete efficiency but lacks succinct verification, RoKoko offers
roughly 52× faster verification on the target machine, reaching roughly 90× on the largest server
benchmark, with similar prover time but roughly 2.4× larger proofs. The trade-off is that RoKoko
achieves polylogarithmic verification time and relies on the vSIS assumption, while Greyhound has
smaller proofs and relies on the more standard Module-SIS assumption but without polylogarithmic
verification. We defer a detailed comparison of RoKoko against prior work to Section 9.5.

1.3   Related work
Group- and hash-based succinct arguments. Group-based arguments such as Groth16 [Gro16],
PLONK [GWC19] and others [PHGR13, GGPR13, WTs+ 18, MBKM19, XZZ+ 19, BFS20, CHM+ 20]
achieve strong efficiency but rely on assumptions like discrete logarithm, and are thus insecure against
quantum adversaries. Hash-based argument systems, such as FRI [BBHR18], Ligero [AHIV23],
Aurora [BCR+ 19], Brakedown [GLS+ 23], and WHIR [ACFY25], offer post-quantum security and
transparent setup. In particular, WHIR achieves extremely efficient verification time. However,
hash-based schemes generally fall short in prover efficiency and proof size compared to lattice-based
alternatives.

Non-succinct lattice-based arguments. Non-succinct lattice-based arguments [LNSW13,BLS19,
ALS20, LNP22] have proof size and verification time linear in the witness size, offering high concrete
efficiency for small relations. These protocols typically focus on the zero-knowledge property rather
than succinctness.

Folding schemes. Orthogonal to “monolithic” SNARKs discussed above (following the terminology
of [BCFW25]), a line of works [BC25a, FKNP24, BMNW25a, BMNW25b, NS25, BC25b, BCFW25]
have proposed post-quantum folding schemes (or accumulation schemes) that can be used as building
blocks of incrementally-verifiable computation protocols [Val08] and proof-carrying data [CT10].
Folding schemes also compile generically into SNARKs [KST21].
    Among recent quantum-safe folding schemes, several rely on lattice assumptions [BC25a,FKNP24,
NS25, BC25b]. As in our setting, the prover must prove (exact) shortness of the witness. To achieve
this, [BC25a, NS25, BC25b] apply the sumcheck protocol to prove the ℓ∞ norm bound. However, as
the communication complexity scales linearly with the range, this approach becomes impractical for
large ℓ∞ bounds and is not well-suited to prove ℓ2 norms.


2     Technical Overview

We give a high-level overview of our lattice-based succinct argument construction, its applications
and its implementation.


2.1    Background

Let R ∼= Z[x]/⟨Φf (x)⟩ be a cyclotomic ring and Rq = R/qR for a prime q. Works on lattice-based
succinct arguments typically consider a sufficiently expressive relation defined over Rq where the
witness is a matrix W ∈ Rmw ×r of norm at most some βw . The goal is always to progressively shrink
the witness dimension mw until it is small enough to be sent in the clear. Two intertwined challenges
must be addressed:

                                                           6
 1. Witness folding. The basic idea of shrinking the witness W is to fold W into Wc by some verifier
    challenge c, and then repack the witness into a smaller matrix W′ .5 The shrink factor ρ, i.e. the
    rate at which the witness dimension is reduced, is roughly the dimension of c. What distinguishes
    the semi-6 and fully-succinct settings is that, in the latter, the verifier must be able to check
    (among other constraints due to norm control) the correctness of folding in polylogarithmic time.
 2. Norm control. While shrinking the witness dimension, we must keep the witness norm at around
    the same level, say βw . This splits into two sub-challenges. Completeness, i.e. the honest prover
    can produce a valid witness for the reduced instance. Folding W into Wc may increase the norm;
    the standard fix is to decompose the folded witness Wc, giving a net dimension reduction when
    the shrinking by c outweighs the decomposition expansion. We take this for granted and omit it
    henceforth. Extraction, i.e. the extractor can recover a valid witness for the original instance. As
    usual for lattice-based arguments, it recovers not an exact witness but a “relaxed” one (W, s)
    with a short “slack” vector s, relaxed in two ways: it only guarantees ∥W · diag(s)∥ ≤ βw′ (not
    necessarily ∥W∥ ≤ βw′ ), and the bound may be looser than the original (βw′ > βw ). This too can
    accumulate throughout composition.

    Numerous prior works address subsets of these challenges with different trade-offs; we highlight
the most relevant.
    Regarding witness folding, [CLM23] proposes using vSIS commitments so that the witness
can be folded in a structured way that enables a polylogarithmic verifier. This method is refined
in [KLNO24b] and adopted in [KLNO25, KLOT25]. In [KLNO24b, KLNO25, KLOT25], folding the
witness by a factor ρ requires communication proportional to ρ per round, forcing them to settle on
                                                                       1/3
ρ = O(1). LaBRADOR [BS23] achieves a shrink factor of ρ = O(mw ) with essentially constant
communication per round by having the prover commit to the cross-terms instead of sending them
in the clear. However, this method of folding breaks whatever structure the original relation might
have, causing the verifier to run in time proportional to mw .
    Regarding norm control, LaBRADOR [BS23] uses random projections to eliminate the slack
vector s. However, the projection matrices are unstructured, which again causes the verifier to run
in time proportional to mw . RoK and Roll [KLNO25] adapted this technique to the fully-succinct
setting by introducing structured projections. Then, to force the extracted witness norm βw′ to be
the same as the original norm βw , SALSAA [KLOT25] adapted the sumcheck protocol to prove
the exact norm of the witness with a prover that runs in strict- (as opposed to quasi-)linear time,
improving upon the norm-check RoK of [KLNO24b].
    The main contribution of this work is to extend the committed folding technique (explored in the
literature in [BS23] or [BC25b] in the context of folding schemes) to the fully-succinct setting while
retaining all techniques introduced in [KLNO24b, KLNO25, KLOT25] for structure-preserving norm
control. This will ultimately allow us to achieve a more favourable trade-off than the state-of-the-art.

Committed random projection Π proj : ΞCOM lin        lin . This RoK addresses norm control : inspired
                                                → ΞCOM
                                          lin
by [KLNO25], it is a self-reduction on ΞCOM that eliminates the extraction-side norm slack. The key
idea relies on a variant of the Johnson–Lindenstrauss (JL) lemma: a random matrix J ∈ Znrp ×mrp
with small i.i.d. entries maps x ∈ Zmrp so that the ratio ∥Jx∥/∥x∥ lies in a bounded interval [αrp , βrp ]
with high probability – the norm is scaled up but tightly controlled. Naively, one would apply such
a J to the entire witness of dimension mw , but the verifier would then need to read all nrp × mw
entries of J, breaking succinctness. Instead, the prover applies a structured variant (I ⊗ J) where
J ∈ Znrp ×mrp with mrp ≪ mw : the verifier only samples the small matrix J, and the block-diagonal
structure preserves the JL guarantee on each block. The JL guarantee ensures that a short projection
image implies the original witness is short, i.e. without slack.
    We instantiate this idea in three variants, which differ in the granularity at which they project
and hence in their cost. The coarse variant (Π proj-c ) projects ring elements directly and is used while
mw ≥ mrp : it is the cheapest, since computing (I ⊗ J)W only requires additions and subtractions
of ring elements. The fine variant (Π proj-f ) projects their coefficients via the trace-dual embedding
(used once mw drops below mrp , relaxing the dimension requirement to mrp | φmw ), and then batches
the projection and lifts to constraints over Rq : the consistency between W and its projection is
a statement over Zq , which is batched with random Zq challenges into nbat constraints over Rq ,
so that the round communicates nbat additional ring elements. The lift-and-batch variant (Π proj-fl )
generalises the fine variant by exploiting the chain of intermediate subfields Fqa of Rq to gradually
lift the projection image instead of immediately lifting to Rq , which removes the overhead of nbat
ring elements and yields further asymptotic improvements. All variants share the same (I ⊗ J)
structure, and – crucially – the prover commits to the (gadget-decomposed) projection image rather
than sending it in the clear.

both the projection height and the witness width, and is sent in the clear; in our setting, widening
the witness and increasing the projection height to accommodate a large ρ would make this image
prohibitively large for uncompressed transmission. Committing to the image reduces that overhead.

2.3     Putting Everything Together
Composition and asymptotics. The full composition proceeds in two phases: first, a coarse chain
(Π lin ◦ Π fold-split ◦ Π proj-c ) is iterated while mw ≥ mrp ; once mw drops below mrp , the fine chain
(Π lin ◦ Π fold-split ◦ Π proj-f ) takes over for the remaining rounds8 . With ρ = λ, the full composition
yields a prover runtime of O(mw ) Rq operations (linear-time), verifier runtime of O(λ3 log mw / log λ),
and proof size of O(λ log3 mw / log2 λ) bits. Compared to SALSAA [KLOT25], which is effectively
limited to ρ = O(1) because their verifier complexity grows linearly in ρ, we reduce proof size by a
factor of log λ while matching prover complexity, at the cost of a theoretically larger verifier runtime.

Applications. A succinct argument for ΞCOM lin immediately yields a polynomial commitment scheme
(PCS): the polynomial evaluation claim p(v) = t is a linear constraint ℓT Wr = t mod q on the
committed coefficient matrix W, which is already a ΞCOMlin  instance (as noted above). If we instead

Implementation. We implement RoKoko in Rust, targeting almost-splitting power-of-two cyclotomic
rings (Rq ∼= (Fq2 )φ/2 , with φ = 128, q ≈ 250 ). Almost-splitting rings (residue degree e = 2) provide

release the ring arithmetic layer as an independent open-source library. On top of it, we build a
sumcheck module with a generic interface for expressing sumcheck relations, and instantiate it to
deliver a PCS. For a PCS over 226 coefficients in Zq (single-threaded, λ = 128, κ = 2−100 ), our
implementation produces a 112 KB proof in 3.38 s (commit + prove), with verification in 8.12 ms.
Compared to SALSAA [KLOT25], this is a ∼10× reduction in proof size and ∼8× faster proving,
while verification is ∼1.8× slower; compared to Greyhound [NS24], we offer ∼52× faster verification
(reaching ∼90× on the largest instance run on the server-grade machine; see Table 8).

in binary with µ bits, the high-order (µ − µi ) bits encode ⌊(ωi + j)/mi ⌋. Since 0 ≤ j < mi , we have

range [ωi , ωi + mi − 1] share the common prefix pi .

5    Committed Random Projection

Random projection controls the norm growth during extraction. The extractor for folding (Section 6)
outputs a witness with a “slack” factor ϱ: the extracted W satisfies ∥W · diag(s)∥2 ≤ βw′ for some s
with ∥s∥2 ≤ ϱ. In recursive composition, this slack would accumulate and blow up the norm bound.
Applying random projection before folding ensures that the projected witness satisfies a strict norm
bound (without slack) with high probability. We give two projection RoKs, inspired by [KLNO25]. A
coarse variant Π proj-c (Fig. 2) applies a random projection matrix to full ring elements; it is simple
and efficient but requires the witness dimension to be large enough for the projection to compress.
A fine variant Π proj-f (Fig. 3) instead projects the coefficients of the witness ring elements, handling
smaller witness dimensions. Both are self-reductions for the committed linear relation ΞCOM  lin , adding

fresh commitments to the projected witnesses.

(as g is not known to it). During knowledge extraction, the extractor knows g.

vSIS parameters. To assess the hardness of vSIS, we model it as an SIS-type problem whose
associated Euclidean kernel lattice has dimension N := φ · n, where φ is the cyclotomic degree and n
is the module rank. Under this identification, a random n-tuple over Rq defines a random Zq -linear
constraint, and solving vSIS amounts to finding a short nonzero kernel vector, as in SIS [MR09].

q = poly(λ) and β = poly(λ). Then log q = Θ(log λ) and log β = Θ(log λ), so the dependence on

9     Implementation and Experiments

As a baseline we consider the experiments of [NS24], focusing on the polynomial-commitment instan-
tiation for norm-bounded coefficients from Zq embedded into ring elements. For best performance
we work with power-of-two cyclotomic rings R = Z[ζf ], f = 2k , which admit efficient arithmetic via
the Number-Theoretic Transform (NTT). We take R to “almost split” modulo q, i.e. Rq ∼          = (Fq2 )φ/2 .
We do not use the “lift-and-batch” technique (Section B): for our concrete parameters its extra
interaction rounds and protocol complexity outweigh the communication savings, so projections use
solely Π proj-c and Π proj-f . Almost-splitting also lets us use Fqe (e = 2) as a challenge set and batch
across the φ/e NTT slots. We select q ≈ 250 so that runtimes benefit from the AVX-512-IFMA
instructions available on modern Intel processors. We operate over the f-th cyclotomic ring for
f = 256 (i.e. φ = 128). All benchmark suites prove a ΞCOM    lin  relation with random tensor-structured
ℓ, r, equivalent to opening a multilinear polynomial at a random point. The witness ℓ∞ norm is set
to 231 , matching [NS24].

9.1    Approximate challenge sampling

We select a modulus q such that Rq ∼
                                   = (Fq2 )φ/2 , i.e. our cyclotomic ring splits into factors of degree
2. This is because:

 – The knowledge error of the RoKs we use (e.g. Π proj-c , Π proj-f , Π lin ) is at least 1/q e , where e is
   the residue degree of the ring modulo q. If e = 1, as in the case of fully splitting power-of-two
   cyclotomic rings, the knowledge error becomes too large.
 – For Π fold-split we require the existence of a low-norm challenge set [LS18, ALS20, BC25b] so
   that the difference of any two distinct elements is invertible. We require that the set is large
   enough to ensure a sufficiently small knowledge error and the operator norm of the challenges
   is small. Such set cannot exist if the ring fully splits as the largest subfield of such ring is Zq
   itself, which itself is not large enough to provide a sufficiently small knowledge error. In other
   work, e.g. [BS23, NS24], the issue is avoided by using “almost-not-splitting” cyclotomic rings,

                                                            46
      i.e. rings that split into factors of large degree. Then, as shown in [LS18], low-norm challenges’
      differences are always invertible. However, such rings do not support NTT-based multiplication
      directly, leading to a significant computational overhead16 . We take a different approach and
      sample challenges so that the coefficients are sampled uniformly from the ternary set {−1, 0, 1}.
      The set of challenges with ternary coefficients is not a strong sampling set by definition, but
      the probability that the difference of two sampled challenges is non-invertible is small. More
      precisely, the probability of sampling two elements so that their difference is non-invertible is
      bounded in Lemma 32 of [BL25] by

9.2      Implementation details
Ring arithmetic. Our implementation is done in Rust. The heart of our implementation is efficient
cyclotomic ring arithmetic, which is crucial for the performance of our protocols. As mentioned
above, we select parameters so that our ring is “almost-splitting”, i.e. it splits into factors of degree 2,
which allows us to leverage the incomplete NTT for efficient polynomial multiplication: each slot
contains a degree-1 residue polynomial (ai + bi X) in Zq [X]/(X 2 − ψi ), and multiplication reduces
to φ/2 independent products of such pairs.
    We provide two variants of the ring arithmetic implementation:
    The first variant uses Intel HEXL [BKS+ 21] via a foreign-function interface. Multiplication of
degree-φ cyclotomic ring elements in incomplete-NTT form is decomposed into 7 separate HEXL
calls using the schoolbook formula: 5 element-wise modular multiplications and 2 element-wise
modular additions, all operating on vectors of length φ/2. This variant can be viewed as a Rust
re-implementation of the ring arithmetic from [CCC+ 25], designed for proof-friendly CKKS-like
FHE schemes (with the distinction that we do not support RNS decomposition). In our benchmarks,
this approach matches the performance of the original C++ implementation in [CCC+ 25].
    The second variant is based on our own pure-Rust re-implementation of the relevant subset of
HEXL, targeting NTT-based arithmetic for moduli q ≈ 250 . The performance improvement over the
first variant is twofold. First, we fuse the entire multiplication into a single AVX-512 kernel: instead
of 7 separate passes over the data, our kernel performs a single pass that loads all inputs and writes

comes entirely from the improved memory access pattern and the reduced multiplication count17 .
    We remark that these observations, although presented in general terms, have been concretely
verified in our benchmarks conducted on the same hardware as the rest of our experiments (see
below). That being said, our benchmarking machine features a mid-range mobile CPU. Non-
consumer workstations, servers, or cloud CPUs with different microarchitectures may exhibit
different performance characteristics – for instance, larger L1 caches may reduce the relative benefit
of our fused kernel, different AVX-512 implementations may shift absolute throughput, and less
aggressive frequency downscaling under AVX-512 workloads could further improve performance. We
leave a more detailed analysis across different microarchitectures for future work.
    We have open-sourced our ring arithmetic implementation as an independent library18 . The
library is thoroughly tested and benchmarked, and primarily consists of a re-implementation of
the relevant subset of Intel HEXL, enhanced with our fused kernel and Karatsuba optimisation.
We chose to develop our own implementation rather than modify the original HEXL codebase to
avoid the overhead of foreign-function calls between Rust and C++, and to have greater flexibility
in applying optimisations. Our re-implementation (within the set of overlapping functionalities)
achieves performance on par with the original HEXL.

Projection arithmetic. For projections, both coarse and fine variant, we rely on highly-efficient
intrinsics from the AVX-512 instruction set to implement the required matrix-vector multiplications,
taking advantage of the structure of the projection matrices (for both prover and verifier side).
In projections, we deliberately delay using modular reduction as much as possible, allowing us to
perform multiple additions and multiplications before reducing the results modulo q, which leads to
significant performance improvements.

Platform and portability. Due to the level of optimisations, we limited our focus on Intel microarchi-
tectures with AVX-512-IFMA support, which are widely available in modern Intel CPUs. HEXL
does not support any other microarchitectures, except x86-64. Our independent implementation of
the ring arithmetic does not inherit this limitation and can be executed on any microarchitecture
supported by Rust, albeit with a significant performance degradation.

More efficient commitment. The commitment is a matrix-vector product whose right operand is
short: the committed witness consists of balanced base-216 digits. We therefore do not evaluate
FW over Rq at all. Instead we compute it exactly over the integers, modulo a product of a few
NTT-friendly primes below 214 , and reduce to q once at the end.

9.3   Parameters selection
We select parameters to achieve λ = 128 security against known attacks listed in Lattice Estimator
[APS15]. Our codebase is equipped with an “estimator” mode, which allows us to easily experiment
with different parameters. Prover, while executed in the “estimator” mode, outputs the estimated
security level, accounting for the norm growth during the extraction process. For statistical soundness,
we set overall soundness error κ ≈ 2−100 (mainly impacted by the size of NTT slots) following the
industry standards.
   Our parameters are selected semi-manually, by preparing the configuration for all test suites.
Then, we run the test suites in “estimator” mode and adjust the parameters accordingly.
   To reduce communication, we deviate slightly from the protocol description and allow the
prover to perform even more aggressive norm decomposition on the “final level” of the recursive
commitment. Then, a separate proof for the norm of the final level is provided (as an additional
constraint in fsc ). This allows us to reduce the rank of the final level significantly, leading to smaller
proof sizes.

9.5   Comparison with prior work concrete performance

We position ourselves as a significant improvement over the state-of-the-art lattice-based SNARKs,
both in terms of asymptotic complexity and concrete performance. We view SALSAA [KLOT25]
and Greyhound [NS24] as the most relevant prior works, since they are the only lattice-based
SNARKs whose concrete performance numbers are close to ours. Compared to SALSAA, we achieve
a significant improvement in combined commitment and prover time and proof size, while verifier is
roughly 2 times slower. Compared to Greyhound, we achieve a significant improvement in verifier
time and some improvement in the prover time. Commitment time reported in Table 1 suggests
that our commitment is faster than Greyhound, but we attribute this to the fact that Greyhound
uses slightly smaller cyclotomic rings, therefore does not benefit that much from the NTT-based
multiplication (and in general, Greyhound suffers from overhead of RNS-style ring arithmetic).
Proof size of our scheme is larger than Greyhound’s by a constant factor. We believe that this is
mainly due to parameters selection, i.e. executing our protocol with additional levels of recursion
rank would lead to smaller proof sizes, but we have not explored that direction, since the verifier
time scales linearly with the number of levels, and we wanted to keep the verifier time as low as
possible.

9.6   Towards a general-usage library

We consider our work as an ongoing effort towards building a general-purpose library for lattice-based
succinct arguments. We outline several directions for future work.

(a) For the sake of this work, we restricted our focus to the PCS-like relations. However, our
    sumcheck implementation is done in a highly-modular fashion, allowing us to easily plug in
    different relations as the first step of the reductions chain, which we view as a step towards
    building a general-purpose library for lattice-based succinct arguments.
(b) Our current API is restricted to calls to the functions within the same process. A natural
    extension is to support serialisation/deserialisation of proofs, enabling network communication
    between prover and verifier.

                                                   50
(c) The parameters are hard-coded in the codebase and picked semi-manually. An important direction
    is to support dynamic parameter selection, allowing users to specify security levels and other
    parameters at runtime.
(d) Our ring arithmetic implementation is optimised for power-of-two cyclotomic rings, splitting into
    degree-2 factors. A natural direction is to extend the implementation to support more general
    rings, or more concretely, to extend support for splitting into degree-4 factors, which would
    allow us to further reduce the knowledge error. With the intention of still using the “native” (i.e.
    without defining an auxiliary, fully splitting, composite modulus and then projecting back to the
    original ring) approach, we expect that the performance degradation for the prover would be at
    most 1.4× (since around half time is spent on the element-wise multiplications, which would
    be slower by a factor of at most 1.8× following the numbers from [CCC+ 25]) and the verifier
    performance would negligibly degrade (since the verifier spends most of the time batching the
   “fine” projections with Zq challenges, which would be unaffected by the change of the ring).
