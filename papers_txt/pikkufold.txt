 PikkuFold: Efficient Folding in a Few Kilobytes
                                     Michał Osadnik
                                    Aalto University, Finland



      Abstract. Folding is a powerful technique for constructing efficient succinct proof
      systems, especially for computations expressed in a streaming fashion. We present
      PikkuFold, a new lattice-based folding protocol that improves upon state-of-the-art
      folding schemes such as SALSAA (ePrint 2025/2124) and Cyclo (EUROCRYPT
      2026). One folding step communicates 5.7 KB in addition to the commitments to its
      fresh inputs, compared with ≥ 30 KB for Cyclo and ≥ 60 KB for SALSAA on similar
      instances, with a prover more than an order of magnitude faster and the verifier in the
      millisecond range. At the heart of our construction are layered random projections,
      whose algebraic structure makes them fast to verify and whose final image is short
      enough to send to the verifier directly, eliminating the cost of auxiliary commitments.
      We use these techniques to replace Cyclo’s costly and restrictive range proofs while
      keeping the increase in the accumulator norm small and additive across multiple folds.
      PikkuFold is the first lattice-based construction that does not require any in-protocol
      commitments beyond those of the fresh input relations. Such commitments account
      for much of a folding transcript: every prior lattice-based scheme commits to a
      decomposed or otherwise transformed witness during the fold, immediately increasing
      the communication by dozens of kilobytes. We also provide two contributions of
      independent interest beyond folding schemes:
        (i) a Johnson–Lindenstrauss theorem for biased ternary matrices modulo q with
            certified concrete constants, which replaces the heuristic parametrisation used
            in prior works, and
       (ii) a thorough analysis of the short-challenge sampler with fixed Hamming weight
            and operator-norm rejection, offering a wide range of parameter sets. Using
            this sampler as a drop-in replacement would lead to immediate improvements
            across a wide range of lattice-based protocols.


1     Introduction
Succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (SNARKs) have emerged as a key prim-
itive in modern cryptography, finding applications in blockchains [XZC+ 22, GKO24,
LM25, LZW+ 25], privacy-preserving authentication [OREHS24], and verifiable computa-
tion [ACGS24, CCC+ 25]. However, a fundamental memory bottleneck limits their practical
deployment [LZW+ 24, ZSCZ25]: generating a proof requires the prover to hold the entire
computation in memory simultaneously. For large computations, this is often infeasible,
and it fails to exploit the fact that many real-world computations proceed in a streaming
or incremental fashion.
    Practical scenarios such as blockchain state transitions and verifiable outsourced
computation (e.g., cloud/edge ML inference and long-running services) call for a more
scalable approach. This has motivated the study of incrementally verifiable computation
(IVC) [Val08, NPR19] and the more general notion of proof-carrying data [CT10, BCL+ 21,
BDFG21, CCG+ 23]. Early IVC constructions relied on SNARKs, recursively producing

at each step a proof that the previous verifier accepted [Val08, BCCT12, BCTV14]. A

accumulator instance, avoiding the overhead of full recursive proof verification at every
step.

they avoid “public-key assumptions”, these constructions require codeword encodings and
numerous Merkle authentication paths, leading to relatively large communication. Their
concrete efficiency also remains less thoroughly explored.

1.1     Lattice-Based Folding Schemes
A major recent advance is the development of lattice-based folding schemes, including
Lova [FKNP24], LatticeFold [BC25a], LatticeFold+ [BC25b], Neo and SuperNeo [NS26],
SALSAA [KLOT25], Cyclo [GLLO26], and ProtogaLattice [BNP26].
   The special role of lattice-based cryptography in the construction of modern folding
schemes stems from the availability of simple linearly homomorphic commitments. At the
heart of each of the aforementioned schemes is an Ajtai-style commitment scheme [Ajt96],
whose security is based on the Short Integer Solution (SIS) problem. A commitment to a
short vector w ∈ Rm q is a vector y ∈ Rq such that

     in [GLLO26], computing commitments to the decomposition is computationally
     expensive.
(A3) Approximate proof via Johnson–Lindenstrauss. Yet another approach, informally de-
     scribed in [Osa26] and treated more formally in [KLOT25], uses Johnson–Lindenstrauss
     projections, which are norm-preserving in the sense that ∥Jw∥ ≈ ∥w∥, in a structured
     variant that admits a succinct description. Although Jw has a smaller dimension
     than w, it is still too long to send to the verifier. The prover therefore commits
     to Jw and proves that the commitment is correct. The resulting relation cannot
     be folded immediately, so the accumulator must contain at least two commitments,
     even before accounting for decomposition.
(A4) Approximate norm-preserving extraction via subtractive set. Subtractive sets [AL21]
     have not been used for folding schemes, but they can serve as the extractor’s challenge
     set, yielding a witness wacc

     in lattice-based SNARKs [AL21, CLM23, KLNO24]. However, such sets are very
     small, necessitating amplification of the folding, which again increases the size of the
     accumulator relation.
    Approaches (A3) and (A4) incur extraction norm growth: the extracted witness is
slightly larger, by a multiplicative factor, than the original witness. Norm-upgrading
techniques [LNP22, KLNO24, KLOT25] address this problem by proving the exact norm
of a witness already known to satisfy a relaxed bound β ′ ≥ β.
    Cyclo [GLLO26] makes a complementary observation. It shows that, instead of choosing
between multiplicative norm growth in the extraction direction – which becomes exponential
in the number of folding steps – and an expensive range proof for the accumulator, one
can prove a norm bound only for the input relations (not the accumulator) and accept
additive norm growth, which is only linear in the number of folding steps. Consequently,
norm growth in the correctness direction also becomes additive, eliminating the need for

decomposition for a bounded number of folding steps. The trick of Cyclo is simply to avoid
combining the accumulator commitment with any challenge. This requires the accumulator
to remain a single relation of a form similar to Equation (1).
    Therefore, to take advantage of these optimisations, Cyclo applies approach (A1) to
fresh inputs. This limits its applicability to relations whose coefficient ℓ∞ -norm bound β
is sufficiently small. For larger bounds, Cyclo resorts to a relatively expensive extension
commitment, which is essentially a recommitment to an aggressively decomposed witness.
At the same time, the single-relation accumulator required by Cyclo cannot directly carry
the additional projection relation introduced by approach (A3). Thus, Cyclo provides
additive norm growth without efficient support for large-norm inputs, whereas projection-
based approaches support large norms at the cost of an additional commitment and
accumulator relation.
    This raises the following question:

    Is it possible to construct a lattice-based folding scheme that simultaneously achieves:

      (i) norm preservation, up to small additive growth in both the correctness and
          extraction directions,
     (ii) communication efficiency, in the sense that the prover sends no additional Ajtai
          commitments during folding, and
    (iii) range-check-free efficiency, in the sense that the cost of folding does not depend
          linearly on the coefficient ℓ∞ -norm bound β?


1.2      Our Contributions
In this work, we answer the above question in the affirmative. We present PikkuFold, a
new lattice-based folding scheme that achieves all three properties simultaneously. Our
contributions are as follows.

(C1) We construct a folding scheme based on layered random projections. Each layer
     is structured enough for efficient verification and reduces the witness dimension.
     After the final layer, the projection image is short enough to send directly to the
     verifier. The layer-correctness claims admit a multilinear description and are verified
     by a single ring-sumcheck invocation. Consequently, the prover sends no additional
     commitment to the projection image, and the accumulator remains a single relation
     compatible with additive norm growth.
(C2) We prove a Johnson–Lindenstrauss theorem for biased ternary matrices under modu-
     lar reduction. Previous concrete parameters for such matrices were justified heuris-
     tically [GHL22, BS23]. Our theorem supplies the rigorous tail bounds needed to
     instantiate the projections of (C1) without this heuristic, and it does so at a cost of
     only a few percent in the resulting norm-preservation constants.
(C3) We construct and analyse an efficient sampler for low-norm, fixed-weight ternary
     challenges. The sampler first draws from the exact fixed-weight distribution and
     then rejects elements whose multiplication operator norm is too large. Its rejection
     threshold exposes an explicit tradeoff between challenge norm and sampling time.
     We additionally give a heuristic variant for almost-splitting cyclotomic rings.
(C4) We provide concrete parameter selections for PikkuFold and a vectorised implemen-
     tation at specialised parameters. Concretely, the folding protocol communicates
     roughly 5.7 KB, excluding the fresh input commitments, while retaining millisecond
     verification. At matched input sizes, the prover is more than an order of magnitude
Michał Osadnik                                                                               5

      faster than the SALSAA implementation on the same machine. Thus, once the
      inputs are committed, an entire lattice-based fold fits in only a few kilobytes of
      communication.

    The contributions above are modular. The folding scheme of (C1) is analysed through
abstract guarantees on the projection and challenge distributions. Contributions (C2) and
(C3) establish suitable guarantees using largely independent techniques and may also be
used outside folding schemes, for example in lattice-based SNARKs. They are combined
only when selecting concrete parameters and evaluating (C4).
    Although we state our folding scheme for polynomial-evaluation relations, it serves as
a building block for more expressive lattice-based constructions. In particular, it yields a
folding scheme for AIR relations (Section 7.2) with little additional machinery. Moreover,
the bounded folding depth caused by additive norm growth can be extended by periodically
performing decomposition and an exact norm check, a costly operation amortised over the
folding steps (Section 7.1).

1.3    Perspective
At our concrete parameters, the folding messages excluding fresh input commitments
occupy a few kilobytes, and the Ajtai commitments now dominate total communication.
This shifts the main bottleneck from the folding protocol to the commitment layer. A
natural next step is therefore to combine PikkuFold with techniques for smaller, recursively
composed commitments, in the spirit of [BS23, BBL+ 26a, KLN+ 26].
    A second open direction is zero knowledge: the presented protocol is not zero knowledge,
and hiding the witness throughout sumcheck without losing its communication and prover
efficiency requires additional techniques, for example those developed in Libra [XZZ+ 19].
    We envision using PikkuFold as a building block for incrementally verifiable com-
putation (IVC) [Val08, KST22]. Informally, an IVC folds not only the witness but also
the verifier’s execution of the preceding fold, covering both its algebraic claims and the
correctness of the hash execution, assuming a Fiat–Shamir transform. Such an arith-
metisation of the hash function is a nontrivial task. The arithmetisation of standard
hashes such as SHA-256, BLAKE3 and SHA-3 has recently become an active research
area [DP25, Bin26, BRW26]. These works use polynomial commitments over binary fields
or field-agnostic polynomial commitments, such as those of [ZCF24, Dia25, QGZ26, DP26].
Porting these techniques to the lattice setting is an interesting open problem.

1.4    Paper Organisation
The three main technical components use largely independent techniques, so a single
technical overview would obscure their modularity. We therefore present each component
in its own self-contained section. In Section 3, we construct and analyse the folding scheme
under abstract guarantees on the projection and challenge distributions. In Section 4, we
prove the modular Johnson–Lindenstrauss theorem used to instantiate the projections,
while Section 5 develops the low-norm challenge sampler. We combine these components
in Section 6, where we select concrete parameters and evaluate their joint performance.
Finally, Section 7 explains how to mitigate the bounded-depth constraint of PikkuFold
and how to embed the protocol into a more expressive folding scheme supporting AIR
relations.

    A standard approach to proving that a witness w is short is to send a Johnson–
Lindenstrauss (JL) projection of it. A JL projection gives the desired norm certificate. For
suitable factors ℓ, u > 0, a sampled matrix J satisfies
                                     ℓ∥w∥ ≤ ∥Jw∥ ≤ u∥w∥
with high probability. The prover sends the projection image v = Jw, and the verifier
checks that v is short. Proving Jw = v then allows the extractor to infer that w is short
as well.
    The first natural implementation, used in the lattice-based proof systems of [BS23,
NS24], uses an unstructured JL matrix J. Its projection image is small enough to send
directly, but verification of Jw = v is the obstruction. Although this equality is relatively
easy to prove, the verifier must evaluate an unstructured matrix whose width is the full
witness dimension. Its running time is therefore linear in the witness size.
    A second natural attempt is to use the structured projection introduced in [KLNO25]
and incorporated into the folding schemes of [KLOT25, Osa26]. Instead of a dense matrix,
it uses a block projection Ir ⊗ J0 , where the small matrix J0 ∈ Zn0 ×m0 is shared across
all blocks. Its description and multilinear extension are succinct. Writing ς := m0 /n0 for
the shrinkage, however, one layer maps a witness of dimension N0 only to an image of
dimension N0 /ς, which is still too large to send. Prior work therefore commits to this
image, adding an entire commitment to the communication transcript.
    Our solution is a layered random projection (LRP). We recursively project the output
of each layer into the next layer until the image is short. Every layer has a succinct
description and reduces the current dimension, while preserving the norm up to controlled
factors. The intermediate projection images remain local to the prover. Only the final
image is sent to the verifier, after it has become short enough for direct transmission.
The projection structure may vary between layers. In our instantiation, the earlier layers
project complete ring elements, while the final layer descends to their coefficients. Figure 1

4      A Ternary Johnson–Lindenstrauss Projection
In recent years, the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma has been used extensively in lattice-
based succinct arguments, yet its concrete parameters there have been justified only
heuristically. The reason for its popularity is that these projections can be used to argue
about the norm of a vector without revealing it.
    The first use of this Johnson–Lindenstrauss norm certificate for proving shortness
appeared in [GHL22], which also gave a concrete heuristic analysis of its parameters.
Later, the projection was equipped with a modular counterpart in [BS23], which permits
norm arguments when the projection is computed modulo q. Since then, the ternary
Johnson–Lindenstrauss projection has been used in several works, including [LNP22, BS23,
GHL22, NS24, LSS24, KLNO25, KLOT25, KLN+ 26, BBL+ 26b, Osa26, BBL+ 26a]. The
construction of [LSS24] has also been submitted to the NIST Threshold Call [LNO+ 26].
    The intuition that the ternary Johnson–Lindenstrauss projection approximately pre-
serves norms is well known and holds asymptotically. Its concrete parameters at cryp-
tographic security levels, however, are difficult to verify directly. The broad use of this
projection in cryptography motivates a rigorous analysis of its concrete parameters, which
is the main contribution of this section.
    In this section, we provide such an analysis for the parameters relevant to cryptography,
namely λ ∈ {64, 96, 128, 192, 256} and projections with 2λ rows. Several steps require finite
calculations at these concrete parameters, so we refrain from providing exact numerical
constants for arbitrary choices of λ and row count. An accompanying Sage notebook can
be used to derive bounds for other choices of λ and the number of rows.1
    The ternary distribution makes the projection efficient to evaluate. Our analysis
establishes four estimates. For a row uj of J, it bounds the individual inner product, the
upper and lower tails of the projected norm, and the lower tail after reduction modulo q.
Outside the respective failure events, these estimates have the form

5    Sampling Small-Norm Challenges
Short challenges with invertible pairwise differences are crucial building blocks for a large
family of folding schemes [KLOT25, BC25b, BC25a, GLLO26, NS26, Osa26, BNP26] and
argument systems [BLS19, BLNS20, AL21, CLM23, LNP22, BS23, AFLN24, NS24, LSS24,
KLNO25, KLOT25, Che25, BBL+ 26b, KLN+ 26, BBL+ 26a].
Michał Osadnik                                                                              35

   To see why the choice of challenges matters, consider one folding step with challenge c:
                   wfold = wacc + cw,       ∥wfold ∥ ≤ ∥wacc ∥ + ∥c∥op ∥w∥.
The multiplication operator norm of c therefore governs witness growth in the folding
direction. Extraction additionally requires distinct challenges to have invertible differences.
A useful challenge set must be large, have invertible pairwise differences, and contain
elements with small multiplication operator norm.
    The naive construction bounds every power-basis coefficient of c by B. The short-
invertible-elements theorem of [LS18] makes the resulting set a strong sampling set for
suitable conductors and moduli. For a power-of-two conductor, the direct expansion
estimate is only ∥c∥op ≤ φB. Restricting the challenge to exactly s non-zero coefficients
improves this estimate to sB, while retaining (2B)s φs possible challenges.
    We further reduce the expansion factor by rejection sampling. The sampler draws
uniformly from the bounded fixed-weight set and rejects any challenge whose multiplication
operator norm exceeds a threshold γ. If the acceptance probability is p, the expected
number of trials is p−1 and the accepted set loses exactly − log2 p bits of cardinality. Since
rejection only removes elements, it preserves pairwise-difference invertibility. The concrete
measurements below show how the operator-norm threshold decreases as more sampling
time is allowed.
    The exact invertibility criterion imposes restrictive conditions on the conductor and
modulus. Efficient implementations instead favour moduli for which the cyclotomic

5.3    Sampling Over Almost-Splitting Cyclotomics
The exact criterion in Theorem 3 restricts the conductor and modulus. These restrictions
can preclude moduli for which the cyclotomic polynomial admits an efficient incomplete
NTT. We therefore consider a power-of-two conductor f = 2φ and moduli for which
xφ + 1 factors into distinct irreducible polynomials of degree e. This is the setting our
implementation instantiates, so it is the only one the concrete parameters of Section 6
need. It is also the setting in which the prediction can be checked directly. The predicted
point probability lies far below any measurable frequency at the implementation’s moduli,
but it remains observable at small almost-splitting primes. No corresponding heuristic
claim is made for B > 1 or non-power-of-two conductors.

7.2    Folding AIR Relations
Another apparent limitation of PikkuFold is that it only folds the principal relation Ξlin .
Here, we argue that this relation is exactly what is needed to fold the witnesses of an
AIR relation. The Algebraic Intermediate Representation (AIR) [BBHR18b] originated
from the line of work on hash-based STARKs [BBHR18a, Sta21]. AIR is particularly
useful when verifier succinctness is a priority, as the instance description is as small as the
description of the transition function. In practice, this arithmetic relation is often used
to encode the transition function of a RAM program. The construction of [BBHR18b]
already does so, giving an AIR for the DNA profile match (DPM) program, and later
STARK-friendly architectures use the same encoding, among them Cairo [GPR21] and
the RISC-V zkVM of RISC Zero [BGt23].

A.3     Retrying at a stored challenge
The extractor must produce two successful executions at the same folding challenge. It
obtains the first by running the prover once and storing the challenge. It obtains the
second by re-sending the stored challenge to fresh executions until one succeeds. The
stored challenge is not a uniform sample from C k . It was kept because the prover succeeded
at it, so challenges at which the prover succeeds more often are stored more often. The
coins of the second execution are selected in the same way, since the loop keeps the first
success. The bounds used later in the proof of Proposition 3, on the sumcheck, the trace
batching, and the projection, are stated for uniform coins. Conditioning them on success
would multiply each by the inverse success probability, which is unbounded.
    A second difficulty arises in the retry condition. It is tempting to let the retry loop
wait for a success in which the new challenge coordinate also differs from the stored one
by a unit, so that non-unit differences never occur. The bad-event bounds would then be
Michał Osadnik                                                                               55

divided by the probability of success and a unit difference, and the ratio of that to the
plain success probability is again unbounded.
    Both problems have the same resolution. Every loop selects on success alone. Every
bad event is charged with the probability that a fresh execution succeeds and is bad, before
any selection. The number of retries is counted in expectation over the entry into the loop,
not conditionally on it. With this accounting the bias cancels exactly: a challenge is stored
with probability proportional to the success probability at it, and the expected number of
retries at it is the inverse of the same quantity. The next two lemmas prove this for a loop
that repeats all continuation coins at a fixed challenge, and for a loop that repeats one
coordinate of the challenge. Both are stated in the form used by Proposition 3.
