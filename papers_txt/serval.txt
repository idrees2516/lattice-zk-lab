      Abstract. Polynomial commitment schemes (PCSs) are a core primi-
      tive in modern proof systems, especially SNARKs. Lattice-based PCSs
      are a natural post-quantum alternative, but existing constructions typi-
      cally enforce lattice smallness only up to a multiplicative slack in the ex-
      tracted ℓ2 norm. We present Serval, a lattice-based PCS with slack-free ℓ2
      soundness (norm-preserving extraction) under standard SIS/LWE-type
      assumptions. Following the norm-proof paradigm of Lyubashevsky et al.
      (Crypto ’22), we reduce exact ℓ2 enforcement to a self-inner-product
      argument plus a binary validation that avoids modular wraparound.
      Our main technical insight is a reduction that rewrites the binary check
      as inner-product-shaped constraints that match the algebraic form of
      the PCS evaluation and commitment-consistency checks. This align-
      ment yields a unified divide-and-conquer protocol that proves all con-
      straints simultaneously in O(log L) rounds, giving succinct evaluation
      proofs and efficient verification. At L = 220 and matched security param-
      eters (λ = 128), our instantiated proof size is 8× smaller than Fenzi et al.
      (JoC ’24) and 85× smaller than SLAP (Eurocrypt ’24), while remaining
      compatible with the recent construction of Cini et al. (Crypto ’24).

      Keywords: Lattice-based cryptography, Polynomial commitments


1   Introduction
A polynomial commitment scheme (PCS) lets a prover commit to a polynomial f
using a commitment Com(f ), and later prove that a claimed value y equals f (x)
(or f (⃗x) in the multilinear case) using a proof π. We write L for the dimension of
the coefficient representation: L = D + 1 for a degree-D univariate polynomial,
and L = 2n for an n-variate multilinear polynomial.
    PCSs are a standard primitive in verifiable secret sharing [5], secure multi-
party computation [10], authenticated data structures [26], and zero-knowledge
succinct non-interactive arguments of knowledge (zkSNARKs) [8,14,17,16]. In
particular, PCS verification is often a major cost driver in recursive and proof-
carrying settings, motivating schemes with sublinear verification in L.
     Many deployed PCSs are built from discrete-logarithm assumptions [29,13,27]
and are vulnerable to quantum adversaries. Code-based alternatives [7,25] are
post-quantum, but they typically do not provide algebraic structures that enable
efficient composition (e.g., linear combinations and batching of claims). Lattice-
based PCSs, in contrast, offer post-quantum security together with algebraic
structure, making them a strong candidate for composable proof systems.
     A central obstacle is to achieve norm-preserving extraction (no norm slack)
without sacrificing efficiency. 3 At a high level, lattice soundness reduces to
extracting a small-norm witness for a relation of the form:
                         n                                  o
                   R := (A, F, B; f⃗) : Af⃗ = F ∧ ∥f⃗∥2 ≤ B .

The linear constraint Af⃗ = F is easy to verify, but enforcing the concrete ℓ2
bound is substantially harder. Many lattice proof systems guarantee only that
extraction succeeds with a slack factor, namely ∥f⃗∥2 ≤ αB for some α > 1.
    This gap has been studied since early work on identification and proof proto-
cols based on lattices [30,37]. A common approach is to shrink the challenge space
to reduce slack, but this increases the soundness error and forces many repeti-
tions. As a result, proof sizes become large, and while ring or module structures
improve constants, they do not remove the basic tension between slack, challenge
space, and efficiency.
    A different line of work targets tight ℓ∞ bounds via algebraic identities.
Representative works [21,20,4] transform element-wise constraints such as f⃗ ∈
{0, ±1}n into the polynomial identity f⃗ ◦ (f⃗ − ⃗1) ◦ (f⃗ + ⃗1) = ⃗0, and verify it using
techniques like Chinese remainder theorem (CRT) or number theoretic trans-
form (NTT). These decompositions achieve slack-free ℓ∞ soundness for small
alphabets (e.g., {0, ±1}), but their prover typically performs quasi-linear work
in the vector length due to the required structured transforms. More importantly,
a tight ℓ∞ bound is often not the right target for lattice-based proof systems.
Many lattice primitives and reductions naturally control ∥ · ∥2 (e.g., via Gaus-
sian sampling or noise growth), and enforcing a stringent ∥ · ∥∞ bound can force
conservative parameters (e.g., larger moduli) while typically not strengthening
the intended ℓ2 -based hardness statement.
    More recent work targets ℓ2 norm proofs directly. Lyubashevsky [33] decom-
poses an ℓ2 constraint into a quadratic inner-product relation together with an
auxiliary linear bound, and proves both with linear prover time, proof size, and
verification time. Beullens et al. [9] further reduces the resulting ℓ2 -soundness
slack to a constant factor of 2 using a modular Johnson–Lindenstrauss argument.
    Despite these advances, integrating norm proofs into structured lattice proof
systems remains delicate, especially for lattice-based PCSs. A PCS must support
fast evaluation proofs and preserve commitment reuse, batching, and aggrega-
tion. Naive norm-proof integration can break one or more of these properties.
As a result, existing lattice-based PCSs commonly handle lattice smallness using

1.1   Related Work
Recent lattice-based PCSs have achieved sublinear (often polylogarithmic) ver-
ification for evaluation proofs using either inner-product arguments or recursive
folding. Inner-product-based schemes encode evaluation as an inner product and
prove an IPA together with commitment consistency [2,15,38,18]. They typi-
cally obtain logarithmic proof size, but still require linear online work for at
least one party. Some works further reduce online verification via preprocessing
or delegation, at the cost of auxiliary keys/tables or additional setup complex-
ity [11,23,29]. Recursive (split-and-fold) approaches verify evaluation through a
recursion tree, yielding a polylogarithmic object checked by the verifier [7,22,3].
Hybrid designs combine folding with proof-of-proof compaction, exemplified by
Greyhound [36] and by transparent SIS-based schemes using FRI-style fold-
ing [19,39].
    Orthogonally, norm proofs introduce an additional soundness axis. Tight
coefficient-set checks can be enforced via algebraic identities and structured
transforms [21,20,4]. For ℓ2 bounds, more recent techniques reduce the sound-
ness slack to a small constant (notably 2) via modular Johnson–Lindenstrauss
arguments, and have been incorporated into compact proof systems such as
LaBRADOR [9]. In lattice-based PCSs, this norm axis is commonly handled by
tolerating slack so that evaluation proofs retain sublinear structure. Achieving
slack-free ℓ2 soundness under standard SIS/LWE-type assumptions while pre-
serving batching, aggregation, and sublinear verification remains less explored.
    Serval addresses this gap by integrating slack-free ℓ2 enforcement into a struc-
tured PCS framework without sacrificing succinct evaluation proofs and efficient
verification. We provide a detailed comparison in Appendix A. Table 1 summa-
rizes representative post-quantum PCSs along the axes most relevant to this
work (setup, norm slack, and concrete proof size at matched security).

1.2   Our Results
We present a lattice-based PCS for univariate and multilinear polynomials that
supports succinct evaluation proofs and enforces a slack-free ℓ2 norm bound for
the extracted witness.
    At a high level, we cast evaluation, commitment consistency, and range con-
straints into arithmetic relations and reduce them to inner-product relations.
For evaluation and commitment consistency, the inner product is taken with a
public vector, while for range constraints it is taken with a private witness. Our
construction follows a divide-and-conquer paradigm that treats all constraints
uniformly. For a fixed security level, the proof size and verification time are

Efficient evaluation and commitment-consistency proofs. We follow an
inner-product-based approach for evaluation and use a leveled lattice-based vec-
tor commitment [12] for the coefficient vector. Both the evaluation vector and the
leveled commitment admit compatible tensor structures. This enables a log L-
round split-and-fold protocol without preprocessing or CRS generation.

Slack-free ℓ2 -norm enforcement via binary decomposition. Our norm
enforcement follows the paradigm of Lyubashevsky et al. [33]. We prove the ℓ2 -
norm constraint by proving (i) a quadratic self-inner-product relation with (ii)
a coefficient bound strong enough to lift the modular statement to an integer
statement. Concretely, for a claimed small norm f⃗ ∈ ZL·ℓ  q , we prove a statement

of the form (i) ⟨f , f ⟩ ≡ b (mod q) with a range constraint 0 ≤ b ≤ B 2 for a
public bound B, and (ii) a bound ∥f⃗∥∞ ≤ U such that L · ℓ · U 2 < q/2. These
imply ∥f⃗∥22 ≤ L · ℓ · ∥f⃗∥2∞ ≤ L · ℓ · U 2 < q/2, so the integer value ⟨f⃗, f⃗⟩ equals
its residue modulo q. Thus, the bound 0 ≤ b ≤ B 2 yields the desired slack-
free guarantee ∥f⃗∥2 ≤ B. Accordingly, the extractor’s output satisfies the same

    In our construction, the coefficient vector is constrained to be binary. This al-
lows us to guarantee the ℓ∞ -norm bound by directly proving the binary property,
rather than relying on an approximate ℓ∞ bound as Lyubashevsky et al. [33].
    Accordingly, for a large-norm vector over ZL         q , the decomposition length is set
to ℓ = ⌈log q⌉ via an unsigned base-2 decomposition. This is a standard design
choice in various lattice-based primitives, most notably the GSW cryptosystem
[24]. We note, however, that compared to schemes such as Greyhound [36] which
employ a larger base, this choice increases the witness size by a constant fac-
tor, thereby marginally raising the prover’s computational overhead in practice.
Nonetheless, the asymptotic complexity remains unaffected, and this binary de-
composition provides significant structural advantages for both the verification
process and the efficiency of proof construction.
    To prove the inner-product relation, we use a standard divide-and-conquer
protocol that reduces a length-L · ℓ inner product to length-L · ℓ/2 recursively,
resulting in log L rounds. To prove binarity, we start from the arithmetic condi-

Working over the modular ring. Our protocol is defined over a modular
ring Rq . Embedding coefficient constraints over Zq into Rq is straightforward via
standard coefficient mappings and ring automorphisms. The ring setting also ad-
mits large challenge spaces (e.g., of size about 2λ with appropriate parameters),
which reduces the soundness error.


Concrete efficiency. After applying our optimizations, the resulting PCS
achieves O(log L) proof size and O(log L) verification complexity for a fixed
security level (measured in ring elements/operations). Table 1 summarizes the
concrete comparison at 128-bit security along the axes most relevant to this work

Greyhound [36], our proof size is larger due to the structure limit and additional

the self-inner-product norm check, which keeps verifier work polylogarithmic
in N and preserves composability.

3.6     Parameter Selection and Efficiency Analysis

Parameter Selection. We group parameters by their roles in the core protocol.

 – Decomposition parameters. These parameters influence concrete effi-
   ciency and admissibility. They are the gadget-decomposition bases (σ, ι) for
   inner commitments, and (σα , ια ) for the potentially larger-norm vector ⃗sα .
 – Hardness parameters. These parameters determine the hardness of M-SIS.
   They are the rank κ, the prime modulus q, the ring dimension d, and the
   M-SIS norm bound βM-SIS required by the reduction (cf. (4)).
 – Challenges. These parameters control soundness and completeness of fold-
   ing. They are the challenge space C, its size |C|, and bounds (τ, T ) on the
   ℓ2 -norm and ℓ∞ -operator norm of its elements.

5     Optimization and Implementation

5.1   Proof Compaction

We present an optional optimization of Fig. 2 that reduces concrete proof size by
aggregating the verifier’s checks. The starting point is structural: Fig. 3 consists
almost entirely of dot-product–shaped constraints over Rq (and a small number
of linear-map consistency checks), and our protocol already supplies the tensor
and decomposition machinery needed to express them uniformly. We therefore
aggregate all per-round verification constraints into a single LaBRADOR in-
stance [9], yielding a proof-of-proof.
    This optimization enforces two requirements. First, the log N -round split-
and-fold phase must be non-interactive so that the full set of checks is fixed before
aggregation. Second, each verifier check must be written in the LaBRADOR
relation format (quadratic dot products plus linear terms).


Fiat–Shamir transform. We apply Fiat–Shamir to the recursive phase after
the prover sends (vr , vrα ). The prover derives each folding challenge ⃗ci ∈ C 2 as
a deterministic function of the transcript prefix up to round i − 1. We include
the round index and a domain separator in the hash input to avoid cross-round
collisions. To ensure that ⃗ci ∈ C 2 (satisfying invertibility and coefficient/norm
constraints), we design the challenge-generation algorithm shown in Algorithm 1,
which enforces these requirements.

6   Conclusion
Serval (Slack-free Euclidean Radius Verification for Algebraic Lattices), is a post-
quantum PCS that gives succinct evaluation proofs with a slack-free ℓ2 binding
guarantee under standard M-SIS and a transparent setup. Like its namesake,
Serval targets the bound sharply: it enforces the Euclidean radius without slack.
Our design reduces commitment consistency, evaluation correctness, and lattice
smallness to one inner-product-shaped relation over Rq , so a single log N split-
and-fold engine drives the entire protocol and yields polylogarithmic verification
with essentially linear proving.
    The crux is an exact norm mechanism that fits this recursion. We decompose
slack-free ℓ2 soundness into a ring self-inner-product claim that prevents modular
wraparound and a binary coefficient constraint that pins ℓ∞ exactly, and we


                                          29
    Fig. 6. Ring-operation counts for Greyhound and Serval (y-axis in log scale)


express binarity itself via randomized inner-product checks with tensor structure.
To preclude adaptive fabrication, the prover commits to the derived auxiliary
witness before the verifier samples the follow-on challenges. Because every check
shares the same recursion and state, the composed protocol avoids a separate
range-proof subsystem and stays naturally batching- and recursion-friendly.
    Under the stated parameter regime (prime q with the required invertibility
condition for challenges, and q > 2N ℓd for the exact-norm reduction), sound-
ness follows from coordinate-wise special soundness of the shared recursion plus
the negligible error from t binary-check repetitions. In this scope, our concrete
comparison shows competitive proof sizes and fast verification while providing
the stronger no-slack norm guarantee that prior post-quantum PCSs typically
relax. An immediate direction is tighter constant-alphabet range enforcement
that preserves the same log N engine, and a full end-to-end instantiation with
Fiat–Shamir and LaBRADOR-style compaction to reduce constants in practice.

A    Additional Related Works
We provide additional context for Table 1 by summarizing representative post-
quantum PCS frameworks and where norm-soundness slack enters their analyses.
This section is intended as a high-level comparison along the axes used in Table 1
(basis/setup/slack/proof size); for precise statements, we refer to the cited works.

Inner-product-based approaches. A common lattice-based route commits to the
coefficient vector and proves evaluation by reducing it to an inner product ⟨f⃗, ⃗x⟩
with ⃗x = (1, x, . . . , xL−1 ) in the univariate case [2,15,38,18]. These schemes typ-
ically yield logarithmic-size evaluation proofs (measured in Zq or ring elements),
while at least one party performs work linear in L due to commitment consis-
tency or binding checks. Some works reduce online verification via preprocessing
or delegation (e.g., Dory-style techniques or structured evaluation support), at
the cost of auxiliary keys/tables or stronger setup requirements [29,11,23]. Along
the norm axis, most inner-product-based lattice PCSs guarantee extraction only
up to a multiplicative slack, or tighten slack by strengthening the underlying as-
sumption (e.g., k-SIS) rather than by proving an exact ℓ2 bound under standard
SIS/LWE-type assumptions [23,2,18].

Recursive approaches. Recursive (split-and-fold) PCSs verify an evaluation proof
by a smaller proof system, which is itself verified recursively, yielding a polyloga-
rithmic object checked by the verifier [7]. Optimized variants achieve very small
verifier time but often rely on large auxiliary data and/or structured assump-
tions, and their norm guarantees follow from the underlying extractor for the
commitment primitive and thus inherit multiplicative slack [22,3]. Consequently,
these constructions typically do not provide norm-preserving extraction under
standard SIS/LWE-type assumptions.

B.1    Interactive Proofs

Let R ⊆ {0, 1}∗ × {0, 1}∗ × {0, 1}∗ be a ternary relation. For (pp, x, w) ∈ R, we
call pp the public parameters, x the statement, and w a witness. For fixed pp
and x, we write R(pp, x) := { w : (pp, x, w) ∈ R }.

Definition 3 (Interactive proof system). An interactive proof system Π =
(S, P, V) for R has a probabilistic polynomial-time (PPT) setup algorithm S and
an interactive protocol between a PPT prover P and a verifier V. P convinces
V that there exists w with (pp, x, w) ∈ R. Both P and V receive x and public
parameters pp ← S(1λ ). P also holds a private witness w ∈ R(pp, x). At the end,
V outputs accept or reject. The transcript is the sequence of messages exchanged.
